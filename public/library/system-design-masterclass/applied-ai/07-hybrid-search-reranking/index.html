    Hybrid Search and Reranking¶

    Hybrid retrieval is a staged ranking system, not a checkbox that combines keyword and vector search. A production design must define what each retriever can recover, where authorization filters run, how ranks are fused, what the reranker may change, and which quality gain justifies each millisecond and dollar.

    Evidence vocabulary¶

    • [Source] is a statement supported directly by a dated source in Sources.
    • [Derived] is a calculation or consequence derived from stated inputs.
    • [Design] is a recommended production contract, not a claim made by a cited paper.
    • [Measure] is a hypothesis that must be validated on the system's own corpus and traffic.

    Design target¶

    Assume an enterprise search API over 40 million permissioned chunks. Queries include exact identifiers, natural-language questions, abbreviations, and rare product names. The service targets 99.9% monthly availability, 350 ms p95 server latency, no unauthorized result disclosure, and a measurable relevance improvement over lexical search.

    Invariants¶

    1. Authorization is enforced before content, snippets, scores, or result counts leave the retrieval boundary.
    2. Every response identifies the query, corpus, lexical index, embedding, sparse model, fusion, and reranker versions.
    3. Reranking never repairs a relevant document missing from its candidate set.
    4. Fusion consumes deterministic ranked lists with stable document IDs and tie-breaking.
    5. Offline quality, online utility, latency, and cost are separate release gates.
    6. Empty, partial, stale, timed-out, and policy-filtered results are distinguishable outcomes.
    7. A degraded mode may remove expensive stages but never remove authorization or deletion enforcement.

    Retrieval and ranking architecture¶

    flowchart LR
    Q[Query] --> N[Normalize and classify]
    N --> P[Principal and policy filter]
    P --> B[BM25 retriever]
    P --> D[Dense retriever]
    P --> S[Sparse neural retriever]
    B --> F[RRF or learned fusion]
    D --> F
    S --> F
    F --> H[Hydrate authorized fields]
    H --> R[Cross-encoder or late interaction]
    R --> O[Stable top K response]
    N --> L[(Stage evidence)]
    B --> L
    D --> L
    S --> L
    F --> L
    R --> L

    The retrievers increase candidate recall through different matching signals. Fusion creates one bounded candidate set. Hydration retrieves the text or token representations needed by the reranker. The reranker improves ordering inside that set. Each boundary records enough evidence to attribute a regression.

    Candidate generators¶

    BM25 lexical retrieval¶

    BM25 rewards query terms that occur in a document, discounts terms common across the corpus, and saturates the benefit of repeated term frequency. A common form is:

    BM25(q, d) = sum over t in q of
  IDF(t) * tf(t,d) * (k1 + 1)
  / (tf(t,d) + k1 * (1 - b + b * |d| / avgdl))

    k1 controls term-frequency saturation. b controls document-length normalization. Exact formula and IDF variants differ by implementation, so version both the engine and parameters. [Source] BM25 belongs to the probabilistic lexical-ranking family described by Robertson and Walker; it does not provide a calibrated probability of relevance.

    Lexical retrieval is strong for identifiers, quoted phrases, error codes, names, and rare tokens. It is sensitive to analyzers, stemming, token boundaries, synonyms, field boosts, and corpus statistics. Reindexing can therefore change rank even when documents do not change.

    Dense retrieval¶

    A dense retriever maps query and document text into fixed-width vectors. A bi-encoder usually computes a similarity such as dot product or cosine similarity and uses approximate nearest-neighbor search for scale. [Source] Dense Passage Retrieval demonstrated independently encoded question and passage vectors with inner-product retrieval for open-domain question answering.

    Dense retrieval can connect paraphrases that share few terms, but it may blur exact values and rare identifiers. Its behavior depends on embedding model, input prefix, truncation, distance function, vector normalization, quantization, ANN parameters, and training domain. Store all of them in the index manifest.

    Learned sparse retrieval¶

    Learned sparse models produce vocabulary-sized sparse representations whose nonzero dimensions remain lexical terms, including learned expansion terms. [Source] SPLADE uses sparse lexical representations with regularization intended to control efficiency. Sparse neural retrieval can retain inverted-index execution and improve semantic matching, but posting-list growth and model inference cost are real capacity concerns.

    Do not call every token-weight model BM25. Record whether a score came from observed term frequency, learned expansion, or another feature. Keep separate score namespaces because raw values are not comparable by default.

    Retrieval choice table¶

    Method Primary signal Typical strength Typical weakness Index cost
    BM25 observed terms and corpus frequency exact strings and rare terms vocabulary mismatch postings plus stored fields
    dense bi-encoder vector similarity paraphrase and conceptual match identifiers and domain drift vectors plus ANN graph or partitions
    learned sparse learned term weights and expansion semantic lexical match expansion latency and postings growth model inference plus sparse postings
    metadata filter structured equality/range policy authority and scope candidate starvation if applied late filter indexes or partitions

    [Measure] The winning combination depends on query mix and corpus. Evaluate slices such as exact ID, acronym, multilingual, short ambiguous query, long natural-language question, fresh content, and restrictive ACL.

    Authorization filter placement¶

    flowchart TD
    A[Authenticated principal] --> T[Tenant or security partition]
    T --> C{Retriever supports filter-aware search?}
    C -->|Yes| F[Apply ACL predicate during candidate search]
    C -->|No| O[Bounded overfetch inside permitted isolation domain]
    O --> H[Authorize during hydration]
    F --> H
    H --> V{Enough authorized candidates?}
    V -->|Yes| R[Rerank]
    V -->|No| E[Return sparse or empty result]

    Post-filtering a global top 100 may leave no authorized results even when relevant authorized documents exist below that global cutoff. Overfetch reduces but does not eliminate this candidate-starvation problem. Prefer physical tenant or security-domain partitioning where practical, filter-aware ANN or postings execution, and mandatory authorization again during hydration. Never increase an unauthorized overfetch bound until latency and side-channel risks are reviewed.

    If the contract forbids unauthorized items from influencing candidates at all, the no-filter branch must use an authorized exact scan or a stricter partition, not global ANN plus hydration checks. Neither extra candidates nor a larger timeout creates authorization. The pgvector RRF implementation, inspected 2026-09-08, illustrates missing-list contributions and rank fusion, not a tenant-security implementation; do not copy its unrestricted example query as a production policy boundary.

    Reciprocal Rank Fusion¶

    Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) combines ordinal ranks without assuming comparable raw-score scales. [Source] Cormack, Clarke, and Buettcher published RRF in 2009.

    RRF(d) = sum over ranking r of w_r / (c + rank_r(d))

    c is a rank constant and w_r is an optional retriever weight. A missing document contributes zero. c=60 is common in examples, not a universal optimum. Tune constants and candidate depths only on representative judgments, then freeze them in a versioned fusion policy.

    Deterministic algorithm¶

    function fuse(rankings, weights, c, limit):
    score = map document_id -> 0
    provenance = map document_id -> []
    for retriever in stable_retriever_order:
        deduplicate retriever.results by document_id
        for position, result in enumerate(results, start=1):
            score[result.id] += weights[retriever] / (c + position)
            provenance[result.id].append(retriever, position, result.raw_score)
    return first limit sorted by (-score, canonical_document_id)

    Persist input ranks and fusion contributions. A stable tie-break prevents evaluation noise and pagination duplication. Deduplicate canonical documents before final ranking when multiple chunks or aliases represent the same result; retain the best supporting chunks separately.

    Worked calculation¶

    Suppose BM25 returns [A, B, C, D] and dense retrieval returns [C, E, A, F], with equal weights and c=60.

    A = 1/61 + 1/63 = 0.032266
C = 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.032266
B = 1/62          = 0.016129
E = 1/62          = 0.016129
D = 1/64          = 0.015625
F = 1/64          = 0.015625

    [Derived] A and C tie, so canonical ID order or another declared tie-break decides their order. The RRF values are fusion scores, not probabilities. See Lab 04 for an executable fixture and the calculations workbook.

    Reranking choices¶

    Cross-encoder¶

    A cross-encoder jointly encodes the query and each candidate text, then predicts a relevance score. [Source] Nogueira and Cho showed BERT used as a passage reranker for a first-stage candidate list. Joint attention can capture fine query-document interactions, but cost grows with candidate count and sequence length.

    Use a cross-encoder when a bounded candidate set fits the latency budget and fine-grained relevance matters. Batch candidates for accelerator efficiency, but cap batch wait so low traffic does not inflate tail latency. A timeout returns the fused order with a reranker_status=degraded, not a partial arbitrary order.

    Late interaction¶

    Late-interaction systems encode query and document tokens separately, retain multiple token vectors, and compare them at query time. [Source] ColBERT introduced efficient late interaction using contextualized token-level representations and a MaxSim-style operator. It trades more index storage and query-time token matching for richer interaction than one vector per document.

    Late interaction can serve as retrieval, reranking, or both depending on implementation. Do not assume it has cross-encoder quality or bi-encoder cost. Measure index bytes, cache locality, candidate depth, accelerator use, and p99 under the target corpus.

    Reranker contract¶

    {
  "query_id": "q-01H...",
  "query_text": "opaque-or-redacted-by-policy",
  "principal_scope_digest": "sha256:...",
  "corpus_epoch": 42,
  "candidates": [
    {
      "document_id": "doc-123",
      "chunk_id": "chunk-7",
      "text_digest": "sha256:...",
      "fusion_rank": 3,
      "features": {"bm25_rank": 1, "dense_rank": 17}
    }
  ],
  "reranker": {"name": "cross-encoder-x", "version": "2026-08-14"},
  "deadline_ms": 90,
  "top_k": 10
}

    The response returns score, rank, finish_reason, and per-candidate input digest. The service rejects candidates from another tenant, epoch, or query. It never accepts arbitrary URLs or fetches candidate text itself.

    Quality and latency budget¶

    flowchart LR
    A[20 ms admission and normalization] --> B[70 ms parallel retrieval]
    B --> C[25 ms fusion and hydration]
    C --> D[140 ms reranking]
    D --> E[35 ms response]
    E --> F[60 ms reserve]

    For a 350 ms p95 target, the illustrative budget sums to 20 + 70 + 25 + 140 + 35 + 60 = 350 ms. [Design] Reserve is not free time; it absorbs network variance, queueing, and unmodeled work. Stage p95 values do not add to end-to-end p95 in general. Use per-request traces and distribution-aware load tests.

    Suppose retrieval produces 300 candidates, fusion keeps 100, and a cross-encoder processes 100 pairs at 1,200 pairs/s effective throughput:

    fluid service-work estimate = 100 / 1,200 s = 83.3 ms
at 80 queries/s, pair demand = 80 * 100 = 8,000 pairs/s
minimum equivalent workers = 8,000 / 1,200 = 6.67
with 30% failure and headroom reserve = ceil(6.67 / 0.70) = 10 workers

    [Derived] Ten equivalent workers cover the stated steady demand after reserving 30% capacity, before batching inefficiency, replicas, zone loss, and tail effects. Sensitivity matters: reranking 200 candidates doubles pair demand. Candidate depth is therefore a capacity control, not only a quality parameter.

    Aggregate pairs/s does not determine an individual query's latency. The 83.3 ms estimate assumes the stated worker can devote that rate to this query's batch; measure actual batch latency and queueing separately, particularly when throughput comes from overlapping many queries.

    Evaluation and release gates¶

    Build immutable query sets with graded document or passage judgments, unanswerable cases, ACL cases, freshness cases, and hard query slices. Prevent tuning against the final holdout.

    Stage Metric Failure it localizes
    candidate union Recall@N relevant evidence never entered reranking
    fusion MRR, nDCG@K, Recall@K rank combination harmed ordering
    reranker delta nDCG@K and win/loss pairs reranker reordered good candidates badly
    policy unauthorized-return rate, authorized Recall@K security leak or filter starvation
    serving p50/p95/p99 latency, timeout rate operational budget breach
    product successful-search rate, reformulation, task completion offline gain did not improve utility

    Release only when mandatory slices pass fixed thresholds, paired quality comparisons are confidence-aware, and cost and latency gates pass at projected load. Online interleaving or A/B tests can measure preference, but they do not replace explicit security and correctness tests.

    Failure attribution record¶

    {
  "query_id": "q-01H...",
  "versions": {
    "corpus_epoch": 42,
    "lexical_index": "lex-17",
    "embedding": "emb-9",
    "sparse_model": "splade-4",
    "fusion": "rrf-6",
    "reranker": "ce-12"
  },
  "counts": {
    "bm25": 100,
    "dense": 100,
    "sparse": 100,
    "union": 241,
    "authorized": 89,
    "reranked": 80,
    "returned": 10
  },
  "timing_ms": {"retrieval": 61, "fusion": 4, "hydrate": 13, "rerank": 87},
  "degraded": [],
  "sampled_rank_evidence_ref": "evidence://search/q-01H..."
}

    Log raw query text and document text only under explicit privacy policy. Prefer stable IDs, digests, bounded sampled rank evidence, and separately protected debugging access.

    Failure modes and controls¶

    Failure Discriminator Safe response
    analyzer change breaks exact IDs BM25 recall falls only on identifier slice rollback lexical index epoch
    ANN recall collapse exact-vector search remains stable while ANN overlap falls raise search effort or rollback ANN build
    embedding drift old index plus old query encoder wins shadow comparison restore compatible encoder/index pair
    sparse expansion explosion posting bytes and query fan-out rise cap expansion, quarantine model, retain old index
    late ACL filter starves candidates large prefilter count and tiny authorized set use filter-aware search or partitioning
    RRF regression persisted ranks recompute to a different fused order rollback fusion policy
    reranker timeout candidate recall stable, reranker deadline exceeded return fused order with degraded marker
    reranker candidate ceiling relevant item absent from union improve retrievers or candidate depth
    duplicate chunks dominate many top results share canonical document ID group before final top K
    cache leaks scope same key serves different principal-scope digests purge and key by policy version and scope

    Related incident discriminators appear in Applied AI Failure Cards.

    Security and privacy¶

    • Treat query text and document text as sensitive tenant data.
    • Bind every cache entry to tenant, principal-scope digest, corpus epoch, policy version, query normalization version, and the complete retriever/fusion/reranker tuple. Revalidate current permissions and deletion state before returning cached content; a versioned key alone does not enforce a revocation that happened after the entry was written.
    • Validate filters against an allowlisted schema; the model or client cannot remove mandatory predicates.
    • Protect index backups, rank traces, and embeddings under the source retention and deletion policy.
    • Rate-limit broad or adversarial queries that maximize posting scans or ANN work.
    • Do not expose raw scores, total matches, or timing when they create an unauthorized existence oracle.
    • Sign or checksum immutable manifests and verify them before activation.

    Index and reranker migration¶

    1. Build a new immutable index epoch from a declared corpus manifest.
    2. Validate counts, tombstones, ACL distributions, exact-vs-ANN overlap, and golden queries.
    3. Shadow query encoding and retrieval without exposing candidate text across policy boundaries.
    4. Dual-read a sampled cohort and compare per-query candidates, ranks, latency, and cost.
    5. Canary the complete compatible tuple: query normalizer, retriever, index, fusion, and reranker.
    6. Promote through an atomic routing manifest; never mix an embedding query encoder with an incompatible vector index.
    7. Retain the old epoch until rollback and deletion obligations are proven.
    8. Stop dual reads and remove old data only after acceptance criteria and rollback window expire.

    [Design] Rollback should publish a new activation record pointing to a known-good immutable tuple. Mutating an old manifest destroys auditability.

    Interview exercise¶

    Design search for 40 million internal support articles across 20,000 tenants. Half of the queries contain exact product codes; the rest are natural-language questions. The service must return 10 authorized results under 350 ms p95 and support weekly embedding upgrades.

    Cover:

    1. query and corpus slices;
    2. BM25, dense, and optional sparse candidate depths;
    3. ACL filter placement and tenant isolation;
    4. RRF calculation and deterministic ties;
    5. cross-encoder versus late-interaction tradeoffs;
    6. a latency and capacity calculation;
    7. stage-specific evaluation and failure attribution;
    8. dual-index migration, rollback, and deletion.

    L5 and L6 signals¶

    L5¶

    • Defines candidate recall before discussing reranker quality.
    • Computes RRF and at least one latency or pair-throughput budget correctly.
    • Enforces authorization during retrieval and hydration.
    • Separates offline relevance, online utility, latency, and cost gates.
    • Handles reranker timeout with a safe, observable degraded mode.
    • Migrates compatible index/model tuples with dual reads and rollback.

    L6¶

    • Governs shared query taxonomy, judgment policy, and retriever contracts across teams.
    • Quantifies zone or accelerator loss and the receiving fleet's headroom.
    • Defines exception authority for candidate depth, security partitions, and model changes.
    • Allocates storage, inference, and evaluation cost by tenant or product.
    • Names falsifying evidence, owners, retention, review cadence, and old-epoch removal criteria.

    Final design checklist¶

    • Candidate sources, depths, timeouts, and deduplication are explicit.
    • Filter placement preserves both authorization and recall.
    • Fusion formula, constants, weights, and tie-breaks are versioned.
    • Reranker input, deadline, fallback, and candidate ceiling are explicit.
    • Quality is evaluated by stage and by predeclared slice.
    • Capacity includes candidate depth, failures, and headroom.
    • Migration activates only compatible immutable versions.
    • Logs and caches preserve tenant, ACL, privacy, and deletion boundaries.

    Sources¶

    • [Source, primary, 1994-11] Stephen Robertson et al., Okapi at TREC-3. Supports the probabilistic lexical-ranking lineage and BM25-style term weighting. The exact production formula remains implementation-specific.
    • [Source, textbook, 2008-07-07] Manning, Raghavan, and Schutze, Introduction to Information Retrieval. Supports lexical retrieval, evaluation, and inverted-index terminology.
    • [Source, primary, 2009-07] Cormack, Clarke, and Buettcher, Reciprocal Rank Fusion Outperforms Condorcet and Individual Rank Learning Methods. Defines RRF.
    • [Source, primary, 2020-04-10] Karpukhin et al., Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering. Supports bi-encoder dense passage retrieval with inner-product search.
    • [Source, primary, 2021-07-12] Formal et al., SPLADE: Sparse Lexical and Expansion Model for First Stage Ranking. Supports learned sparse lexical expansion and efficiency regularization. Title/version verified 2026-09-08; this identifier is the original SPLADE paper, not SPLADE v2.
    • [Source, primary, 2019-01-13] Nogueira and Cho, Passage Re-ranking with BERT. Supports cross-encoder-style reranking of first-stage candidates.
    • [Source, primary, 2020-04-27] Khattab and Zaharia, ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT. Supports token-level late interaction.
    • [Source, benchmark, 2021-04-26] Thakur et al., BEIR: A Heterogeneous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models. Supports evaluation across heterogeneous retrieval datasets; it does not substitute for product-specific judgments.

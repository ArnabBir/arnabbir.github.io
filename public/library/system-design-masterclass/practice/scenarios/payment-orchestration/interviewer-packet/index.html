    Interviewer Packet¶

    Practice home | Rubric | Exemplars

    This file contains disclosures, probes, and calibration material. Keep it closed to candidates until the first design pass is complete.

    Interview objective¶

    Assess whether the candidate can make irreversible external effects safe, establish financial authority, recover ambiguous outcomes, and evolve the design with operational and organizational judgment. Reward depth on a coherent path over breadth across payment features.

    Mode selection¶

    Mode Use when Required evidence Optional extension
    15 minutes Screening or warm-up Scope, invariants, API, confirm trace, timeout One refund race
    30 minutes Standard L5 loop Safe core, lifecycle, data, ledger, outbox, telemetry Reconciliation
    45 minutes Deep L5 or L6 signal Full core plus scaling, repair, tradeoffs Multi-region
    60 minutes Dedicated L6 exercise Full design, failure response, migration, leadership Adversarial review

    Opening script¶

    Design a payment orchestration platform for merchants. Providers may respond synchronously, asynchronously, or ambiguously. Start by clarifying scope and stating correctness invariants. I will provide assumptions when you ask. Please narrate authority, transaction boundaries, retry identity, and failure behavior as you draw.

    Do not steer the candidate toward intent, ledger, or outbox terminology. The prompt already requires those capabilities; let the candidate define their boundaries.

    Assumptions available on request¶

    Disclose only when asked or when the selected mode requires forward progress.

    Candidate asks about Interviewer disclosure Signal to observe
    Payment methods Start with tokenized cards and two providers. Provider calls support a merchant-supplied operation reference. Narrows scope without designing rails
    Authorization/capture Automatic capture is required. Manual and partial capture may be discussed as an extension. Distinguishes authorization from money movement
    Currency Multiple currencies; no conversion inside one payment. Amounts use integer minor units. Names per-currency invariants
    Balances The platform tracks merchant payable balances; settlement is downstream. Introduces ledger without making it a bank
    Refunds Partial refunds are required and may race. Total successful plus reserved refunds cannot exceed captured amount. Looks for atomic reservation
    Provider behavior Calls can time out after acceptance. Callbacks can duplicate, reorder, or be delayed. Status query exists but is rate-limited. A merchant-supplied reference alone does not promise deduplication: ask the candidate to specify provider key scope, retention, and lookup visibility, then record the agreed exercise assumptions. Models unknown, retention expiry, and resolution budget
    Idempotency Merchants retry for days and may accidentally reuse a key with changed payload. Uses durable key plus request hash
    Webhooks Merchant endpoints can be slow or unavailable for hours. Global ordering is not required. Isolates endpoints and supports replay
    Ledger Auditors require immutable history and balanced entries per currency. Names posting authority and corrections
    Scale Use the prompt numbers; one merchant can be 20% of traffic. Quantifies amplification and skew
    Regions Each merchant may have a home region. A short write outage is preferable to duplicate money movement. Chooses ownership and fencing
    Compliance General services receive tokens, not raw card numbers or security codes. Maintains trust boundary and redaction
    Existing system For migration, use the synchronous legacy processor in migration.md. Preserves authority during evolution

    Checkpoints by time¶

    Minute 5¶

    Look for scope, an authority question, and money invariants. If absent, ask:

    Which facts must never become contradictory, even during retry?

    Minute 15¶

    Look for durable operation identity before the provider call and an unknown outcome. If absent, ask:

    The provider captures but the response is lost. The merchant retries. Trace every identity and state transition.

    Minute 30¶

    Look for ledger timing, outbox, callback races, and repair. If absent, ask:

    Which single commit makes a successful result valid, and how does a merchant event leave that commit?

    Minute 45¶

    Look for quantified backlogs, skew, regional ownership, and evolution triggers. Ask:

    What measurement would make you split the ledger, and what new failure state would that create?

    Minute 55¶

    Look for migration gates and operational ownership. Ask:

    A canary posts correct entries but its projection lags for 20 minutes. Do you promote, pause, or roll back? Who decides?

    Branching follow-ups¶

    Choose one branch based on the candidate's current strength. Do not ask every question.

    Branch A: Candidate relies on exactly once¶

    1. Which boundary provides exactly once: client/API, provider, queue, database, or webhook?
    2. A consumer commits its database work and crashes before acknowledging the message. What repeats?
    3. What stable business key makes the repeat harmless?
    4. How do you detect a missing effect rather than only a duplicate?

    Strong evidence: replaces the claim with durable identity, idempotent transitions, inbox/outbox, and reconciliation.

    Branch B: Candidate handles provider timeout well¶

    1. The provider status endpoint is throttled for an hour. How do you allocate query capacity?
    2. A callback and resolver observe capture simultaneously. Which write wins and what happens to the loser?
    3. The provider cannot find the operation. When may you retry the financial request?
    4. How does unknown age affect merchant UX and operations escalation?

    Strong evidence: sticky operation, conditional transition, durable loser replay, bounded retry budgets, quarantine when evidence remains insufficient.

    Branch C: Candidate separates the ledger immediately¶

    1. What customer status exists after provider capture but before ledger acknowledgement?
    2. Can settlement consume that payment?
    3. What is the posting idempotency reference?
    4. What measured constraint justifies accepting this distributed workflow now?

    Strong evidence: explicit pending-ledger state, durable posting command, blocked finality/settlement, reconciliation, and willingness to begin colocated.

    Branch D: Candidate keeps one database¶

    1. Which rows commit together?
    2. How do you partition without creating cross-shard ledger transactions?
    3. At what sustained saturation or ownership threshold do you split?
    4. How will the state model change after the split?

    Strong evidence: sees colocation as a deliberate safe baseline, not an inability to scale.

    Branch E: Candidate proposes active-active writes¶

    1. Region A accepts a $70 refund while Region B accepts a $50 refund against $100. What merge is valid?
    2. How are stale writers fenced after failover?
    3. Which operations truly require global writes rather than local reads?

    Strong evidence: converges on single home ownership, epochs, recovery pause, and globally available replicas.

    Branch F: Candidate is operationally strong¶

    1. Which metric pages a finance on-call rather than an SRE?
    2. How do you express value at risk without high-cardinality merchant labels?
    3. What automated repair is safe for a captured-at-provider/missing-local break?
    4. Which chaos test could accidentally move real money, and how do you isolate it?

    Strong evidence: separates technical and financial SLIs, defines approval boundaries, and uses provider sandboxes or simulators for destructive tests.

    Branch G: Candidate is migration strong¶

    1. A retry arrives after its merchant cohort flag changed. Which implementation owns it?
    2. Shadow ledger totals match globally but not per merchant. Can you promote?
    3. What does rollback mean after valid ledger entries exist?
    4. When can legacy mutable columns be deleted?

    Strong evidence: recorded generation, per-entity/value comparison, immutable rollback, and consumer inventory.

    Evidence notes¶

    Capture candidate statements, not interviewer interpretations.

    Topic Positive evidence Risk evidence
    Authority
    Unknown outcome
    Idempotency
    Transaction boundary
    Ledger
    Concurrency
    Reconciliation
    Operations
    Scale/regions
    Evolution/leadership

    Calibration anchors¶

    Weak L5¶

    Component-oriented answer with shallow failure or operations evidence but no confirmed 17/40 safety-cap defect. If retries can duplicate money or unknown outcomes are guessed, classify below L5 according to the rubric, not weak L5. Record any interviewer-supplied repair separately from independently demonstrated evidence.

    Borderline L5¶

    Safe direction and coherent lifecycle, but one important atomicity, race, or repair gap remains after probing. Can implement a bounded component but needs review on financial workflows.

    Strong L5¶

    Independently produces safe end-to-end flow, explicit transaction boundaries, ambiguity resolution, reconciliation, and actionable operations. Tradeoffs are appropriate to current scale.

    Borderline L6¶

    Adds platform evolution, regional posture, quantified risk, and cross-team interfaces, but rollout ownership or decision gates are incomplete.

    Strong L6¶

    Shapes ambiguous requirements, protects business invariants across teams, manages migration and incident risk, defines reversible decisions, and gives other teams an operable contract.

    Use annotated exemplars and the evidence rubric to resolve close calls.

    Calibrated debrief¶

    Deliver a four-part debrief in under three minutes.

    1. Demonstrated strength: cite one exact decision and why it reduces risk.
    2. Highest-risk gap: describe one concrete failure sequence, not a missing buzzword.
    3. Level evidence: name the rubric dimension and what was independently demonstrated versus prompted.
    4. Next-level action: give one design artifact or exercise that would close the gap.

    Debrief examples¶

    Strong L5:

    You made the provider operation durable before the call and kept timeout as unknown, which protected the core money boundary. The main gap was regional recovery: failover had replicas but no stale-writer fencing. This is strong L5 evidence because the single-region implementation and repair loops were independently complete. To reach L6, write the ownership epoch, promotion gate, and in-flight migration protocol.

    Borderline L6:

    You connected the ledger split to measured database saturation and introduced a pending-ledger protocol. The remaining risk was rollout ownership: the plan had shadow comparison but no value-at-risk threshold or authority to pause promotion. That supports borderline L6 rather than strong L6. Add cohort gates, immutable rollback behavior, and accountable finance and SRE owners.

    Interviewer anti-patterns¶

    • Do not award points for naming Kafka, SQL, or Redis without semantics.
    • Do not force the candidate to reproduce one architecture.
    • Do not penalize a safe single-database baseline merely for lacking microservices.
    • Do not reveal the expected state names before the candidate models ambiguity.
    • Do not average a duplicate-money defect into a passing total.
    • Do not treat omitted optional features as a failure when the selected timed mode excludes them.
    • Do not open the reference case study during the interview.

    Post-session reading¶

    After scoring and debrief, invite the candidate to compare their design with Design a Payment System. Ask them to write three deltas: one correctness improvement, one unnecessary complexity they would remove, and one operational decision they would add.

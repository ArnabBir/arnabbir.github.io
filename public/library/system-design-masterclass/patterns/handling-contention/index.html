    Pattern: Handling Contention¶

    Contention appears when concurrent operations coordinate on the same logical resource. More replicas do not solve a hot lock because the invariant still needs one authoritative decision. A concert onsale illustrates the problem: 50,000 users attempt to buy 1,000 seats in one minute, and thousands may select the same front-row seat. The design must preserve "no oversell" while bounding retries, queueing, and unfair advantage.

    When to use and avoid¶

    Use optimistic concurrency when conflicts are rare, pessimistic locks for short high-conflict transactions, serialization for a very hot key, and escrow when divisible rights can be partitioned. Avoid distributed locks when a database constraint suffices. Avoid adding a queue if users require immediate decisions but queue delay has no bound; admission control must accompany serialization.

    Mechanism Fits Loses when
    compare-and-set/version rare conflicts retry storms under high conflict
    row lock short local transaction lock convoy and deadlock
    unique constraint uniqueness cannot express richer policy alone
    single-key command queue hot key, ordered decisions one-key throughput ceiling
    escrow/reservation pools divisible finite capacity rights become stranded
    waiting room demand far above capacity fairness and token operations become product-critical

    State the invariant and owner¶

    For assigned seating, a database unique constraint on (show_id, seat_id) is the final defense: at most one live claim owns a seat. For general admission, the invariant is sold + active_holds <= capacity. Place that check and mutation in one transactional owner. Caches, queue positions, and availability counters are hints; none may authorize the final sale independently.

    Make hold lifecycle explicit: AVAILABLE -> HELD -> SOLD, with HELD -> AVAILABLE on expiry. Use server or database time and store an absolute expiry. A payment response arriving after expiry is not automatically failure; it is a late outcome requiring an explicit policy such as honor from a grace pool, reacquire capacity, or refund.

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Available
    Available --> Held: conditional claim
    Held --> Sold: payment confirmed before deadline
    Held --> Available: server-time expiry
    Held --> Reconciling: payment outcome unknown
    Reconciling --> Sold: verified success and capacity honored
    Reconciling --> Available: verified failure
    Reconciling --> Refunding: late success cannot be honored

    Queueing explains collapse¶

    Contention is often a queueing problem disguised as a database problem. If one reservation owner makes 2,000 decisions/s and 20,000 commands/s arrive, backlog grows by 18,000/s. In ten seconds, 180,000 requests wait. Faster retries add work without adding decision capacity.

    Utilization near 100% produces nonlinear waiting time. For an illustrative M/M/1 queue with service rate mu = 2,000/s, arrival rate lambda = 1,800/s, utilization is rho = 0.9; expected time in system is 1 / (mu - lambda) = 5 ms. At 1,980/s, rho = 0.99 and expected time rises to 50 ms. Real arrivals and service times need not follow M/M/1 assumptions. Pauses and burst correlation can make tails much worse, while less-variable service can reduce waiting; use the model to identify pressure, not predict a universal percentile. Operate below saturation and reject excess work before it consumes the constrained tier.

    Little's Law, L = lambda * W, relates stable mean occupancy to mean residence time, not p99. At 2,000 completions/s and a 250 ms mean, average occupancy is 500 requests. If 20,000 requests are concurrently in that stage at the same completion rate, mean residence is 10 seconds. Admission bounds still need burst and tail tests; a percentile target is not a substitute for the mean.

    Conflict amplification¶

    Optimistic concurrency reads version v, computes an update, then executes UPDATE ... WHERE version = v. This is efficient when collisions are uncommon. Under a flash crowd, losers re-read and retry, creating conflict amplification: physical attempts divided by successful logical operations.

    Suppose 1,000 clients race for one remaining seat. One succeeds and 999 fail. If every loser retries three times without learning the seat is gone, the database sees about 3,997 writes or conditional attempts for one success. Amplification is nearly 4,000x. Exponential backoff only spreads waste; it does not create another seat. Return a definitive 409 unavailable and prohibit retries for terminal conflicts.

    For a counter with 100 units, bounded retry can help while inventory remains. Use jitter and a retry budget, for example at most two retries within 150 ms. Re-read authoritative state before each retry. Stop immediately on sold out, deadline expiry, or admission-token invalidity. Track attempts per success; rising amplification is an early overload signal.

    flowchart TD
    R[Command arrives] --> A{Valid admission token?}
    A -- no --> X[Reject before database]
    A -- yes --> C{Conflict rate below threshold?}
    C -- yes --> O[CAS with bounded retry]
    C -- no --> Q[Route to keyed serializer]
    O --> S{Succeeded?}
    S -- yes --> H[Return durable hold]
    S -- terminal conflict --> U[Return unavailable]
    S -- transient and budget remains --> O
    Q --> S

    Locks, serialization, and fencing¶

    A row lock works when the transaction is short and entirely inside one database. Acquire locks in a stable order to reduce deadlocks, do not call payment while holding the lock, and set a lock timeout shorter than the request deadline. Pessimistic locking converts repeated conflicts into waiting, but a convoy can still exhaust connection pools.

    A keyed command queue gives one logical owner ordered access to an event, section, or inventory bucket. Persist commands before processing so owner failure does not lose accepted requests. The owner can batch compatible operations, answer immediately when sold out, and publish authoritative results. This simplifies contention but does not exceed one owner's throughput for that key.

    Ownership must be fenced. A lease alone is unsafe: owner A can pause past expiry, owner B can acquire the lease, and A can resume and write. Assign a monotonically increasing fencing token on acquisition. Every protected write includes the token, and storage rejects a token lower than the latest accepted value.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant A as Owner A token 41
    participant L as Lease service
    participant B as Owner B token 42
    participant D as Inventory store
    A->>L: acquire event owner
    L-->>A: token 41
    A--xA: long pause
    B->>L: acquire after expiry
    L-->>B: token 42
    B->>D: claim with token 42
    D-->>B: accepted
    A->>D: stale claim with token 41
    D-->>A: rejected as fenced

    The token must be checked by the resource being protected. A distributed lock that issues tokens but a database that ignores them does not prevent stale writes. Database transaction isolation and constraints remain the final correctness boundary.

    With maximum-seen fencing, token 41 may still be accepted before token 42 reaches storage. If takeover must revoke the old worker immediately, install 42 before reporting takeover complete. etcd's atomic KV guarantees do not extend an atomic transaction to an external inventory store (accessed 2026-09-08).

    Escrow and preallocated rights¶

    Escrow turns one global counter into independently spendable rights. For 10,000 general-admission tickets and 10 reservation partitions, allocate 1,000 rights to each. A partition can create a hold locally only while its balance remains positive. The global invariant holds because total rights never exceeds capacity.

    Rights can become stranded: partition A has 400 idle rights while partition B is sold out. Rebalance using a durable transfer protocol. First freeze or decrement rights at the donor, creating transfer ID T; only then credit the receiver idempotently. Never credit before the debit commits. Periodically reconcile available rights + active holds + sold + rights in transfer = capacity.

    Choose pool size from skew and transfer latency. If a busy partition consumes 200 rights/s and redistribution takes five seconds, retain at least 1,000 rights of local headroom to avoid stalling. Small pools balance efficiently but transfer frequently; large pools reduce coordination but strand more capacity. A central reserve of perhaps 10% can absorb skew without clawing rights back from every partition.

    Escrow fits divisible capacity, not assigned seat A10. Assigned resources can instead be partitioned by section, while the unique seat claim remains authoritative.

    Virtual waiting room¶

    When demand exceeds reservation capacity by an order of magnitude, place a waiting room before expensive authentication, catalog fan-out, and database work. It controls admission rate rather than merely displaying a queue.

    1. On arrival, create a durable or verifiable queue entry bound to account, event, and device risk signals.
    2. Apply the declared fairness policy: FIFO by trusted arrival, randomized batches, or explicit priority windows.
    3. Admit at a rate derived from reservation completion and error signals, not a fixed optimistic number.
    4. Issue a short-lived signed token containing event, account, admission epoch, expiry, and nonce.
    5. At the reservation gateway, verify signature and claims, enforce per-account concurrency, and consume or rate-limit the nonce.

    If the reservation tier safely completes 1,500 commands/s, start admission below that, perhaps 1,200/s, and adjust slowly. With 50,000 arrivals and 1,200/s admission, draining the initial crowd takes roughly 42 seconds before accounting for think time. Show an interval, not a false exact rank, when users may abandon or entries may be weighted.

    Fairness is a product policy, not an implementation detail. Strict global FIFO requires a trusted total order and creates pressure around clock accuracy and geographic latency. Randomized admission can reduce latency advantage but feels less predictable. Priority access must be explicit and auditable. Bind tokens to identity and enforce quotas so bots cannot open thousands of queue positions.

    Reservation algorithm¶

    For assigned seating, process this durable command:

    1. Validate admission token, account limit, show state, and idempotency key.
    2. Read an existing result by (account_id, operation, idempotency_key) and compare its canonical request hash. Return the saved result only for the same request; reject key reuse with different seats or show.
    3. Begin a short transaction. Evaluate expiry using authoritative decision time after acquiring any required ownership lock, not a stale timestamp captured before waiting.
    4. Insert a hold with unique (show_id, seat_id) and expiry, or conditionally change an expired hold.
    5. Insert the idempotency result under a uniqueness constraint and the outbox event in the same transaction. If a concurrent identical request wins the key, roll back this entire attempt and return its committed result; the preliminary read in step 2 is only a fast path.
    6. Commit, then return the hold ID and server deadline.

    Never call the payment provider inside this transaction. Payment uses the hold ID and its own idempotency key. A reconciler queries unknown provider outcomes and applies a conditional state transition based on hold version and policy.

    Failures and overload¶

    Failure Rule
    response lost after claim same idempotency key returns the existing hold
    payment succeeds after hold expiry compensate/refund or honor from a reserved grace pool
    owner crashes durable commands replay; fenced successor owns a higher epoch
    waiting room overload shed before the reservation tier and preserve existing admitted users
    clock disagreement database or authoritative server time controls expiry
    escrow receiver retries transfer transfer ID makes credit idempotent
    database slows lower admission rate; do not allow client retries to multiply load

    Reserve capacity for expiration, reconciliation, and payment callbacks; accepting holds at 100% database capacity can prevent holds from completing or releasing. Under severe overload, prioritize already-issued holds and payment finalization over new browsing and claims. Return stable outcomes: 409 for unavailable inventory, 429 for account or admission limits, and 503 when authoritative decisions cannot safely be made.

    Migration path¶

    1. Begin with a transactional unique constraint or conditional counter.
    2. Add idempotency, short transactions, and bounded retries.
    3. Instrument conflict amplification, lock wait, and sold-out attempts.
    4. Add per-event admission and waiting-room tokens before a known onsale.
    5. Serialize exceptionally hot keys or allocate escrow rights where capacity is divisible.
    6. Introduce fencing before moving ownership dynamically.

    Migrate gradually. Shadow a waiting-room decision without enforcing it, validate admission rates, then enable it for a small event. When moving from a global counter to escrow, initialize rights from an authoritative snapshot, pause allocations briefly or replay changes after a watermark, verify conservation, and only then let partitions spend rights. Keep a kill switch that stops new holds while allowing payment completion.

    Observability and verification¶

    Measure arrival, admission, success, and rejection rates separately; queue age; lock wait; deadlocks; CAS attempts per success; hot-key distribution; active holds; expiry lag; escrow rights by partition; fencing rejections; payment unknowns; and waiting-room token abuse. Alert on growing attempt amplification and oldest command age before the database saturates.

    Test response loss after commit, lease expiry during a pause, duplicate escrow transfer, late payment success, database clock boundaries, and owner failover under load. Continuously verify conservation equations and unique constraints. Reconciliation is a safety net, not permission to weaken the write path.

    L5 and L6 guidance¶

    An L5 design should state the invariant, choose the authoritative owner, compare optimistic and pessimistic approaches based on expected conflict, define idempotency and hold expiry, and calculate the admitted rate and queue bound. It should explain why retries stop on terminal conflicts and why payment stays outside the seat-lock transaction.

    An L6 design should model conflict amplification and degraded capacity, define fairness as product policy, fence dynamic owners, and show conservation for escrow transfers. It should prioritize in-flight customers during overload, specify migration and rollback controls, and connect admission rate to live queue age and database health. Senior judgment appears in reducing the scope of coordination instead of hiding it behind more replicas.

    Interview prompts¶

    • What invariant prevents oversell at the final write?
    • Why does optimistic retry collapse at extreme contention?
    • Is strict FIFO required, and what does it cost?
    • How are payment and hold expiry reconciled?
    • Where is a fencing token enforced?
    • How do escrow transfers preserve total capacity?

    Pattern: Multi-Step Workflows¶

    A workflow coordinates durable state transitions across services without pretending they share one transaction. Checkout may reserve inventory, authorize payment, create shipment, and confirm an order. Each service can fail independently, responses can be lost, and the process may take longer than one request or deployment. Correctness comes from durable state, idempotent commands, explicit uncertainty, and reconciliation.

    When to use and avoid¶

    Use a saga when steps cross ownership boundaries, failures need compensation, and progress must survive restarts. Keep operations in one database transaction when they share a storage boundary and require atomicity. Avoid automating an irreversible ambiguous step unless the business defines how to investigate or compensate it.

    Style Strength Cost
    orchestration visible state, timers, and central policy coordinator dependency and coupling to flow
    choreography loose producer coupling emergent cycles and poor end-to-end visibility
    database transaction strongest local atomicity limited to one storage boundary
    manual case handles ambiguous irreversible failure delay and operational expense

    Model business states, not call order¶

    paymentService.authorize() followed by inventoryService.reserve() is a call chain, not a durable workflow. A workflow instance stores its identity, definition version, current state, state version, business references, step attempts, deadlines, and terminal outcome. States should communicate business truth: RESERVING, PAYMENT_UNKNOWN, COMPENSATING, or REVIEW, rather than generic STEP_3.

    Identify the point of no return. Before shipment handoff, payment authorization and inventory holds may be reversible. After a carrier accepts a parcel, "cancel" may mean intercept, return, or refund rather than rollback. Compensation is a new business action with its own failures and audit trail; it does not erase history.

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Reserving
    Reserving --> Authorizing: inventory reserved
    Reserving --> Failed: unavailable
    Authorizing --> Confirming: payment authorized
    Authorizing --> Releasing: payment failed
    Authorizing --> PaymentUnknown: timeout
    PaymentUnknown --> Confirming: reconciliation finds success
    PaymentUnknown --> Releasing: reconciliation finds failure
    Confirming --> Completed: order confirmed
    Confirming --> Review: ambiguous shipment result
    Releasing --> Failed: inventory released

    Durable transition algorithm¶

    Process every event or timer with a transactional compare-and-set on workflow version:

    1. Load the workflow and inbox entry by event ID.
    2. If the event was already consumed, return its recorded result.
    3. Validate that the event is legal in the current state and definition version.
    4. Compute the next state, attempt record, commands, and timers deterministically.
    5. In one local transaction, update state and increment state_version, insert inbox deduplication, append outbox commands, and create or cancel timer rows.
    6. Commit before acknowledging the incoming event.
    7. Let independent relays publish outbox commands and timer notifications at least once.

    Use UPDATE workflow SET state=?, state_version=v+1 WHERE id=? AND state_version=v. If zero rows change, another handler won; reload and decide whether the event is now duplicate, stale, or still applicable. Do not hold the transaction open while calling another service.

    For example, workflow 891 in AUTHORIZING at version 7 receives PaymentAuthorized(command-42). Its transaction writes state CONFIRMING, version 8, inbox event pay-event-9, outbox ConfirmOrder(command-43), and a confirmation deadline. A crash after commit but before broker acknowledgment redelivers pay-event-9; the inbox turns it into a no-op.

    flowchart TD
    E[Event or due timer] --> L[Load workflow and inbox]
    L --> D{Event already consumed?}
    D -- yes --> N[Return recorded result]
    D -- no --> V{Legal for state and version?}
    V -- no --> Q[Quarantine or ignore stale event]
    V -- yes --> C[Compute transition]
    C --> T[Transaction: state + inbox + outbox + timers]
    T --> P[Relay publishes command]
    P --> X[Remote service executes idempotently]

    Transactional outbox and inbox¶

    The dual-write problem appears if an orchestrator commits state and separately publishes a command. A crash between operations either loses the command or sends it while state remains old. An outbox solves this within one database: business state and an unpublished message commit atomically. A relay polls or streams rows, publishes, then marks progress.

    Publication is at least once. If the relay publishes and crashes before marking the row, it publishes again. Therefore the receiver needs an inbox or operation table keyed by stable command ID. The receiver transactionally records the ID with its business mutation and returns the original result on duplicate delivery.

    Retention needs care. Do not delete inbox records before the maximum retry and replay horizon unless another permanent business key prevents repetition. Archive outbox rows after broker retention and reconciliation requirements are met. Monitor oldest unpublished row; a healthy orchestrator with a stuck relay still makes no progress.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant O as Orchestrator DB
    participant R as Outbox relay
    participant B as Broker
    participant P as Payment service
    O->>O: commit state and command-42
    R->>O: read unpublished command-42
    R->>B: publish command-42
    B->>P: deliver command-42
    P->>P: commit inbox and durable authorization intent
    Note over P: external provider call uses stable key after commit
    P-->>B: ack
    R--xR: crash before marking publish
    R->>B: republish command-42
    B->>P: redeliver command-42
    P-->>B: acknowledge existing pending intent
    Note over P: pending intent is not payment success

    Timers that survive restarts¶

    In-memory sleep and process schedulers lose deadlines during restarts. Store each timer with workflow ID, timer type, due time, expected state/version, and unique timer ID. A scheduler scans due ranges, leases timers, and emits them at least once. The transition handler decides whether a late or duplicate timer still applies.

    Partition timers by due-time bucket plus hash of workflow ID. A single ordered index on due_at can become a hot range. For 20 million active payment deadlines spread over 15 minutes, average expiry is about 22,222/s. If the scheduler handles 5,000 timers/s per shard, at least five shards are needed, plus failure headroom. Scan slightly ahead into bounded batches but do not mark a timer complete until its event is durably accepted.

    Timer correctness cannot depend on exact firing time. A five-minute deadline means the transition becomes eligible after authoritative time reaches the deadline. The handler compares stored deadline and current database/server time. A delayed timer should still fire; an early or stale timer should not transition the workflow. If an early notification arrives while its timer is still valid, retain or re-arm the durable timer for its due time. Consuming the sole notification as a no-op would otherwise lose the deadline. Cancellation can be represented by status or superseded version rather than requiring race-free physical deletion.

    Unknown outcomes and late success¶

    A timeout says the caller does not know the outcome; it does not prove failure. Retrying payment with the same provider idempotency key is safe only within the provider's contract. Stripe may prune keys after at least 24 hours, making reuse a new request. Without a valid deduplication guarantee, a negative merchant-reference lookup does not rule out an in-flight or not-yet-visible charge. Record PAYMENT_UNKNOWN, reconcile, and escalate rather than treating absence as permission to charge again (accessed 2026-09-08).

    Consider a 10-second client timeout, a two-minute inventory hold, and a payment provider that answers after 130 seconds. By then the hold expired. The workflow needs an ordered policy:

    1. Query whether inventory can be reacquired or whether a small grace allocation exists.
    2. If capacity can be honored, reserve idempotently and continue confirmation.
    3. Otherwise create an idempotent refund or void command.
    4. Keep the order in REFUNDING until provider confirmation; do not label it FAILED while money remains captured.
    5. Escalate to REVIEW after bounded automated attempts.

    Late success events carry original command ID and provider reference. The transition table defines their meaning in every reachable state. Ignoring an event merely because the workflow moved on can lose money. Reconciliation should periodically compare local records to provider settlements and inventory ownership, independent of normal event flow.

    Retry and compensation policy¶

    Classify errors as terminal business failure, transient technical failure, unknown outcome, or invariant violation. Retry transient operations with exponential backoff, full jitter, and a deadline. Keep attempt count and next-attempt time durable. Do not retry card declines or unavailable inventory. Do not execute compensation merely because a request timed out.

    Compensations run in reverse dependency order when required, but parallelize independent actions. Each compensation has a command ID, idempotency contract, retry budget, and terminal escalation. A failed inventory release may be retried for hours; a failed refund may require immediate operations visibility. The original workflow terminal state should distinguish FAILED_COMPENSATED, FAILED_COMPENSATION_PENDING, and REVIEW where product semantics warrant it.

    Quantitative capacity example¶

    Suppose checkout peaks at 8,000 workflows/s. Each successful workflow performs four durable transitions, two timer writes/cancellations, and four outbox inserts. That is roughly 32,000 state transitions/s and 32,000 outbox rows/s, not 8,000 writes/s. If 5% of payment calls become unknown and each causes two reconciliation attempts, add 8,000 * 0.05 * 2 = 800 commands/s plus timer traffic.

    At an average 1.5 KiB across state, inbox, and outbox writes per transition, 32,000 transitions/s produce 49.152 MB/s (46.875 MiB/s) logical database writes before indexes, WAL, replication, and compaction. Partition by order_id for local workflow ordering. Provision the relay for catch-up: after a ten-minute outage at 32,000 outbox rows/s, backlog is 19.2 million messages. A relay that resumes at only the normal rate never catches up; at 64,000/s it needs another ten minutes while new traffic continues.

    Little's Law also sizes active workflow state. At 8,000 starts/s and a 30-second average completion, about 240,000 workflows are active. A payment incident that raises average completion to ten minutes creates 4.8 million active instances and many due timers, even if start rate is unchanged.

    Failures and overload¶

    Failure Recovery
    request times out query step by idempotency key before retry
    duplicate event inbox or event ID suppresses repeated transition
    compensation fails retry independently, then operator queue
    event arrives out of order validate expected workflow state and version
    orchestrator restarts scan durable timers and resume instances
    irreconcilable outcome enter REVIEW; never report false success
    outbox relay stalls state remains durable; alert on outbox age and catch up
    stale workflow worker conditional state version rejects its transition

    During overload, protect transitions that reduce liability: payment result ingestion, refunds, hold releases, and reconciliation should outrank new starts. Apply per-tenant admission before creating workflows. Bound concurrent calls to each dependency with separate pools or bulkheads. If payment is degraded, stop admitting checkouts before millions enter PAYMENT_UNKNOWN.

    Expose accepted, pending, completed, failed, compensating, refunding, and review states honestly. A 202 means accepted for processing, not purchased. Provide stable status and safe client retry semantics.

    Workflow version migration¶

    Persist definition_version on every instance. New workflows use version 3 while running version-2 instances continue with version-2 transition logic. This is safest when old logic can remain deployed until its instances drain.

    When migration is necessary, define an explicit mapping from each old state and data shape to a valid new state. Run migration as a conditional transition: read version and state, transform data, update definition and state version atomically, and record a migration event. Timers and pending commands require mapping too. Never reinterpret an already-issued command under new semantics.

    Use counts by definition and state to estimate drain time. If 2 million workflows run v2 and 99.9% finish within 24 hours, wait before migrating the remaining 0.1%; those 2,000 exceptional instances deserve a reviewed migration rather than a broad risky rewrite. Canary new definitions by tenant or percentage, compare transition and compensation rates, and retain rollback for workflows that have not crossed an incompatible point of no return.

    Schema changes should be additive through the maximum workflow lifetime. Readers supply defaults for old payloads; writers include schema version. Avoid deleting an event field while delayed messages containing the old shape can still arrive.

    Evolution path¶

    1. Keep order-local changes in one service and database transaction.
    2. Add idempotent remote command IDs and persist workflow state.
    3. Replace fragile call chains with durable transitions, outbox/inbox, and timers.
    4. Add unknown-outcome states and independent reconciliation.
    5. Partition by order_id, introduce priority and dependency bulkheads, and test catch-up.
    6. Version workflow definitions so in-flight instances finish or migrate explicitly.

    Migrate from synchronous checkout by first recording shadow workflow state around the existing path. Move one remote step at a time behind an outbox. Preserve the old response contract until status polling or callbacks are available. Reconcile old and new paths before increasing traffic.

    Observability and verification¶

    Measure starts and completions; active and oldest age by state and definition; transition conflicts; timer lateness; outbox age; inbox duplicates; retry and unknown-outcome rates; compensation and refund backlog; dependency latency; and reconciliation mismatches. Attach workflow ID, command ID, and provider reference to traces without exposing sensitive data.

    Test a crash before and after every transaction boundary, response loss after remote success, duplicate and reordered events, late timers, late payment success, compensation failure, relay outage and catch-up, and mixed workflow versions. Model-based tests can generate event permutations and verify that impossible states such as COMPLETED without durable payment never occur.

    L5 and L6 guidance¶

    An L5 design should identify service boundaries and the point of no return, draw explicit states, define idempotency and unknown outcomes, and explain atomic state-plus-outbox transitions. It should show durable timers, compensation semantics, and user-visible status.

    An L6 design should quantify active instances and catch-up capacity, prioritize liability-reducing work under overload, design provider reconciliation, and migrate long-lived workflow versions safely. It should challenge whether every step belongs in the saga and define operational ownership for REVIEW. Senior design treats late success and old-version execution as normal states, not edge cases.

    For bounded model planning, typed tools, principal-bound capabilities, durable agent replay, memory, and human approval, continue with the Applied AI Systems track.

    Interview prompts¶

    • Which step is the point of no return?
    • What does each compensation mean to the business?
    • How are unknown outcomes distinguished from failures?
    • How are timers persisted and deduplicated?
    • What happens when payment succeeds after inventory expiry?
    • How are running workflows migrated to a new definition?

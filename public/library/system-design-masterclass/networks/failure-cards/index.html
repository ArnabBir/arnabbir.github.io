    Network Failure Cards¶

    Use one card at a time during incident drills. Begin with discriminating evidence, not a preferred diagnosis. Calculations are order-of-magnitude fixtures; replace them with measured values before acting in production. For every observation, record vantage point, time window, clock source, sampling or drop behavior, relevant counter semantics, and at least one fact the evidence cannot establish.

    These cards are answer-bearing facilitator references, not executable faults or blind prompts. Disclose symptoms first during a blind drill. Never restore compromised trust or unsafe policy merely because a card says "restore prior". Expiry does not automatically terminate live TLS sessions; include non-resumed handshakes in recovery proof. Primary mechanisms are in RFC 9293, RFC 9846, RFC 9000, and RFC 2308; numbers below are fixtures.

    Card 1: DNS answers remain stale¶

    Symptoms: Traffic continues to a retired address after a record change. Failures vary by client network or recursive resolver.

    Discriminators: Compare authoritative answers, recursive-cache answers, and client connection destinations. Check the old TTL and existing connection age.

    Evidence: Timestamped dig-equivalent captures from several resolvers, DNS change audit, per-address connection counts, oldest live connection.

    False conclusions: "The 30-second TTL guarantees migration in 30 seconds." "All remaining traffic is malicious."

    Safe mitigation: Restore the old endpoint if safe, publish overlapping answers, and drain only after old TTL and connection lifetimes decay.

    Rollback: Restore the prior answer set and weights; verify both authoritative and recursive views.

    Owner: DNS control-plane owner with endpoint owner as incident co-owner.

    Calculation: If the previous TTL was 1,800 s and changed at 12:00, a cache refreshed at 11:59:59 may retain the answer until nearly 12:29:59.

    Card 2: Resolver or authoritative DNS failure¶

    Symptoms: Name-resolution latency and SERVFAIL rise while direct-address probes remain healthy.

    Discriminators: Separate stub, recursive, delegation, DNSSEC, and authoritative failures. Compare cached and uncached names.

    Evidence: Resolution phase timing, response codes, recursion path, DNSSEC validation result, cache hit rate.

    False conclusions: "The application is down because requests never arrived." "Increasing application replicas will help."

    Safe mitigation: Fail over to a validated recursive or authoritative path, preserve negative-cache semantics, and reduce nonessential lookup churn.

    Rollback: Return traffic gradually to the repaired resolver while comparing answer parity and latency.

    Owner: DNS platform owner; security owner joins for DNSSEC or suspicious answer changes.

    Calculation: At 50,000 requests/s and a drop from 95 to 60 percent cache hits, upstream DNS load rises from 2,500 to 20,000 queries/s, an 8x increase.

    Card 3: Path packet loss¶

    Symptoms: Tail latency and retransmissions rise before total request errors; large transfers degrade more than small probes.

    Discriminators: Break down by path, protocol, packet size, zone, and destination. Compare TCP retransmissions or QUIC loss with server queue time.

    Evidence: Loss and retransmission counters, RTT distribution, congestion window, interface drops, path traces.

    False conclusions: "CPU is low, so capacity is fine." "A successful ping proves the path is healthy."

    Safe mitigation: Stop retry amplification, shift only to proven spare paths, and lower admission to protect useful goodput.

    Rollback: Restore routing after loss and goodput remain stable through a full observation window.

    Owner: Network path owner with service owner controlling retries and admission.

    Calculation: With framing excluded, 400 MB delivered plus 60 MB retransmitted yields 400 / 460 = 86.96 percent payload transmission efficiency. Actual wire efficiency is lower after headers and other traffic are included.

    Card 4: MTU or PMTU black hole¶

    Symptoms: Small requests work, larger TLS records or responses stall, and connection setup may succeed.

    Discriminators: Test deterministic payload sizes with and without fragmentation-related behavior. Compare IPv4 and IPv6 paths and tunnel overhead.

    Evidence: Packet-size threshold, ICMP Packet Too Big visibility, retransmission pattern, interface and tunnel MTUs.

    False conclusions: "TLS is broken because the stall begins after ClientHello." "The database is slow because large responses time out."

    Safe mitigation: Restore PMTU signaling or apply a validated MSS clamp for TCP at the correct boundary. An MSS clamp does not fix UDP/QUIC packet sizing. Avoid globally shrinking MTU without evidence.

    Rollback: Remove the temporary clamp after end-to-end probes pass at expected sizes.

    Owner: Network/tunnel owner; host networking owner validates endpoint MTU.

    Calculation: A 1,500-byte underlay with 70 bytes of tunnel overhead leaves 1,430 bytes before inner-protocol headers.

    Card 5: NAT source-port exhaustion¶

    Symptoms: New outbound connections fail while established ones continue; failures concentrate on a destination tuple.

    Discriminators: Compare active translations, allocation failures, new-connection rate, reuse lifetime, and connection-pool reuse.

    Evidence: NAT table occupancy, ephemeral-port range, destination tuple cardinality, TIME_WAIT or mapping lifetime.

    False conclusions: "The remote service is refusing all traffic." "Low active requests mean ports cannot be exhausted."

    Safe mitigation: Reuse connections, reduce churn and retries, add source addresses or validated NAT capacity.

    Rollback: Remove emergency source capacity only after churn and allocation-failure alerts remain normal.

    Owner: Egress/network owner with client-library owner.

    Calculation: 28,000 ports / 60 s = 467 new connections/s per source/destination tuple is an idealized ceiling with negligible active lifetime. Include active lifetime as well as the non-reuse interval when estimating actual port occupancy.

    Card 6: Conntrack table saturation¶

    Symptoms: New flows drop across otherwise unrelated services; existing flows can remain healthy.

    Discriminators: Compare table occupancy and insert failures with NIC, CPU, and application errors. Inspect flow lifetime by state.

    Evidence: Entries by protocol/state, insert/drop counters, arrival and expiry rates, configured limit.

    False conclusions: "Every affected service failed simultaneously." "Raising the table limit alone removes the cause."

    Safe mitigation: Shed new flows, stop retries, expire only states proven safe, and add capacity after memory impact is understood.

    Rollback: Restore normal admission and limits gradually while checking occupancy slope.

    Owner: Host or gateway networking owner.

    Calculation: With 100,000 free entries and net growth of 8,000/s, exhaustion arrives in 12.5 seconds.

    Card 7: TLS certificate expiry or name mismatch¶

    Symptoms: Handshakes fail before HTTP; failures may depend on SNI, client trust store, or clock.

    Discriminators: Validate chain, hostname, validity, client clock, signature policy, and served certificate by endpoint.

    Evidence: Sanitized handshake error, certificate fingerprint and validity, SNI, endpoint, trust-bundle version.

    False conclusions: "The network is blocked because no HTTP status appears." "Disabling verification is a mitigation."

    Safe mitigation: Serve the approved overlapping certificate and verify canaries across client populations. Never bypass verification.

    Rollback: Re-select the prior still-valid certificate and trust bundle if the new material is wrong.

    Owner: Certificate automation owner and service identity owner.

    Calculation: If rotation starts 48 hours before expiry but global convergence p99 is 60 hours, policy is short by at least 12 hours plus safety margin.

    Card 8: TLS handshake CPU storm¶

    Symptoms: Handshake latency and edge CPU rise; established connections perform better than new ones.

    Discriminators: Compare full versus resumed handshake rate, reconnect events, accept queue, and application CPU.

    Evidence: Handshakes/s by type, resumption hit rate, CPU per handshake, connection-age distribution.

    False conclusions: "TLS traffic volume is unchanged, so TLS cannot be the cause." "Adding retries helps handshakes complete."

    Safe mitigation: Restore resumption, slow reconnects with jitter, preserve warm connections, and shed unauthenticated excess early.

    Rollback: Remove temporary admission limits after resumption and CPU headroom recover.

    Owner: Edge/TLS owner with client connection-policy owner.

    Calculation: 8,000 handshakes/s * 1.2 ms = 9.6 busy CPU cores before safety margin.

    Card 9: Load-balancer target fails without draining¶

    Symptoms: Resets spike during deployment or removal; long requests fail despite healthy replacement targets.

    Discriminators: Compare connection close reason, target lifecycle state, in-flight requests, and termination timing.

    Evidence: Target state timeline, accepted/new/in-flight connections, reset source, deployment audit.

    False conclusions: "Passing health checks makes immediate termination safe." "Connection count equals request count under HTTP/2."

    Safe mitigation: Stop new assignments, keep target alive through bounded drain, then terminate residual work according to protocol policy.

    Rollback: Re-enable the target only if its binary and dependencies are still safe; otherwise pause rollout.

    Owner: Load-balancer platform owner and workload deployment owner.

    Calculation: 24,000 connections / 300 closes/s = 80 s minimum drain without stale new assignments or long-lived outliers.

    Card 10: Health-check false positive or false negative¶

    Symptoms: Healthy targets are ejected, or unhealthy targets keep receiving production traffic.

    Discriminators: Compare probe path with real request dependencies, thresholds, interval, timeout, and source network.

    Evidence: Raw probe outcomes, application SLI by target, ejection reason, dependency state.

    False conclusions: "The health endpoint returned 200, so all traffic is safe." "One timeout proves the target is dead."

    Safe mitigation: Use thresholded state changes, bounded outlier ejection, and a probe that checks only traffic-critical readiness.

    Rollback: Restore the last validated policy and cap simultaneous ejections.

    Owner: Service owner defines readiness; load-balancer owner implements policy.

    Calculation: With 3 failures required at 5-second intervals, detection is roughly 10 seconds after the first failed probe. From an arbitrary failure onset it is roughly 10-15 seconds, plus relevant probe duration and propagation, depending on whether intervals are measured between probe starts or completions.

    Card 11: Proxy connection-pool saturation¶

    Symptoms: Pool-acquisition time rises, upstream service time stays flat, and proxy queues grow.

    Discriminators: Separate queue time from connect and service time. Check per-destination pool keys and multiplexed stream limits.

    Evidence: In-use/idle/pending counts, acquisition timeout, upstream latency, requests per connection.

    False conclusions: "The upstream is slow because end-to-end latency rose." "An unbounded pool fixes saturation."

    Safe mitigation: Bound and shed the queue, fix skewed pool keys, and increase concurrency only within measured upstream capacity.

    Rollback: Restore the prior pool limit if upstream saturation or error rate increases.

    Owner: Proxy/client-platform owner with dependency owner approving capacity.

    Calculation: At 18,000 calls/s and 22 ms mean time, mean active calls are 18,000 * 0.022 = 396.

    Card 12: Layered retry storm¶

    Symptoms: Attempted load grows while logical traffic is stable; recovery slows after a transient fault.

    Discriminators: Correlate stable operation IDs across client, gateway, service, and dependency attempts.

    Evidence: Attempts per logical operation, retry reason, backoff, deadline remaining, per-layer retry config.

    False conclusions: "Each layer only retries once, so amplification is small." "Retries improve availability regardless of capacity."

    Safe mitigation: Select one retry owner, enforce a shared budget, add jitter, and shed work with insufficient deadline.

    Rollback: Re-enable retries in a canary only after dependency headroom and attempt ratios recover.

    Owner: End-to-end API owner; each platform owner removes hidden retry layers.

    Calculation: Three client attempts, two gateway attempts, and two library attempts produce up to 3 * 2 * 2 = 12 dependency attempts.

    Card 13: HTTP/2 connection concentration¶

    Symptoms: One connection loss or congestion event affects many streams; target load is uneven despite few connections.

    Discriminators: Compare streams and outstanding work per connection, not only connection count. Identify stream limits and connection age.

    Evidence: Active streams/connection, queued streams, resets, per-connection RTT and loss, target work.

    False conclusions: "Least connections means least work." "One multiplexed connection is always most efficient."

    Safe mitigation: Use a small bounded set of connections, cap concurrent streams, and balance by outstanding work or measured load.

    Rollback: Return to the prior connection count if handshakes, target skew, or NAT churn worsen.

    Owner: HTTP client/proxy owner.

    Calculation: Losing one connection carrying 800 of 1,000 active streams disrupts 80 percent of work even if nine idle connections survive.

    Card 14: QUIC or UDP path impairment¶

    Symptoms: HTTP/3 handshake or throughput fails on selected networks while HTTP/2 fallback works.

    Discriminators: Break down by ASN, device, UDP reachability, QUIC version, RTT, and loss; compare fallback success.

    Evidence: QUIC handshake phase, UDP timeout, version negotiation, fallback latency, path MTU and loss.

    False conclusions: "QUIC is globally broken." "Fallback success means the incident has no user impact."

    Safe mitigation: Use bounded, remembered fallback and reduce HTTP/3 exposure for affected cohorts without disabling it globally.

    Rollback: Restore cohort rollout after path-specific canaries meet handshake and goodput gates.

    Owner: Edge transport owner; client owner controls fallback behavior.

    Calculation: A failed 300 ms QUIC attempt before a 165 ms TCP/TLS path yields at least 465 ms, not the 165 ms fallback alone.

    Card 15: Anycast site withdrawal overloads neighbors¶

    Symptoms: A withdrawn site recovers locally while latency and errors rise in neighboring catchments.

    Discriminators: Map route withdrawals to shifted traffic and post-shift headroom at every receiving site.

    Evidence: BGP announcements, traffic by site/ASN, capacity ceilings, cache warmth, retry rates.

    False conclusions: "Withdrawal removes load from the system." "Traffic shifts evenly."

    Safe mitigation: Stage withdrawal, cap advertised scope where possible, prewarm receivers, and shed noncritical traffic before ceilings.

    Rollback: Re-announce only if the original site is safe and route flapping risk is controlled.

    Owner: Global traffic owner with receiving-site owners holding veto over capacity.

    Calculation: A 220 Gbps withdrawal shifting 55 percent to site B adds 121 Gbps there.

    Card 16: BGP route leak or hijack signal¶

    Symptoms: Reachability or latency changes by Internet origin; an unexpected origin AS or path appears.

    Discriminators: Compare control-plane collectors, configured origins, route-object/RPKI state, and data-plane probes.

    Evidence: Prefix, origin AS, AS path, first-seen time, RPKI validation, affected catchments.

    False conclusions: "Application health checks prove routing integrity." "One looking glass represents the whole Internet."

    Safe mitigation: Apply validated prefix filters, coordinate upstream withdrawal or correction, and communicate affected scope. Do not improvise broader announcements.

    Rollback: Remove emergency routing changes in stages after global collectors and probes converge.

    Owner: Network routing owner and security incident owner.

    Calculation: If 35 percent of 80,000 requests/s follow the bad route, up to 28,000 requests/s are exposed before retries.

    Card 17: WebSocket reconnect storm¶

    Symptoms: A brief edge event becomes sustained handshake, authentication, and subscription load.

    Discriminators: Compare reconnect attempts, unique clients, backoff distributions, session-resume success, and downstream replay.

    Evidence: Attempts/client, reconnect delay histogram, TLS CPU, auth QPS, subscription restore size.

    False conclusions: "Traffic is organic growth." "Immediate reconnect minimizes downtime for everyone."

    Safe mitigation: Issue randomized retry guidance, cap reconnect admission, resume from durable cursor, and degrade noncritical subscriptions.

    Rollback: Relax admission slowly after connection-age distribution and downstream queues normalize.

    Owner: Realtime platform owner with client SDK owner.

    Calculation: One million clients reconnecting uniformly over 5 seconds create 200,000 attempts/s; spreading over 120 seconds lowers the mean to about 8,333/s.

    Card 18: Service-mesh sidecar saturation¶

    Symptoms: Service latency rises with sidecar CPU, pending requests, or connection limits while application work stays stable.

    Discriminators: Separate application and proxy timing. Compare sidecar policy/config version and per-cluster pools.

    Evidence: Downstream/upstream request timing, proxy CPU/memory, pending overflow, config distribution state.

    False conclusions: "The application must scale because the pod is slow." "A transparent proxy has no independent capacity ceiling."

    Safe mitigation: Shed at ingress, reduce expensive filters where policy permits, restore known-good config, and scale proxy capacity with measured limits.

    Rollback: Reapply the previous signed config snapshot and verify policy plus traffic behavior.

    Owner: Mesh platform owner; service owner controls workload admission.

    Calculation: At 25,000 requests/s and 0.08 ms proxy CPU per request per direction, two directions consume 25,000 * 0.00008 * 2 = 4 cores.

    Card 19: mTLS trust-bundle split¶

    Symptoms: Calls fail only for selected workload versions or directions during certificate rotation.

    Discriminators: Compare presented issuer, trust-bundle version, certificate epoch, SNI/SAN, and clock on both peers.

    Evidence: Non-secret fingerprints, issuer/subject/SAN, bundle hashes, rollout cohort, handshake error.

    False conclusions: "The new certificate is valid, so every peer trusts it." "Restarting all workloads is the safest repair."

    Safe mitigation: Restore dual trust before changing identity, pause issuance rollout, and canary every communication direction.

    Rollback: Reissue or select the prior still-valid identity while retaining both trusted roots through overlap.

    Owner: Identity platform owner with each service owner validating policy.

    Calculation: A 24-hour certificate with 6-hour worst-case rollout and 4-hour rollback window has only 14 hours left before any added safety margin.

    Card 20: Cross-zone dependency amplification¶

    Symptoms: Network cost and inter-zone bandwidth rise without matching logical traffic growth; zone impairment affects otherwise local calls.

    Discriminators: Map request and response bytes by source/destination zone, retry count, and replica placement.

    Evidence: Flow logs, topology labels, logical versus attempted QPS, response-size distribution, billing dimensions.

    False conclusions: "Round robin is cost-neutral." "Strict locality is always safer."

    Safe mitigation: Prefer locality with bounded spillover, preserve zonal failover headroom, and stop retries that duplicate large responses.

    Rollback: Restore broader balancing if local queues or zonal error budgets degrade.

    Owner: Service owner and traffic platform owner; finance provides billing validation.

    Calculation: 0.96 GB/s * 86,400 s * $0.01/GB = $829.44/day for one billed direction.

    Card-use checklist¶

    • Identify the failure boundary before changing capacity or routing.
    • Capture a timestamped baseline and one discriminator.
    • Record evidence vantage, interval, clock, sampling/drop behavior, counter semantics, and one explicit limitation.
    • Quantify time to exhaustion or post-failover headroom.
    • Assign one incident owner and one rollback authority.
    • Prefer reversible, scoped mitigation over fleet-wide changes.
    • Verify recovery using user, transport, and capacity evidence.

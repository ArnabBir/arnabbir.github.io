    Google News: A Global News Aggregator¶

    This chapter develops an end-to-end design for a Google News-like product. It is structured as both a detailed study guide and a 45-60 minute system design interview walkthrough.

    The central challenge is scaling reads without sacrificing freshness. A news platform must continuously ingest articles from thousands of publishers, group duplicate coverage into stories, rank the result, and serve an effectively infinite feed in under 200 ms, including during unpredictable breaking-news spikes.

    1. Interview Roadmap¶

    Phase Time Goal
    Requirements 5 minutes Define feed behavior, freshness, and scale
    Entities and APIs 5 minutes Establish data ownership and client contracts
    High-level design 15 minutes Build ingestion and feed-serving paths
    Deep dives 15-20 minutes Pagination, caching, freshness, media, and spikes
    Extensions and wrap-up 5-10 minutes Categories, personalization, failures, tradeoffs

    The interview should distinguish two independent paths early:

    1. The write or ingestion path discovers and processes publisher articles.
    2. The read or serving path returns ranked feed pages to users.

    Decoupling these paths lets ingestion continue when serving is busy and lets cached feeds remain available when ingestion is delayed.

    2. Understanding the Product¶

    A news aggregator does not usually author the full article. It discovers content published elsewhere and presents a compact representation:

    • Headline and short snippet.
    • Publisher and publication time.
    • Thumbnail or media preview.
    • Topic or category.
    • A group of publishers covering the same underlying story.
    • A link to the publisher's website for the full article.

    The feed is not merely a reverse-chronological table scan. It balances freshness, importance, source quality, topic diversity, geographic relevance, and duplicate suppression.

    3. Requirements¶

    3.1 Functional Requirements¶

    Core requirements¶

    1. Users can view an aggregated feed of news from thousands of publishers worldwide.
    2. Users can scroll through the feed effectively infinitely.
    3. Users can click an article and be redirected to the publisher's website.

    Clarifications¶

    • The home feed is available to anonymous users.
    • Articles should normally appear within 30 minutes of publication.
    • Multiple articles about the same event should be grouped into one story cluster.
    • The feed may be slightly stale; availability is more important than strict consistency.
    • Deleted, retracted, or policy-violating articles should be removed promptly.
    • Feed pages should not repeat an item within a user's scrolling session.
    • The core design uses a globally or regionally ranked feed, not mandatory personalization.

    Out of scope¶

    • Publisher registration and commercial agreements.
    • Full web-scale crawling of arbitrary sites.
    • User comments and social interactions.
    • Subscription payments and publisher paywall handling.
    • Full misinformation or editorial-policy systems.
    • Saved articles and social sharing.

    Bonus extensions¶

    • Category feeds such as Sports, Politics, Business, and Technology.
    • Personalized feeds based on explicit interests and reading behavior.
    • Local and language-specific editions.

    3.2 Non-Functional Requirements¶

    Requirement Target Why it matters
    Feed latency p95 below 200 ms Scrolling is an interactive, high-frequency path
    Feed availability 99.99% Slightly old news is preferable to no feed
    Freshness 99% of eligible articles discoverable within 30 minutes News rapidly loses value
    Redirect latency p95 below 100 ms A click should feel immediate
    Durability Do not lose accepted article metadata Derived feeds can be rebuilt from canonical articles
    Consistency Eventual for feed ranking and counters Strong consistency is unnecessary for most reads
    Geographic reach Multi-region News readership and publishers are global
    Spike tolerance 10-50x for major events Breaking news creates correlated traffic

    3.3 Correctness Invariants¶

    • One accepted publisher item maps idempotently to one canonical article identity even when polling, push delivery, or event processing retries.
    • A published feed version references only fully written segments; readers never observe a partially activated snapshot.
    • A cursor remains bound to one immutable feed version and position, so pagination does not duplicate or skip items because a newer feed was published.
    • Retractions, policy blocks, and unsafe redirect destinations override ordinary ranking and cache freshness; tombstones prevent delayed events from republishing them.
    • Redirects resolve only to the validated URL stored for an accepted article and publisher, never to a caller-supplied destination.
    • Canonical articles and cluster state are durable sources of truth; feeds, caches, search indexes, and engagement counters are rebuildable projections.
    • At-least-once delivery may repeat work but cannot create duplicate canonical articles or apply the same entity version twice.

    3.4 Capacity Estimates¶

    Assume:

    • 100M daily active users.
    • 20 feed page requests per user per day.
    • 20 cards per feed page.
    • 10M article clicks per day.
    • 100,000 active publishers.
    • 10M new article versions per day before filtering.
    • 2M accepted new articles per day.
    • 5 KB normalized metadata per article, excluding media.
    • 90-day hot article retention in the serving databases.

    Traffic:

    Feed page requests/day = 100M * 20 = 2B
Average feed QPS       = 2B / 86,400 ~= 23,000
Peak feed QPS          = 5 * average ~= 115,000
Breaking-news peak     = 10-50 * normal peak for hot objects

Clicks/day             = 10M
Average redirect QPS   ~= 116
Peak redirect QPS      ~= 1,000+

Accepted articles/day  = 2M
Average accepted rate  ~= 23 articles/second
Burst ingestion rate   = hundreds or thousands/second

    Metadata storage:

    Articles/year          = 2M * 365 = 730M
Raw metadata/year      = 730M * 5 KB ~= 3.65 TB
Indexes and replicas   ~= 10-20 TB/year

    Feed card delivery dominates system traffic. This immediately suggests aggressive precomputation, caching, compact responses, and CDN or edge participation.

    Article versus story cluster¶

    An article belongs to one publisher and has one destination URL. A story cluster represents an underlying event covered by one or more articles. The feed should primarily rank clusters, then select a representative article and expose alternative sources.

    Without clustering, a major event may occupy every feed position with nearly identical headlines.

    5. API Design¶

    5.1 Get the home feed¶

    GET /v1/feed?edition=en-US&limit=20&cursor=<opaque-token>

    Response:

    {
  "items": [
    {
      "storyId": "story_9f3",
      "headline": "Central bank announces new rate decision",
      "summary": "The central bank announced...",
      "publishedAt": "2026-09-05T12:34:00Z",
      "primaryArticle": {
        "articleId": "article_a72",
        "publisher": "Example News",
        "targetUrl": "https://news.example.com/article/abc"
      },
      "sourceCount": 42,
      "thumbnailUrl": "https://media.news.example/story_9f3/640x360.webp",
      "categories": ["Business", "Economy"]
    }
  ],
  "nextCursor": "eyJmZWVkVmVyc2lvbiI6...",
  "generatedAt": "2026-09-05T12:35:00Z"
}

    The cursor should encode or reference:

    • Feed key or edition.
    • Feed snapshot version.
    • Last consumed position or ranking tuple.
    • Expiry time.
    • Optional user/session ID for deduplication.

    Sign and encode the cursor so clients cannot alter internal pagination state.

    5.2 Get a category feed¶

    GET /v1/feed/categories/technology?edition=en-US&limit=20&cursor=...

    This can use the same serving infrastructure with a different feed key: category:technology:en-US.

    5.3 Get a story cluster¶

    GET /v1/stories/{story_id}

    Returns the representative story plus alternative publisher coverage. This endpoint is optional for the core problem but naturally follows from deduplication.

    5.4 Redirect to the publisher¶

    Option A: return the publisher URL in the feed and let the client navigate directly.

    Option B: use a lightweight redirect endpoint for click analytics:

    GET /v1/articles/{article_id}/redirect

    Response:

    HTTP/1.1 302 Found
Location: https://publisher.example/article/abc
Cache-Control: no-store

    The analytics event must not block the redirect. Emit it asynchronously or log at the edge.

    7.2 Source discovery¶

    Prefer explicit publisher integrations:

    • RSS or Atom feeds.
    • Publisher APIs.
    • News sitemaps.
    • WebSub or webhook pushes.
    • Controlled page fetching for approved sources without feeds.

    Publisher pushes offer the lowest latency, but the system cannot require every publisher to support them. Polling remains necessary.

    7.3 Adaptive scheduling¶

    Polling every source at the same rate is wasteful. A national breaking-news publisher may update every minute, while a weekly local paper may update once a day.

    Maintain a source state:

    next_fetch_at
recent_publish_rate
last_success_at
consecutive_failures
etag
last_modified
publisher_priority

    A simplified interval policy:

    interval = clamp(
    3600 * target_updates_per_poll / max(expected_updates_per_hour, rate_floor),
    minimum_interval,
    maximum_interval
)

    Then adjust it:

    Here interval is seconds, target_updates_per_poll is a dimensionless desired batch size, and rate_floor is a positive updates/hour floor. Cap maximum_interval by the source's discovery-delay budget; a quiet source cannot silently escape a promised freshness tier.

    • Fetch sooner when recent requests found new content.
    • Back off when requests repeatedly return 304 Not Modified.
    • Exponentially back off on errors.
    • Temporarily accelerate trusted sources during breaking events.
    • Add jitter to prevent synchronized request bursts.
    • Respect Retry-After, robots rules, and publisher-specific quotas.

    7.4 Safe and polite fetching¶

    • Limit concurrency per publisher domain.
    • Use strict connection and response-size limits.
    • Reject private-network addresses to prevent SSRF.
    • Parse untrusted XML and HTML in sandboxed workers.
    • Disable dangerous XML external entities.
    • Identify the crawler and honor publisher agreements.
    • Avoid downloading full pages when feed metadata is sufficient.

    7.5 Canonicalization and exact deduplication¶

    The same article may appear through RSS, a sitemap, mobile URL, tracking URL, and syndication feed.

    Normalize URLs by:

    • Lowercasing hostnames.
    • Removing fragments.
    • Removing known tracking parameters such as utm_*.
    • Resolving redirects and canonical-link metadata.
    • Applying publisher-specific URL rules.

    Create a unique key such as:

    SHA-256(publisher_id + canonical_url)

    Also calculate a normalized-content hash. Storage-level uniqueness and idempotent upserts protect against retries and duplicate feed entries.

    7.6 Near-duplicate detection and story clustering¶

    Exact URL deduplication does not group different publishers covering the same event.

    Use a staged pipeline:

    1. Extract title, entities, locations, keywords, and publication time.
    2. Generate a text embedding or MinHash signature.
    3. Retrieve candidate clusters from the same language and recent time window.
    4. Compare semantic similarity, entity overlap, and time proximity.
    5. Join the best matching cluster above a threshold; otherwise create a new cluster.

    Example score:

    cluster_similarity =
    0.55 * embedding_similarity
  + 0.20 * named_entity_overlap
  + 0.15 * title_token_similarity
  + 0.10 * time_proximity

    Cluster updates are asynchronous and revisable. Two initial clusters can later merge as more evidence arrives. The feed references stable cluster IDs or receives a redirect from merged IDs. Freeze cluster identity for snapshot pagination, or resolve aliases against a pinned clustering version and deduplicate canonical cluster IDs in session state. Live merges can otherwise make two distinct snapshot IDs render as the same story; immutable positions alone do not prevent that semantic duplicate.

    7.7 Classification and eligibility¶

    Before entering feeds, derive:

    • Language and edition eligibility.
    • Topic categories.
    • Geographic relevance.
    • Named entities and event type.
    • Content safety and spam score.
    • Publisher quality and policy status.

    The classifier emits versioned predictions. Reclassification should not overwrite audit history and should trigger feed updates when eligibility changes.

    8. Feed Generation and Ranking¶

    8.1 Fan-out on read, fan-out on write, or hybrid¶

    Strategy Behavior Strength Weakness
    Rank on read Build each feed during the request Maximum freshness and flexibility High latency and repeated computation
    Precompute every user feed Materialize complete user feeds Fast reads Expensive writes and difficult invalidation
    Precompute shared feeds Materialize global, edition, and category feeds Excellent cache reuse Limited personalization
    Hybrid Shared candidates plus lightweight user reranking Good balance More serving complexity

    For the core anonymous product, precompute shared feeds by edition and category. This is the most important read-scaling decision.

    8.2 Candidate generation¶

    Generate candidates from:

    • Recently created or updated story clusters.
    • Trusted-source breaking-news signals.
    • Region and language eligibility.
    • Category membership.
    • Trending click and engagement counters.

    Candidate generation should retrieve hundreds or thousands of plausible stories, not scan every article.

    8.3 Ranking signals¶

    A deterministic initial score can combine:

    score =
    w1 * freshness_decay
  + w2 * story_importance
  + w3 * source_quality
  + w4 * corroborating_source_count
  + w5 * regional_relevance
  + w6 * engagement_velocity
  + w7 * content_quality

    Freshness may use exponential decay:

    freshness_decay = exp(-ln(2) * age_hours / half_life_hours)

    Different categories need different half-lives. Breaking politics may decay in hours, while an investigative feature may remain valuable for days.

    8.4 Diversity and constraints¶

    Pure score sorting often produces a poor feed. Apply post-ranking constraints:

    • Limit consecutive stories from one publisher.
    • Limit repeated topics and entities.
    • Mix local, national, and international coverage.
    • Guarantee a minimum number of categories.
    • Collapse near-duplicates into one cluster.
    • Avoid multiple stories using identical media.

    This is a constrained reranking stage, not necessarily part of the base ranking model.

    8.5 Versioned feed snapshots¶

    Build immutable, ordered feed versions:

    home:en-US:v1842
technology:en-US:v773
home:en-IN:v1261

    Each version contains ordered cluster IDs and compact card metadata. Keep several recent versions so clients can continue paginating the version on which they started.

    flowchart LR
    Updates[Article and Signal Updates] --> Candidates[Candidate Generator]
    Candidates --> Ranker[Ranker]
    Ranker --> Diversity[Diversity and Policy Rules]
    Diversity --> Snapshot[Immutable Feed Snapshot]
    Snapshot --> Store[(Feed Store)]
    Snapshot --> Redis[(Redis Segments)]
    Redis --> API[Feed API]

    Publishing is a small control-plane transaction around immutable data, not a rename of partially written keys. A builder writes versioned segments and a manifest containing feed key, version, segment count, item count, ranker/policy versions, per-segment checksums, and build watermark. It then performs these steps:

    1. Seal the build so no segment can be changed in place.
    2. Read every segment through the serving decoder and validate checksum, contiguous positions, unique cluster IDs, expected edition/category, required card fields, and current block/retraction policy. Reject suspicious count or score-distribution changes against the active version.
    3. Write the checksum-verified manifest to durable storage with state GLOBALLY_VALIDATED. Every region may activate only this exact candidate checksum.
    4. In each required region, warm at least the first two segments. A region acknowledges the manifest checksum, not merely that a cache key exists. A lagging region continues serving its prior version.
    5. After one region is warm, compare-and-swap that region's active[region,feed_key] from the regional base observed before warming to the new manifest version. There is no global active pointer in this mode. If a concurrent build changed that regional pointer, the CAS loses and the candidate is not exposed there.
    6. Emit RegionalFeedActivated(region,feed_key,new_version,old_version,manifest_checksum). Regional pointers and CDN keys are versioned, so delayed invalidation cannot mix segments from two versions. A globally validated candidate may be active in some regions while others safely retain an older snapshot.
    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant B as Feed builder
    participant D as Durable feed store
    participant V as Validator
    participant C as Regional cache
    participant P as Regional active pointer
    B->>D: Write immutable segments and manifest
    V->>D: Decode all segments and verify checksums
    V->>D: Mark manifest GLOBALLY_VALIDATED
    loop Each serving region
        V->>C: Warm leading segments by version
        C-->>V: Region acknowledgement with manifest checksum
        V->>P: CAS active region and feed key
        alt Regional CAS succeeds
            P-->>V: Candidate is active in region
        else Regional CAS loses
            P-->>V: Keep region's current version
        end
    end

    Rollback is another fenced regional pointer update: compare-and-swap from the known bad version to that region's last checksum-verified version, then emit a regional activation event. Never overwrite segments or blindly set an older pointer, because a concurrent good build may already have replaced the bad version. Retain each region's prior version and warmed leading segments for at least the rollback window. If global validation, local warming, or regional CAS fails, readers in that region stay on the old version; if a post-activation quality alarm fires, rollback changes new sessions while existing snapshot cursors remain valid until normal expiry.

    For example, a 2,000-card feed split into 100-card segments has 20 segments. At 1 KB per compact card it is about 2 MB per version. Across 200 feed keys, 12 retained versions, and three durable replicas, payload storage is roughly 2 MB * 200 * 12 * 3 = 14.4 GB, while warming only two segments per key per region limits each regional hot set to about 200 * 2 * 100 KB = 40 MB before cache overhead.

    8.6 Feed refresh cadence¶

    Use two complementary modes:

    • Periodic rebuild every few minutes for normal updates.
    • Event-driven partial refresh for major stories, retractions, and urgent policy changes.

    Avoid rebuilding all editions for every article. Track affected language, region, and category feed keys.

    9. Requirement 2: Infinite Scrolling¶

    9.1 Why offset pagination fails¶

    With OFFSET 10000 LIMIT 20, the database or search engine may inspect and discard thousands of records. More importantly, newly inserted stories shift positions, causing duplicates or missed items across pages.

    9.2 Snapshot-position cursor¶

    For precomputed feeds, use:

    cursor = signed(feed_key, feed_version, next_position, expiry)

    Page 1 reads positions 0-19, and the returned cursor points to position 20 in the same immutable version.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant C as Client
    participant A as Feed API
    participant R as Redis
    participant S as Feed Store

    C->>A: GET /feed without cursor
    A->>R: Read latest version and segment 0
    R-->>A: v1842, positions 0-19
    A-->>C: Items + cursor(v1842, 20)

    C->>A: GET /feed cursor(v1842, 20)
    A->>R: Read v1842 segment containing position 20
    alt Segment cached
        R-->>A: Positions 20-39
    else Cache miss
        A->>S: Read snapshot v1842 positions 20-39
        S-->>A: Feed entries
        A->>R: Cache segment
    end
    A-->>C: Items + cursor(v1842, 40)

    This provides stable pagination even while version v1843 becomes current for new sessions.

    Positions count scanned snapshot entries, not returned cards. Apply current eligibility to each candidate and backfill from later positions up to a scan budget; the next cursor points after the last scanned entry even when every candidate is retracted. Otherwise a page containing only removed stories can return the same cursor forever. The diagram's increments of 20 assume no filtering. Add a case where positions 20-39 are denied and 40-49 supply ten cards: the next position is 50, not 30 or 40. A snapshot pins order, never old permission to serve a withdrawn article.

    9.3 Cursor expiry and old snapshots¶

    Keep old snapshots for a bounded period, for example 30-60 minutes. If a client returns after expiry:

    • Return a cursor-expired response and restart from the latest feed.
    • Or map the position approximately into the new version while using session deduplication.

    The first behavior is simpler and usually acceptable for a news feed.

    9.4 Cursor alternatives¶

    Cursor strategy Stable while new stories arrive Server state Best use Main cost or failure
    Signed snapshot version plus position Yes None beyond retained snapshots Precomputed home/category feeds Cursor expires when its immutable version is deleted
    Keyset (rank_key,cluster_id) Only if ordering keys do not change None Chronological archive or immutable score order Reranking can still skip or repeat items
    Stateful session cursor plus shown-ID set Yes across feed refreshes One bounded record per session Personalized sessions that intentionally advance versions Redis cost, expiry behavior, and regional routing
    Offset No None Static, small administrative lists Deep scans and position shifts under insertion

    The chosen hot-feed cursor is snapshot plus position because ordering is precomputed and versions are already retained. The chronological archive switches to keyset pagination. A personalized feed may additionally carry a bounded shown-ID token, but that deduplication layer does not make offset pagination correct.

    9.5 Session-level deduplication¶

    Snapshots can still change if the product periodically advances users to a newer feed. Maintain a compact set or Bloom filter of recently shown story IDs:

    • Client can send a bounded list or opaque session token.
    • Server can keep a short-lived session set in Redis.
    • Filter duplicates and backfill from later positions.

    Do not store unbounded feed history per anonymous user.

    9.6 End of feed¶

    "Infinite" means the client can continue requesting older pages, not that data is literally infinite. Feed storage may contain:

    • A ranked hot section for the last 24-48 hours.
    • A chronological archive partitioned by edition/category and time.

    Once the ranked snapshot ends, continue through older eligible clusters using a keyset cursor such as (published_at, cluster_id). Pin an archive cutoff in the snapshot manifest and restrict the ranked section to stories at or after that cutoff and the archive to earlier stories. Without a disjoint boundary or an exact shown-ID set, ranked stories can repeat in the archive. Pin archive ordering timestamps too; corrections must not move a record across an active cursor.

    10. Requirement 3: Redirect to Publishers¶

    10.1 Direct link versus redirect service¶

    Direct links are simpler, faster, and avoid making the aggregator a dependency for navigation. A redirect service provides centralized analytics, abuse blocking, and URL changes.

    A pragmatic approach:

    • Include the canonical publisher URL in the feed for resilient clients.
    • Use an edge-hosted redirect URL when click analytics is required.
    • Fall back to the canonical URL if the redirect service is unavailable.

    10.2 Click path¶

    sequenceDiagram

    The redirect should not synchronously increment a database counter. During breaking news, one story can receive millions of clicks and create a hot row. Stream click events and aggregate them asynchronously.

    10.3 URL safety¶

    • Redirect only to URLs previously validated and stored for accepted publishers.
    • Never redirect to an arbitrary query parameter.
    • Block malicious, expired, retracted, or compromised destinations.
    • Use HTTPS where supported.
    • Keep a domain allowlist tied to publisher ownership.

    11. Deep Dive: Achieving Feed Latency Below 200 ms¶

    11.1 Latency budget¶

    An illustrative server-side budget:

    Stage Budget
    Edge and network 40 ms
    Gateway and authentication 10 ms
    Feed API execution 10 ms
    Redis/feed lookup 10 ms
    Metadata hydration 20 ms
    Serialization and compression 10 ms
    Safety margin 100 ms

    Cross-region database calls or real-time ranking can easily consume the entire budget, so feed data should already be materialized in the serving region.

    11.2 Cache hierarchy¶

    flowchart LR
    Client --> Edge[CDN / Edge Cache]
    Edge --> L1[L1 In-Process Cache]
    L1 --> L2[L2 Redis Cluster]
    L2 --> FeedStore[(Durable Feed Store)]
    • Edge cache public first pages by edition for a few seconds.
    • L1 caches the latest feed-version pointer and hottest segments.
    • Redis stores materialized feed segments.
    • Durable feed storage handles cache misses and snapshot retention.

    First-page cache keys might be:

    feed:{edition}:{category}:{version}:segment:{number}

    Store complete display cards when practical. Fetching 20 IDs and then issuing 20 article reads creates a fan-out latency and availability problem.

    11.3 Cache stampede protection¶

    When a feed segment expires:

    • Use request coalescing or a per-key lease so one request refreshes it.
    • Serve stale data while revalidating.
    • Add randomized TTL jitter.
    • Prewarm the first segments of new feed versions before publishing the version pointer.

    11.4 Compact responses¶

    • Return only card fields needed for the viewport.
    • Use Brotli or gzip compression.
    • Use optimized thumbnail dimensions and formats.
    • Avoid embedding large publisher or taxonomy objects repeatedly.
    • Cache immutable media URLs aggressively.

    12. Deep Dive: Meeting the 30-Minute Freshness Target¶

    Freshness is an end-to-end SLO, not just a crawler interval.

    publication
  -> source discovery
  -> fetch queue wait
  -> network fetch
  -> parse and normalize
  -> deduplicate and classify
  -> policy decision
  -> ranking and feed materialization
  -> regional cache visibility

    Define stage budgets:

    Stage Deadline allocation, not additive p99
    Discover or poll source 15 minutes
    Fetch queue and download 3 minutes
    Normalize and deduplicate 3 minutes
    Classify and filter 3 minutes
    Rank and publish feeds 4 minutes
    Replicate and expose 2 minutes

    These allocations sum to 30 minutes but do not prove a 30-minute p99: six component p99s can fail on different articles. Measure the complete publication-to-visible distribution, or apportion a joint tail-failure budget, including source discovery and backlog.

    Freshness mechanisms¶

    • Prefer push integrations for high-value publishers.
    • Poll active sources every few minutes.
    • Prioritize sources with historically frequent updates.
    • Autoscale consumers based on queue lag.
    • Partition streams by publisher to preserve local ordering.
    • Trigger partial feed refreshes instead of waiting for a global rebuild.
    • Monitor feed_visible_at - publisher_published_at, segmented by source.

    Publisher timestamps are not always trustworthy. Also track discovery and ingestion timestamps to distinguish publisher clock errors from system delay.

    Backpressure¶

    If ingestion falls behind:

    1. Preserve raw events durably.
    2. Prioritize trusted and time-sensitive sources.
    3. Defer expensive enrichment that is not required for eligibility.
    4. Publish a minimally processed article, then enrich it later if policy allows.
    5. Shed duplicate refreshes before unique new articles.

    13. Deep Dive: Media Content¶

    News feeds are image-heavy, while media bytes dwarf metadata.

    flowchart LR
    Article[Accepted Article] --> Extract[Media URL Extractor]
    Extract --> Fetch[Sandboxed Media Fetcher]
    Fetch --> Validate[Type, Size, Safety, Rights]
    Validate --> Original[(Object Storage)]
    Original --> Transform[Resize and Transcode]
    Transform --> Variants[(Image Variants)]
    Variants --> CDN[Global CDN]
    CDN --> Client[Clients]

    Media strategy¶

    • Respect publisher rights and agreements before proxying media.
    • Validate MIME type from bytes, not only headers.
    • Enforce download, pixel-count, duration, and file-size limits.
    • Strip unsafe metadata where appropriate.
    • Generate fixed responsive sizes such as 320, 640, and 1280 pixels.
    • Prefer WebP or AVIF with JPEG fallback.
    • Use immutable content-hashed URLs and long CDN TTLs.
    • Lazy-load media below the viewport.

    Videos should generally remain on the publisher platform. The aggregator can serve a thumbnail and metadata rather than ingest and transcode every full video.

    Failed media¶

    Feed cards must work without media. Use publisher logos or neutral placeholders and never delay feed publication waiting indefinitely for image processing.

    14. Deep Dive: Breaking-News Traffic Spikes¶

    Breaking news creates two simultaneous spikes:

    • Read spike: many users refresh the same feed and click the same story.
    • Write spike: many publishers release related updates and media.

    Read-side protection¶

    • Cache anonymous first pages at the CDN by edition.
    • Use stale-while-revalidate and stale-if-error.
    • Precompute shared feeds instead of ranking each request.
    • Keep feed services stateless and autoscalable.
    • Replicate feed caches regionally.
    • Use request coalescing for cache misses.
    • Degrade optional metadata before rejecting feed requests.
    • Rate-limit abusive refresh loops at the edge.

    Write-side protection¶

    • Buffer fetch and processing work in Kafka or durable queues.
    • Autoscale workers from lag and oldest-message age.
    • Prioritize new canonical stories over repeated cluster updates.
    • Coalesce multiple updates to the same cluster within a short window.
    • Batch feed rebuilds.
    • Isolate media processing from article eligibility.

    Hot-key handling¶

    Do not maintain one synchronous SQL counter for a viral story. Use sharded counters:

    click_count:{story_id}:{shard_id}

    Aggregate shards periodically for ranking. Approximate counters are acceptable for trend signals.

    Graceful degradation order¶

    1. Serve a slightly stale cached feed.
    2. Omit expensive personalization or alternative-source lists.
    3. Use precomputed thumbnails and summaries only.
    4. Reduce page size.
    5. Fall back to a static emergency feed by edition.

    15. Storage Choices¶

    Publisher and source metadata¶

    Use a relational database because source ownership, status, schedules, and uniqueness have clear transactional relationships.

    Canonical articles¶

    Use sharded SQL, a wide-column database, or a document store depending on scale and access patterns. Required operations are:

    • Idempotent upsert by canonical identity.
    • Lookup by article ID and canonical URL.
    • Recent articles by publisher and time.
    • Status and cluster updates.

    Partition historical articles by publication month and hash publisher or article ID within each period. Time partitioning makes retention and archival manageable.

    Story clusters¶

    Store canonical cluster state in a durable database. Use a vector index or approximate nearest-neighbor service only for candidate retrieval; it should not become the sole source of truth.

    Feed snapshots¶

    Options:

    • Redis sorted sets or lists for hot feed segments.
    • DynamoDB/Cassandra for durable regionally replicated feed entries.
    • Object storage for immutable large snapshots and archives.

    A useful combination is Redis for hot segments, a distributed key-value store for recent versions, and object storage for rebuild artifacts.

    Search¶

    Search is not a core requirement here. If users need keyword search, project article and cluster metadata into OpenSearch rather than forcing the feed store to support text queries.

    Data Consistency Rationale

    18. Reliability and Failure Modes¶

    Publisher is unavailable¶

    • Retry with exponential backoff and jitter.
    • Continue serving already ingested articles.
    • Alert only after source-specific thresholds to avoid noisy transient failures.
    • Do not let one domain occupy the fetch fleet.

    Parser or classifier is broken¶

    • Preserve raw payloads for replay.
    • Route poison records to a dead-letter queue.
    • Roll back the model or parser version.
    • Publish minimally processed content only when safety rules permit.

    Kafka is delayed¶

    • Existing feeds remain readable.
    • Freshness degrades while canonical input stays buffered.
    • Autoscale consumers and prioritize article eligibility stages.
    • Alert on oldest-event age, not just message count.

    Redis is unavailable¶

    • Read recent feed snapshots from the durable feed store.
    • Use bounded process-local caches.
    • Serve stale edge responses.
    • Rate-limit requests to protect the fallback store.

    Feed builder fails¶

    • Keep the previous successful version active.
    • Never publish a version pointer before all required segments exist.
    • Validate item count, policy status, duplicates, and schema before activation.

    Region is unavailable¶

    • Geo-route clients to another healthy region.
    • Keep several feed versions replicated across regions.
    • Accept increased latency rather than losing the feed entirely.

    20. Bonus: Category Feeds¶

    Category support is a natural extension of shared precomputed feeds.

    Classification¶

    Assign each story zero or more categories with confidence scores:

    Technology: 0.94
Business:   0.67
Sports:     0.01

    Use publisher sections, text classification, entities, and taxonomy rules. Require thresholds and allow editorial or publisher overrides.

    Serving¶

    Materialize feed keys such as:

    category:technology:en-US
category:sports:en-GB
category:business:en-IN

    The same versioned snapshot, cache, and cursor machinery works without a separate architecture.

    Category-specific ranking¶

    Signals and decay differ by category. Sports scores may emphasize live recency; long-form science reporting may use a longer half-life. Train or configure ranking by category rather than assuming one universal model.

    21. Bonus: Personalized Feeds¶

    User profile¶

    Derive a bounded profile from:

    • Explicitly selected topics and publishers.
    • Clicked, hidden, and followed stories.
    • Topic and entity affinity.
    • Language and region.
    • Recency-weighted reading history.

    Apply privacy controls, retention limits, consent, and account deletion.

    Hybrid personalization architecture¶

    flowchart LR
    Shared[Shared Edition and Category Candidates] --> Merge[Candidate Merger]
    Profile[(User Profile Store)] --> ReRank[Online Reranker]
    Trends[Regional Trends] --> Merge
    Following[Followed Topics and Publishers] --> Merge
    Merge --> ReRank
    ReRank --> Rules[Diversity, Safety, Deduplication]
    Rules --> User[Personalized Page]

    Do not rank the entire corpus during a request. Retrieve a few hundred candidates from shared pools, then rerank them using a low-latency user profile.

    Cold start¶

    • Anonymous users receive edition feeds.
    • New users choose optional interests.
    • Popular regional stories supply a baseline.
    • Exploration introduces unfamiliar categories without overwhelming the feed.

    Avoiding filter bubbles¶

    Personalization should include diversity constraints, source variety, and important public-interest stories. Product policy, not just click optimization, defines feed quality.

    22. Retractions, Corrections, and Deletion¶

    News changes after publication. Model article state explicitly:

    DISCOVERED -> ELIGIBLE -> PUBLISHED
                       -> RETRACTED
                       -> BLOCKED
                       -> EXPIRED

    Retraction flow:

    1. Detect a publisher removal, correction marker, or policy action.
    2. Update canonical article status.
    3. Emit a high-priority invalidation event.
    4. Remove or replace it in affected clusters.
    5. Build a new immutable feed version; old snapshots retain their bytes and are filtered by current retraction state.
    6. Purge Redis and CDN keys when necessary.
    7. Keep an audit trail and tombstone to prevent accidental reingestion.

    Safety invalidations should bypass ordinary batch refresh windows. Define a removal bound, such as 60 seconds after the authoritative policy commit. Every serving layer, including edge cache hits, redirects, and known media URLs, checks a versioned denylist whose maximum permitted age fits that bound; if it cannot refresh, it withholds affected content or fails closed. Purge accelerates removal but does not establish the bound by itself. Snapshot rollback and stale-if-error never roll back this policy gate. Already downloaded cards, cached client redirects, and direct publisher links cannot be recalled by the aggregator; use no-store for redirects if repeat clicks must recheck policy.

    23. Observability¶

    User-facing SLIs¶

    • Feed API availability and p50/p95/p99 latency.
    • Empty-feed and cursor-error rates.
    • First-page cache hit rate by region and edition.
    • Duplicate cards per session.
    • Redirect success and latency.
    • Media error and layout-shift rates.

    Freshness SLIs¶

    • Publication-to-discovery time.
    • Discovery-to-eligible time.
    • Eligible-to-feed-visible time.
    • End-to-end publication-to-feed-visible time.
    • Percentage meeting the 30-minute target by publisher and edition.

    Pipeline health¶

    • Scheduler delay and fetch success rate.
    • Queue depth and oldest-task age.
    • Kafka lag and dead-letter volume.
    • Parser/classifier errors by version.
    • Feed build duration and activation failures.
    • Regional feed-version lag.

    Quality metrics¶

    • Exact and near-duplicate rate.
    • Cluster merge/split correction rate.
    • Publisher and topic diversity.
    • Retraction-removal latency.
    • Click-through rate, dwell proxy, and rapid-return rate.

    Propagate trace context for ingestion and feed requests; sample full traces with elevated sampling for errors and freshness violations. Carry trace IDs through event headers so a stale article can be followed from fetch to feed publication.

    24. Testing Strategy¶

    Correctness and contract tests¶

    • Replay identical poll, push, and Kafka inputs and assert one canonical article and idempotent downstream effects.
    • Publish feed segments concurrently with readers and assert that a version pointer never exposes an incomplete snapshot.
    • Corrupt one segment, omit a position, duplicate a cluster, return a false warm acknowledgement, and race two builders from the same base; validation or CAS must keep the old pointer active.
    • Roll back while a newer healthy build activates and assert that fenced CAS cannot overwrite the healthy pointer; old-version cursors must continue reading only their original segments.
    • Insert new feed versions while paginating and assert no duplicate or omitted cards within the cursor's snapshot.
    • Race retraction events against delayed ingestion, clustering, ranking, and cache fills; the retracted article must remain unavailable and unredirectable.
    • Validate canonicalization, redirect allowlists, event schema compatibility, and cursor tamper/expiry behavior.

    Quality and pipeline tests¶

    • Maintain labeled corpora for exact deduplication, near-duplicate clustering, language, topic, geography, and policy classification; compare precision, recall, and cluster split/merge rates before rollout.
    • Run shadow feed builds and compare item eligibility, diversity constraints, freshness, and ranking movement against the active version.
    • Test raw-payload replay and full feed reconstruction from canonical state after parser, classifier, or schema changes.

    Load and failure tests¶

    • Reproduce a breaking-news step spike with one viral story, publisher skew, synchronized refreshes, cold caches, and media fan-out.
    • Inject slow publishers, poison records, Kafka lag, Redis loss, failed feed builders, and regional failover while checking the documented degradation order.
    • Verify the 30-minute freshness budget stage by stage and confirm that backpressure prioritizes unique, eligible articles over duplicate enrichment work.

    25. Security, Abuse, and Policy¶

    • Authenticate publisher configuration changes.
    • Prevent SSRF and DNS rebinding in fetchers.
    • Sandbox parsers and media processors.
    • Validate redirect domains.
    • Rate-limit feed requests and click endpoints.
    • Detect publisher spam, copied content, malware, and coordinated manipulation.
    • Separate editorial/policy overrides from ranking code and audit every action.
    • Encrypt data in transit and at rest.
    • Minimize and expire user behavior data used for personalization.
    • Protect ranking signals against artificial click inflation.

    Publisher quality is an input, not a permanent truth. Maintain explainable versioned signals and an appeal or review process.

    26. Important Tradeoffs¶

    Precomputed feeds versus rank on read¶

    Precomputation wins for anonymous, highly shared feeds because it converts expensive ranking into cheap indexed reads. Online ranking is reserved for lightweight personalization or rare filters.

    Freshness versus stability¶

    Always switching users to the latest order improves freshness but causes duplicate and missing cards during scrolling. Versioned snapshots keep a session stable while new sessions receive the freshest version.

    Polling versus push¶

    Push gives excellent freshness but cannot cover every publisher. Adaptive polling is universal but consumes bandwidth and can miss a strict freshness target for slow schedules. Support both.

    Storing full articles versus metadata¶

    The core product redirects to publishers, so store only licensed or operationally necessary excerpts and metadata. Preserving raw fetch payloads for a limited replay window is different from republishing complete content.

    Exact versus approximate ranking signals¶

    Trending counters and engagement velocity can be approximate. Article identity, publisher ownership, retraction state, and redirect targets require correctness.

    Availability versus consistency¶

    Serve the previous feed version during pipeline or cache failures. A stale but safe feed is preferable to an error page, but retractions and policy blocks require priority invalidation.

    End-to-end behavior¶

    1. The scheduler polls sources adaptively, while capable publishers push updates directly.
    2. Fetchers use conditional requests, preserve raw payloads, and publish durable ingestion events.
    3. Workers normalize URLs, deduplicate exact copies, cluster related articles, classify them, and enforce policy.
    4. Ranking jobs create immutable global, regional, and category feed snapshots.
    5. New snapshots are fully written and warmed before an atomic version-pointer switch.
    6. Feed APIs serve complete card segments from edge caches, local caches, or Redis, with durable snapshots as fallback.
    7. Snapshot-based cursors provide stable infinite scrolling while newer sessions receive fresher versions.
    8. Clicks redirect immediately to publishers and emit asynchronous analytics events.
    9. Media uses a separate validation, transformation, object-storage, and CDN path.
    10. Retractions use high-priority invalidation, while ordinary ranking changes remain eventually consistent.

    28. Interview Deep-Dive Questions¶

    Why not query the latest articles table for each feed request?¶

    At 100,000+ peak QPS, repeatedly filtering, joining, ranking, deduplicating, and diversifying the same anonymous feed wastes compute and increases tail latency. Precompute shared feed snapshots and serve compact segments from cache.

    How does pagination remain stable when new articles arrive?¶

    The cursor references an immutable feed version and the next position. New articles appear in a newer version but do not shift the user's active snapshot.

    How do articles become visible within 30 minutes?¶

    Use publisher push where available, adaptive polling elsewhere, durable queues, bounded stage budgets, lag-based autoscaling, and event-driven partial feed refreshes.

    How do you prevent every headline about the same event from filling the feed?¶

    Perform exact URL/content deduplication followed by semantic story clustering using embeddings, entity overlap, title similarity, language, and time proximity. Rank clusters rather than raw articles.

    What happens if Redis fails?¶

    Serve stale edge or process-cache entries and fall back to the durable feed store. Apply load shedding to protect that store. Redis contains rebuildable projections, not canonical data.

    How do you handle a breaking-news spike?¶

    Exploit shared CDN and Redis cache keys, serve stale while revalidating, coalesce misses, precompute feeds, buffer ingestion in Kafka, batch cluster updates, and degrade optional hydration or personalization first.

    How do you update or retract an article already cached globally?¶

    Change canonical status, emit a priority invalidation, update the cluster and affected feed versions, and purge cache/CDN keys. Preserve a tombstone to prevent reingestion.

    Should click counting happen synchronously during redirect?¶

    No. Return the redirect immediately and emit an asynchronous event. Aggregate sharded or streamed counters later so viral stories cannot create hot-row contention.

    Where does strong consistency matter?¶

    Source ownership, article identity, redirect safety, and retraction state need strong or idempotent correctness. Feed ordering, clustering, ranking, and engagement counters can be eventual.

    29. L5 and L6 Expectations¶

    L5 Expectations: Core¶

    • Separates ingestion from feed serving.
    • Defines article, publisher, and feed entities.
    • Uses queues for asynchronous processing.
    • Uses Redis/CDN for read scaling and cursor pagination.
    • Redirects users to publisher URLs.

    L5 Expectations: Strong Signals¶

    • Quantifies feed QPS and freshness requirements.
    • Designs adaptive polling, idempotent ingestion, deduplication, and story clustering.
    • Uses precomputed, versioned feed snapshots for low latency and stable pagination.
    • Handles media separately and explains failure degradation.
    • Addresses breaking-news spikes and cache stampedes.

    L6 Expectations¶

    • Frames freshness as a measurable end-to-end SLO with stage budgets.
    • Separates canonical state from rebuildable projections.
    • Designs safe feed activation, replay, regional replication, and retraction invalidation.
    • Balances ranking quality, diversity, publisher policy, cost, and latency.
    • Presents category and personalization as evolutionary extensions rather than required initial complexity.

    30. Five-Minute Interview Summary¶

    I would split Google News into an ingestion plane and a feed-serving plane. An adaptive scheduler polls RSS, Atom, APIs, and sitemaps, while publishers that support push can send updates directly. Fetchers use conditional requests and publish raw items to Kafka. Downstream workers normalize URLs, perform exact deduplication, cluster semantically related coverage into stories, classify topics and geography, apply policy filters, and write canonical article and cluster records.

    The read path must support over 100,000 peak feed requests per second below 200 ms, so I would not rank the corpus per request. Ranking jobs continuously build immutable feed snapshots by edition and category. They store complete display cards in versioned segments in regional Redis, with a durable feed store behind it and short-lived CDN caching for anonymous first pages. A cursor contains the feed version and next position, providing stable infinite scrolling even while newer versions are published.

    Clicking an article redirects immediately to the validated publisher URL; analytics are emitted asynchronously. Media is validated and transformed in a separate pipeline and served from object storage through a CDN. During breaking news, shared cache keys, stale-while-revalidate, request coalescing, queue buffering, and partial feed refreshes absorb spikes. Existing feed versions remain available during ingestion failures, while retractions use priority invalidation across feed stores and caches.

    31. Final Takeaways¶

    1. Separate ingestion from read serving so each scales and fails independently.
    2. Treat canonical articles and story clusters as durable truth; feeds and caches are projections.
    3. Rank story clusters rather than raw articles to prevent duplicate coverage from dominating.
    4. Precompute shared feeds to turn expensive ranking into fast indexed reads.
    5. Use immutable feed versions and snapshot cursors for consistent infinite scrolling.
    6. Cache complete card segments instead of creating a per-item hydration fan-out.
    7. Measure freshness from publisher publication through regional feed visibility.
    8. Combine publisher push with adaptive, polite polling.
    9. Move media bytes to object storage and a CDN through a safe transformation pipeline.
    10. Design breaking-news behavior explicitly: stale serving, coalescing, buffering, batching, and graceful degradation.
    11. Make ingestion and event consumers idempotent because retries are unavoidable.
    12. Prioritize availability for ordinary feed updates but fast invalidation for retractions and safety actions.

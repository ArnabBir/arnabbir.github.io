    Design a Global Load Balancer¶

    This case study owns the product and control-plane design. Use Proxies and Load Balancing for connection handling, backend selection, health, and draining mechanics.

    1. Interview roadmap¶

    Design L4/L7 load balancing for internet and internal services. Clarify protocols, health, affinity, locality, TLS, configuration rate, and failure policy. Start with stateless regional proxies and a strongly versioned control plane, then add global traffic steering, connection-aware algorithms, passive health, overload protection, draining, and safe rollout. The core tension is keeping the data plane fast and autonomous while routing state changes safely.

    2. Product framing¶

    Owners define virtual services, listeners, backend pools, health checks, routing, and security policy. Clients connect to a stable VIP/hostname and are sent to healthy capacity. The balancer proxies or forwards traffic; service discovery supplies candidates, and application state remains canonical elsewhere. Targets: 99.999% regional forwarding availability, under 2 ms p99 L4 and 10 ms L7 overhead, health removal within 10 seconds, and configuration rollout within 30 seconds.

    3. Requirements¶

    Support TCP/UDP, HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, gRPC, optional QUIC, TLS termination/passthrough, host/path/header routes, weights, affinity, retries, timeouts, canaries, health checks, connection draining, WAF/rate hooks, access logs, and global/regional failover. Preserve client identity where required. Configuration must be auditable and rollbackable. We exclude service implementation and authoritative discovery.

    4. Correctness invariants¶

    • A request is evaluated against one complete configuration generation.
    • Only eligible endpoints for the selected route, subset, and security policy receive new traffic.
    • Draining stops new assignment before terminating existing work.
    • Retry attempts share one deadline and bounded budget; non-idempotent requests are not retried without an idempotency contract.
    • Affinity is bounded and cannot override health or authorization.
    • A listener never presents another tenant's certificate or route.
    • Control-plane unavailability does not interrupt established forwarding.

    5. Capacity estimates¶

    Assume 50 million requests/s, 10 million concurrent connections, a 5 Tb/s provisioned egress budget, one million virtual services, and 20 million endpoints. At 10 KB average response, payload demand is 50M * 10 KB * 8 = 4 Tb/s; 5 Tb/s adds 25% for protocol overhead and reserve rather than following directly from the payload calculation. At small RPC sizes packet and TLS CPU may dominate instead. A proxy with 100,000 concurrent connections needs roughly gigabytes for socket state. Provision cells at no more than 50-60% steady CPU so a zone can fail without queue collapse in a three-zone balanced topology. A global backend fleet restart can generate millions of health and discovery changes.

    6. Entities and configuration model¶

    VirtualService(id, tenant, listeners[], routes[], generation)
Route(match, priority, backend_pool, retry, timeout, affinity)
BackendPool(id, discovery_ref, algorithm, locality, outlier_policy)
Endpoint(id, address, zone, weight, health_generation, drain_state)
Certificate(hostnames, key_ref, activation, expiry)
Bundle(cell, generation, checksum, minimum_binary_version)

    Canonical configuration is versioned; compiled bundles are read-only projections. Endpoint health and load are ephemeral, versioned observations, not customer configuration. APIs use conditional versions, idempotency keys, and opaque pagination cursors.

    7. Interfaces¶

    PUT  /v1/virtual-services/{id} If-Match: generation
POST /v1/backend-pools/{id}:drain {endpoint, deadline}
GET  /v1/rollouts/{generation}
Data plane: TCP/UDP listeners and HTTP/gRPC proxy

    Responses expose accepted, validated, canary, and globally deployed states. Backend requests carry trace/request IDs, forwarded identity under a trusted contract, and remaining deadline. Configuration validation catches overlapping routes, missing pools, redirect loops, and invalid certificates.

    8. High-level architecture¶

    flowchart LR
 C[Clients] --> G[DNS/anycast global steering]
 G --> E[Regional LB cells]
 E --> B[Service backends]
 A[Config API] --> S[(Versioned store)]
 S --> K[Compiler and rollout]
 K --> E
 D[Service discovery] --> E
 H[Health system] --> E
 E --> T[Async telemetry]

    The control plane compiles routes, certificates, and policy. Each data-plane cell has multiple zones and local endpoint/config snapshots. No request waits for discovery, rollout, logs, or global health.

    9. Connection and request flow¶

    At L4, a frontend hashes the connection tuple plus service into a forwarding table entry and sends packets to a proxy/backend using direct server return or full NAT. At L7, the proxy accepts TLS, validates SNI/Host, parses bounded headers, selects the highest-priority route, authenticates, chooses a pool and endpoint, enforces deadline, and streams with backpressure.

    autonumber

 participant L as Load balancer

 participant B as Backend

 L->>L: pin config generation, route
 L->>H: choose eligible endpoint
 L->>B: attempt 1 with deadline
 alt safe retryable failure and budget
  L->>B: attempt 2 elsewhere
 end
 B-->>L: response stream
 L-->>C: response with backpressure

    10. Endpoint selection algorithms¶

    Round robin is simple but ignores unequal work. Weighted round robin handles declared capacity, not live queueing. Least-connections works for long connections but a connection may carry many multiplexed streams. Weighted least-request uses (active_requests+1)/weight; power-of-two choices approximates it with less shared contention. EWMA latency captures performance but can punish endpoints receiving harder requests.

    Use power-of-two weighted least-request within the preferred healthy locality, with EWMA as an outlier signal. Consistent hashing is opt-in for caches or affinity; bounded-load rendezvous hashing spills from overloaded owners. Algorithms operate on locally observed state to avoid a centralized scheduler bottleneck.

    11. Health, ejection, and recovery¶

    Active probes establish readiness from multiple balancers. Passive observations detect failures visible to real traffic. One client's network problem must not globally eject a backend, so local outlier detection uses minimum volume, consecutive failure/rate thresholds, exponential ejection duration, and a cap on ejected capacity. Control health aggregates only corroborated evidence.

    Endpoint states are WARMING, HEALTHY, DRAINING, EJECTED, and REMOVED. Recovery ramps weight gradually to avoid sending a cold instance full load. Panic mode is explicit: if healthy capacity falls below a threshold, either use degraded endpoints or fail closed depending on service class.

    stateDiagram-v2
 [*] --> WARMING
 WARMING --> HEALTHY: readiness and ramp
 HEALTHY --> EJECTED: outlier threshold
 EJECTED --> WARMING: probation succeeds
 HEALTHY --> DRAINING: owner request
 DRAINING --> REMOVED: grace expires
 REMOVED --> [*]

    12. Deep dive: retries, hedging, and deadlines¶

    Retries improve transient failures but multiply load. The proxy derives each attempt timeout from the client's remaining deadline, retries only safe methods/statuses, excludes the failed endpoint, and consumes a retry token replenished by successful originals. A 10% retry budget bounds amplification. POST requires a declared idempotency key understood by the backend; transport failure after bytes were sent is ambiguous.

    Hedging sends a second attempt only after a percentile delay and only for cheap idempotent requests. Cancel losers, but assume cancellation may not prevent effects. Circuit breakers cap endpoint/pool concurrent requests and pending queues. Immediate bounded rejection is safer than waiting beyond useful deadlines.

    13. Deep dive: affinity and consistent hashing¶

    Cookie affinity is easy but signs pool and expiry to prevent tampering. Source-IP affinity performs poorly behind NAT and mobile networks. Consistent hashing minimizes movement on membership change but can overload the new owner and makes zone failover disrupt many sessions. Use virtual nodes or rendezvous hashing with bounded load and short-lived cookies. Affinity never selects unhealthy/draining nodes. Stateful applications still need shared state or resumable sessions.

    14. Deep dive: configuration rollout¶

    The compiler resolves references and emits an immutable bundle. Validators check semantics and resource limits. Roll out to test cells, then one canary per region, compare errors/latency/no-route metrics, and expand. Each worker verifies signature/hash and atomically swaps; existing connections may remain pinned while new requests use the new generation. Rollback publishes a higher generation based on old content, avoiding clocks and generation reversal, while retaining current certificate revocations and mandatory deny rules. Dangerous changes support approvals and dry-run traffic simulation.

    15. Partitioning, scaling, and overload¶

    Partition global traffic into regional cells via DNS or anycast. Inside a cell, flow hashing spreads connections across workers; consistent hashing minimizes disruption. Endpoint snapshots partition by service, while hot services receive dedicated workers/cells. Connection tables replicate only where failover requirements justify their cost; usually connection loss is retried by clients.

    Overload controls are hierarchical: listener connection limits, tenant token buckets, header/body caps, route concurrency, bounded pending queues, backend circuit breakers, and egress bandwidth. Preserve health/config traffic and established streams; reject new low-priority requests first. Load shedding returns protocol-specific overload signals and Retry-After. Autoscaling is slower than queue growth, so admission control is mandatory.

    16. Consistency and idempotency¶

    Configuration writes are linearizable per virtual service; edge rollout and discovery are bounded eventual. Atomic generation swap prevents mixed routing state. Health is intentionally local and eventual. API retries deduplicate by operation ID. Request retry does not imply exactly-once effects; application idempotency does. Access logs are best effort unless a durable acknowledgement path is explicitly designed. A durable exporter may redeliver, so downstream consumers deduplicate stable event IDs; billing cannot infer complete usage from a lossy asynchronous log.

    17. Reliability, failover, and multi-region¶

    A worker crash loses its connections unless flow state is replicated; frontends remap new flows. Zone loss is absorbed by spare capacity in surviving zones. Region loss shifts traffic only after destination capacity checks; otherwise global failover converts one outage into two. DNS has cache delay, while anycast can reroute abruptly and requires route dampening.

    Control loss freezes last-known-good bundles. Discovery loss retains endpoints to a stale deadline and combines passive health. Bad rollout auto-halts/rolls back. TLS keys remain locally available with expiry alarms. Regional control ownership uses fenced epochs; data cells remain autonomous.

    18. Security and abuse prevention¶

    Use mTLS to backends, HSM-backed TLS keys, hostname ownership, scoped APIs, signed bundles, secret-free logs, network segmentation, and trusted identity-header stripping/reinjection. Defend against DDoS, Slowloris, malformed HTTP, request smuggling, TLS exhaustion, route injection, SSRF routes, header spoofing, cross-tenant certificates, and health endpoint abuse. Limit parsing and decompression before allocation.

    19. Observability and testing¶

    Measure accepted/rejected connections, requests, bytes, handshake and proxy latency, route/pool/status, active/pending load, retries/hedges, deadline exhaustion, ejections, panic mode, endpoint skew, config/endpoint age, rollout coverage, TLS expiry, and regional capacity. High-cardinality IDs are sampled, not metric labels.

    Property-test route precedence and hashing movement. Fuzz protocol parsers. Replay shadow traffic against bundles. Load-test long connections, HTTP/2 multiplexing, hot routes, retry storms, slow clients, and zone evacuation. Chaos-test stale discovery, half-open backends, config corruption, certificate distributor loss, and regional capacity shortage. Synthetic probes traverse every listener and failover path.

    20. Important tradeoffs¶

    Proxying enables rich policy but costs CPU and adds a hop; direct return wins high-throughput L4. Central load knowledge is current but unscalable; local choices win. Aggressive ejection reacts quickly but can amplify correlated failure. Stateful failover preserves connections at major complexity; most services accept reconnect. Anycast is fast but less controllable than DNS.

    flowchart TD
 P[Versioned policy] --> C[Validated signed bundle]
 C --> R[Autonomous regional cells]
 D[Local discovery snapshots] --> R
 H[Active plus passive health] --> R
 R --> A[Bounded-load endpoint choice]
 A --> B[Deadline and retry budget]
 B --> S[Service backends]

    21. Final architecture¶

    Global steering selects a capacitated region; autonomous cells atomically consume bundles and endpoint snapshots; workers use local bounded-load selection, layered admission, and conservative retries.

    22. Interview deep-dive questions¶

    Why power-of-two? How does draining race discovery? When is panic mode safe? Can a POST retry? How does HTTP/2 alter least-connections? How do you evacuate a region without overload?

    23. L5 and L6 expectations¶

    An L5 candidate covers routing, algorithms, health, TLS, retries, draining, scaling, and failure. An L6 candidate separates planes, quantifies spare capacity and amplification, defines safety modes, handles migrations and bad rollout, and ties isolation to cost and organization.

    24. Five-minute summary¶

    Five-minute summary: version and compile policy away from traffic; route clients to a healthy region; atomically load local config and endpoint snapshots; select endpoints with weighted power-of-two choices; combine hysteretic active health with local outlier detection. Bound connections, queues, retries, and affinity. Drain before removal, ramp recovery, preserve last-known-good state through control outages, and fail over only into proven spare capacity.

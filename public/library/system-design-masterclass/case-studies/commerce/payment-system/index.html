    Design a Payment System¶

    Interview Roadmap¶

    Clarify rails, money movement, and whether the platform holds balances. This design accepts merchant payment intents, routes card or bank payments, records an immutable double-entry ledger, sends webhooks, and supports refunds. State money invariants before availability goals. Draw a single-region synchronous write path, trace an ambiguous provider timeout, then scale through account-owned partitions, reconciliation, and region fencing.

    Product Framing¶

    Merchants create a payment intent, collect a tokenized instrument, authorize it, optionally capture it later, and may cancel before capture or refund after capture. Providers respond synchronously, asynchronously, or ambiguously. The platform must expose a stable business state despite those differences and retain an auditable financial history.

    Assume multiple currencies but no conversion within a payment. Settlement to merchant banks, disputes, and risk decisions are represented as downstream workflows; building card networks or a general bank is out of scope.

    Requirements¶

    • Create, confirm, query, cancel, and refund payment intents.
    • Support automatic capture and separate authorization/capture, including partial capture and authorization expiry.
    • Route to providers, process signed callbacks, and retry safe operations.
    • Maintain merchant payable balances and immutable accounting entries.
    • Deliver ordered-enough, retryable webhooks and reconciliation reports.
    • Never lose or invent money; target 99.99% query and 99.95% confirmation availability.
    • Return confirmation decisions in under two seconds at p99 when the rail responds.

    Correctness Invariants¶

    1. Every posted ledger transaction balances: sum of debits equals sum of credits per currency.
    2. A merchant idempotency key plus operation creates one logical effect.
    3. A provider operation ID is stable across retries, and one provider event is applied once logically.
    4. Payment state can advance only through permitted transitions; ledger entries are appended, never edited.
    5. Captures cannot exceed authorized amount, and successful refunds plus pending refund reservations cannot exceed captured amount.
    6. A client or webhook never proves money moved; verified provider state and ledger posting do.
    7. Cancel, capture, refund, callback, and timeout resolution serialize against the same intent version so mutually exclusive effects cannot both win.

    Capacity Estimates¶

    Design for at least 10,000 peak payment operations/s. At 50 million payments/day the average is 580/s; a 17x event peak is about 10K/s. Status polling and dashboards can generate 50,000 peak read QPS. At 2 KB per intent, attempts, indexes, and metadata, transactional records add about 100 GB/day. Four ledger lines per payment at 250 bytes add 50 GB/day. Keep seven years in immutable archive while hot operational tables retain recent months.

    One customer operation is not one backend operation. At 10K confirms/s, assume one intent write, one attempt write, one provider call, four ledger-entry writes, two outbox events, and three projection or webhook jobs: roughly 10K * 12 = 120K internal operations/s before retries. If 5% of provider calls time out and each causes two status queries, that adds 10K * 0.05 * 2 = 1K provider requests/s. A one-hour webhook outage at two events per payment accumulates 10K * 2 * 3,600 = 72M deliveries; draining it in two hours while live traffic continues requires an extra 10K deliveries/s of safe capacity. Size databases, provider quotas, stream partitions, and delivery storage from these amplified rates, not only API TPS.

    Provider and merchant skew matter. A large merchant can create 20% of traffic, and a provider incident can cause retry amplification. Per-tenant and per-provider queues, quotas, and circuit breakers are therefore part of correctness under overload.

    APIs¶

    POST /v1/payment-intents

    capture_method=automatic asks confirm to authorize and capture as one logical operation. manual makes successful confirmation produce REQUIRES_CAPTURE with an authorized amount and expiry. Capture may be partial only if the rail and merchant contract permit it; each request atomically reserves capture capacity. A successful non-final partial capture produces PARTIALLY_CAPTURED, while final_capture=true or exhaustion of the authorization produces SUCCEEDED and releases any unused remainder. A capture after expiry or above the remaining authorization returns 409 without calling the provider.

    Cancel succeeds only while amount_captured=0. It cancels an unconfirmed intent locally or voids an untouched authorization in REQUIRES_CAPTURE through a stable provider operation. A cancel racing with the first capture uses optimistic intent versioning: one reserves the transition and the loser receives the resulting canonical state. An ambiguous void remains CANCEL_PROCESSING, not CANCELED, until callback, query, or reconciliation proves the result. Once any partial or final capture succeeds, cancel returns 409; the merchant must refund captured funds, while an unused authorization remainder is released only by a final capture, expiry, or an explicit provider operation modeled separately from intent cancellation.

    202 means processing or customer action required and includes a client secret or polling URL. 201 or 200 returns the canonical resource; 409 represents an invalid state transition or idempotency payload conflict, not an unknown provider result. A retry returns the original response. Cursor pagination uses immutable creation ID and time. Merchant webhook consumers receive event IDs and must deduplicate.

    The baseline has one write component and one relational transaction boundary: payment state, balanced ledger rows, and the outbox event are colocated in Shared payment, ledger, and outbox SQL. The relay runs only after commit. There is no synchronous "record payment, then call ledger" gap in this baseline. Separate the ledger service and partitions only after throughput or organizational boundaries justify it; that later design commits verified capture plus a durable posting command and state CAPTURED_PENDING_LEDGER in payment SQL, then advances to SUCCEEDED only after the ledger acknowledges its idempotent posting reference.

    Requirement Flows¶

    Confirm Payment¶

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant M as Merchant
    participant P as Payments
    participant D as Shared payment, ledger, outbox SQL
    participant R as Provider
    M->>P: Confirm(intent, key)
    P->>D: Persist attempt and operation ID
    P->>D: COMMIT attempt before external call
    P->>R: Authorize or authorize-and-capture(operation ID)
    alt success
        R-->>P: provider reference
        P->>D: BEGIN and lock intent version
        alt Capture verified
            P->>D: Update payment plus insert balanced ledger rows plus outbox
        else Authorization only
            P->>D: Update REQUIRES_CAPTURE plus outbox
        end
        P->>D: COMMIT atomically
        P-->>M: SUCCEEDED or REQUIRES_CAPTURE
    else challenge
        P->>D: ACTION_REQUIRED
        P-->>M: 202 challenge
    else timeout
        P->>D: UNKNOWN
        P-->>M: 202 processing
    end

    The attempt and stable operation ID commit before the provider call. Risk evaluates a versioned decision input. Routing is sticky for the operation; retrying through another acquirer could double-authorize. A timeout transitions to UNKNOWN, and a resolver queries by operation ID or waits for a callback. Once capture success is verified, the baseline's second database transaction atomically changes payment state, inserts the balanced ledger transaction, and inserts its outbox event; no observer can see SUCCEEDED without the corresponding posting.

    Refund¶

    Within a transaction, reserve refundable capacity so concurrent refunds cannot exceed capture. Persist a provider refund operation, invoke it idempotently, and post reverse ledger entries only when provider success is verified. A definitive provider failure with no possible late success releases the reservation; ambiguity remains pending. Refund completion and merchant notification may be asynchronous.

    Authorization, Capture, and Cancel¶

    Authorization proves the provider has reserved up to an amount until an explicit expiry; it is not merchant revenue and does not credit merchant payable. Capture consumes all or part of that reservation and posts the financial ledger transaction only after verified provider success. Cancel before authorization is a local terminal transition; cancel after authorization requests a provider void. Expired unused authorizations transition through provider evidence or a scheduled resolver, not merely the application clock.

    Webhooks¶

    An outbox event creates one delivery per subscribed merchant endpoint. Workers sign timestamp, event ID, and body; retry with exponential backoff and jitter for days. Ordering is guaranteed only per payment intent when required, not globally. A poison endpoint cannot block others because queues and concurrency are tenant-scoped. Merchants can list events and replay deliveries.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant O as Outbox relay
    participant Q as Endpoint queue
    participant W as Webhook worker
    participant M as Merchant endpoint
    O->>Q: Persist delivery(event, endpoint)
    Q->>W: Lease due delivery
    W->>M: POST body with event ID, timestamp, signature, key version
    alt 2xx response
        M-->>W: Accepted
        W->>Q: Mark delivered
    else Timeout, 429, or 5xx
        W->>Q: Schedule exponential backoff with jitter
    else Permanent 4xx or retry horizon exceeded
        W->>Q: Disable or mark exhausted and alert merchant
    end
    Note over Q,M: Merchant can fetch event and request replay

    Reuse the same immutable event ID and body across automatic attempts, but give each HTTP attempt a delivery-attempt ID. This lets merchants deduplicate business events while both sides diagnose individual retries. A merchant-requested replay creates a new delivery referencing the original event and is rate-limited and audited.

    Hosted Tokenization and Trust Boundary¶

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant B as Customer browser
    participant H as Hosted payment fields
    participant V as Isolated token vault
    participant M as Merchant backend
    participant P as Payment API
    B->>H: Enter PAN and CVV in provider-owned origin
    H->>V: TLS submit card data
    V->>V: Encrypt PAN and create scoped token
    V-->>B: Single-use payment method token
    B->>M: Return opaque token only
    M->>P: Confirm intent with token and client secret
    P->>V: Resolve token inside PCI boundary
    V-->>P: Provider-specific credential reference

    The merchant page, analytics scripts, logs, and general payment services never receive PAN or CVV. Browser isolation requires a distinct hosted origin, restrictive CSP and frame-ancestors, integrity-controlled scripts, and no postMessage payload containing card data. Tokens are scoped to merchant, environment, purpose, and lifetime; possession by another merchant is useless. The vault-to-provider adapter boundary is tightly authenticated, audited, and segmented from public API fleets.

    Deep Dive 1: Double-Entry Ledger¶

    For a $10 capture with a $0.30 fee, debit a provider receivable account $10, credit merchant payable $9.70, and credit fee revenue $0.30. Settlement later debits merchant payable and credits bank cash. Refunds create new reversing entries rather than changing capture history. Each posting has a unique business reference, balanced entries, currency, effective time, and append sequence.

    Do not use a cached balance to decide whether money exists unless it is transactionally maintained with entries. Periodically recompute balance from checkpoints plus entries and compare with the serving projection. Corrections use explicit adjustment transactions under audited permissions.

    Refund is also an independent resource: a partially captured intent may be refunded without erasing its remaining capture/authorization state. The refund arrows above summarize the fully captured case; retain captured, reserved-refund, refunded, and authorization amounts separately instead of forcing every combination into one status. A definitive failed capture or void returns to the applicable prior payment state; UNKNOWN remains tied to its specific operation until resolved.

    Deep Dive 2: Idempotency and Ambiguity¶

    Store (merchant, operation, key, request_hash, resource_id, response) before side effects. Same key and hash returns the stored result; a different hash is rejected. Provider callbacks deduplicate by verified provider event ID and additionally guard state transitions, because some providers produce distinct events for the same fact.

    Separate the platform's financial-record retention from the provider's idempotency window. Persist the provider key, request hash, first-submission time, documented retry deadline, and provider resource reference. A stable string is not permanent deduplication: Stripe's idempotency contract allows pruning after at least 24 hours and treats reuse after pruning as a new request (accessed 2026-09-08). Beyond the adapter's verified horizon, resolve the existing operation by resource reference or reconciliation rather than blindly resubmitting, even with the same key. A negative eventually consistent lookup alone does not prove the first request had no effect.

    Exactly-once delivery is not assumed. Within the proven provider idempotency and reconciliation contract, durable intent, stable provider IDs, idempotent posting references, inbox/outbox records, and reconciliation combine to provide once-only business effects. If neither callback nor query can resolve an old unknown, quarantine it for operations instead of guessing failure.

    Deep Dive 3: Routing and Retries¶

    Routing considers instrument support, geography, cost, success rate, latency, merchant policy, and provider health. Use slowly changing health signals; routing every request away from a brief error can overload a healthy provider. Once an operation reaches a provider, keep retries there unless the rail proves no financial effect occurred. Hedging payment writes is unsafe. Retry only documented transient failures with a retry budget; decline codes are terminal.

    Deep Dive 4: Reconciliation¶

    Provider settlement files and APIs are independent evidence. Ingest raw files immutably, validate totals and signatures, normalize records, and match by provider reference, operation ID, amount, currency, and time. Classify missing-local, missing-provider, amount mismatch, and duplicate-provider cases. Automatic repair is limited to deterministic transitions; money adjustments require policy and audit. Reconciliation lag is a financial SLI, not a back-office afterthought.

    Partitioning and Scaling¶

    Partition payment intents by merchant plus hash of intent to spread large tenants; maintain a narrow global lookup from intent ID to shard. Ledger partitions must keep accounts touched by one transaction together. A platform can use clearing accounts between partitions: each local transaction balances against a clearing account, and paired transfers reconcile globally. This complexity is justified only beyond a single database's write capacity.

    Archive terminal attempts and webhook payloads by month. Read replicas serve dashboards, never confirmation decisions. Per-provider queues impose concurrency and rate limits. Backpressure rejects low-priority retries before new customer confirmations, but never drops persisted workflow work.

    Consistency and Reliability¶

    Payment state and ledger posting require strong consistency within their transaction boundary. Dashboards, webhooks, reports, and settlement views may lag with visible as_of times. Outbox and inbox patterns bridge asynchronous boundaries. Optimistic versions serialize callback, poller, refund, and cancel races.

    If the database is unavailable, reject new financial writes. If a provider is slow, open a circuit and return processing only after intent is durable. If the stream fails, outbox backlog grows while money state remains intact. If ledger posting is separated and unavailable, cap pending captures and prevent settlement until repaired. Every service restart resumes leased work from persisted state.

    Multi-Region Strategy¶

    Assign each merchant or payment intent a home region with synchronous multi-zone storage. Keep financial writes in that region and replicate asynchronously for disaster recovery. Failover requires fencing the old writer through a monotonically increasing region epoch enforced by claim and posting authorities. Before new financial work, prove that the recovered prefix contains all relevant acknowledged intents, provider-operation identities, reservations, and postings. A nonzero asynchronous RPO can violate that requirement; hold affected work until authoritative recovery/reconciliation rather than recreating operations under fresh keys. Recovery may temporarily stop confirms rather than risk duplicate money movement. Globally active reads use replicas; local collection services may forward tokenized details to the home region. Data residency can create separate legal ledgers connected through explicit intercompany clearing, not hidden cross-region writes.

    Security and Abuse Prevention¶

    Minimize PCI scope with tokenization and hosted collection. Encrypt secrets using managed keys, rotate webhook signing keys, and isolate provider credentials. Authenticate merchants with scoped credentials and optional mTLS; require strong controls and dual approval for refunds, routing changes, and ledger adjustments. Redact PAN, CVV, tokens, and personal data from logs. Risk controls detect card testing, refund abuse, replayed callbacks, account takeover, and velocity anomalies. Maintain immutable access and financial audit trails with retention policy.

    Webhook endpoints are untrusted outbound destinations. Require HTTPS, reject URL credentials and fragments, resolve DNS through a controlled resolver, block loopback, link-local, private, metadata, and internal service ranges for every resolved address, and connect through an egress proxy that rechecks the destination after redirects. Pin the chosen public IP for the request while validating TLS hostname against the registered host; cap redirects, body bytes, response time, and concurrency. These controls limit SSRF, DNS rebinding, and endpoints that intentionally hold sockets open.

    Use versioned webhook signing keys with an overlap window: sign new deliveries with the new key, publish both public verification keys or expose both secret versions to an authenticated merchant console, and accept operator rollback during a bounded period. Provider and merchant API credentials follow create, activate, drain, revoke, and delete stages; never replace a key in place. Record key ID, use, principal, and rotation lineage without logging secrets.

    Callbacks and merchant requests carry a timestamp or nonce inside the authenticated payload. Reject timestamps outside a narrow window, verify the signature over the raw body before parsing, bind provider callbacks to endpoint and merchant context, and atomically insert (provider, event_id) before applying state. For APIs, idempotency prevents duplicate effects but does not replace authentication or nonce/rate controls. For webhooks, the receiver should verify signature and timestamp, store event_id, then acknowledge; a captured request replay cannot create a second business effect.

    Observability and Testing¶

    Monitor approval rate by normalized reason, unknown-state age, provider latency and circuit state, idempotency conflicts, ledger posting lag, unbalanced-posting rejection, webhook age, reconciliation breaks and value, and per-tenant queue depth. Alert on value at risk, not only counts. Trace by intent, attempt, operation, ledger transaction, and provider reference without sensitive payloads.

    Test ledger conservation with property-based tests and replay all state transitions. Contract-test provider adapters using signed fixtures. Inject timeouts before and after provider acceptance, duplicate and reordered callbacks, database failover, stale region epochs, and corrupt settlement files. Run daily recomputation of balances and restore drills. A release that changes money logic shadows calculations and compares postings before activation.

    Important Tradeoffs¶

    A synchronous monolithic transaction is simplest and safest but eventually limits independent scaling. Splitting ledger from payment improves isolation while introducing a visible pending state. Home-region writes add latency but avoid multi-primary financial conflicts. Provider routing improves success and cost but complicates ambiguity. Immutable entries consume storage but make audit and correction tractable. Availability must sometimes yield to financial integrity.

    Product-interface tradeoffs¶

    Choice Simpler interface More capable interface Decision pressure
    Capture Confirm always captures Explicit manual capture and partial capture Hotels, marketplaces, and fulfillment need delayed capture, but merchants must handle expiry and races
    Cancel Generic cancel at any time Cancel before capture, refund after capture One button is friendly, but distinct resources and statuses make money semantics auditable
    Async state Hide pending behind a long request Return 202, status API, and webhooks Explicit processing handles rail latency and mobile retries without pretending a timeout is failure
    Card collection Merchant posts card data Hosted fields return scoped tokens Hosted collection reduces PCI and breach scope but constrains checkout customization
    Webhooks Fire-and-forget callbacks Signed events, retries, event retrieval, replay A richer contract costs storage and tooling but is operable under endpoint failures
    Errors Provider codes exposed directly Stable platform codes plus provider detail Stable codes preserve integrations when routing changes; detail still supports diagnosis

    Start with automatic capture if the product serves immediate digital fulfillment. Add manual and partial capture only with explicit authorization-expiry UI, capture idempotency, and webhook states. Do not overload cancel to manufacture a refund after capture: preserving the distinction lets merchants, support teams, and ledgers explain whether funds were merely reserved or actually moved.

    Final Architecture¶

    flowchart TD
    Edge[Regional edge and token vault] --> Home[Merchant home cell]
    Home --> Pay[Payment state machine]
    Pay --> PDB[(Intent, attempt, and posting-command shards)]
    Pay --> Route[Sticky provider routing]
    Route --> Rails[External rails]
    PDB --> Ledger[Balanced ledger poster]
    Ledger --> LDB[(Account-owned ledger partitions)]
    Ledger --> Ack[Durable posting acknowledgement]
    Ack --> Pay
    PDB --> Events[(Payment outbox and stream)]
    Events --> Hooks[Isolated webhook queues]
    Events --> Reports[Settlement projections]
    Files[Provider files and queries] --> Recon[Reconciliation]
    Recon --> Pay
    Recon --> Ledger
    PDB -. fenced DR .-> DR[(Recovery region)]

    This final diagram is the later separated-ledger evolution, not the baseline above. A verified capture first commits CAPTURED_PENDING_LEDGER and an idempotent posting command in PDB; the balanced ledger poster consumes that command, commits to LDB, and emits an acknowledgement that lets the payment state machine advance to SUCCEEDED. Settlement excludes pending-ledger captures, and reconciliation repairs commands or acknowledgements without reposting the unique business reference.

    L5 and L6 Expectations¶

    An L5 answer defines idempotency, models payment states, handles provider timeout, uses balanced immutable entries, and separates canonical writes from asynchronous webhooks. An L6 answer additionally reasons about account partitioning, reconciliation value at risk, regional fencing, rail policy, operational repair, migrations, compliance boundaries, and why availability sometimes loses to integrity.

    Five-Minute Summary¶

    The system exposes payment intents while hiding provider-specific workflows. Persist an intent and stable provider operation before any external call. A timeout becomes unknown and is resolved by callback, provider query, and reconciliation; it never triggers an unrelated charge. Successful captures post immutable, balanced double-entry transactions, while refunds reserve refundable capacity and add reversing entries. Idempotency records, conditional state transitions, inbox/outbox delivery, and unique posting references create once-only business effects over at-least-once transport. Start with payment and ledger in one multi-AZ SQL transaction; split by account only when throughput demands it. Assign each intent one fenced home region. Isolate providers and merchants with bounded queues, reconcile against independent provider evidence, tokenize card data, and monitor unknown money and reconciliation value rather than HTTP success alone.

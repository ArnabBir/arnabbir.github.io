    Flash Sale System¶

    This chapter develops an end-to-end design for selling a tiny amount of inventory under extreme, synchronized demand. It is structured as both a detailed study guide and a 45-60 minute system design interview walkthrough.

    Imagine that 10,000 pairs of a limited-edition shoe go on sale at exactly noon while tens of millions of users attempt to buy them. The system must remain available, allocate inventory fairly, let winners complete payment, and never sell more units than physically exist.

    The hard part is not the product page. It is coordinating scarce inventory across concurrent requests and an unreliable, multi-second payment workflow without holding a database transaction open.

    1. Interview Roadmap¶

    Phase Time Goal
    Requirements 5 minutes Define sale rules, correctness, scale, and fairness
    Entities and APIs 5 minutes Establish reservation and order boundaries
    High-level design 15 minutes Build view, reserve, and purchase paths
    Deep dives 15-20 minutes Contention, expiry, burst handling, and fairness
    Wrap-up 5 minutes Payment races, failures, tradeoffs, and final design

    A strong interview answer should surface the reservation requirement without prompting:

    View item -> Reserve one unit -> Pay before reservation expires -> Confirm purchase

    Payment takes seconds and involves an external provider. Holding an inventory row lock or database transaction during payment would destroy throughput and reliability. A reservation removes inventory from circulation immediately and gives payment a bounded time window.

    2. Understanding the Problem¶

    A flash sale differs from ordinary e-commerce in several ways:

    • Demand arrives at a known instant instead of gradually.
    • Demand may exceed supply by thousands of times.
    • One SKU or a handful of variants become extreme hot keys.
    • Users care about fairness and visible progress, not only raw latency.
    • Bots and duplicate attempts can consume scarce capacity.
    • Payment is slower and less reliable than inventory allocation.
    • Overselling is a correctness failure, not a tolerable stale-read issue.

    The system should absorb excess demand before it reaches inventory or payment systems.

    3. Requirements¶

    3.1 Functional Requirements¶

    Core requirements¶

    1. Users can view a flash-sale item and sale status.
    2. During an active sale, an eligible user can reserve one available unit for a limited time.
    3. A user with an active reservation can complete payment and purchase the unit.

    Clarifications¶

    • The example sale contains 10,000 units across size or color variants.
    • A reservation lasts five minutes by default.
    • A user can hold at most one reservation for the sale.
    • A unit cannot belong to two active reservations or completed orders.
    • An expired or cancelled reservation returns its unit to inventory.
    • A sold-out response may be eventual for display, but allocation must be exact.
    • Clients may retry every mutation; APIs must be idempotent.
    • Users arriving before the start join a randomized waiting room; arrivals after the start join behind them.

    Out of scope¶

    • General catalog browsing and search.
    • Shipping, fulfillment, returns, and order history.
    • Sale creation and merchant administration.
    • Product recommendations.
    • Complex tax and fraud implementation details.
    • Multi-item carts spanning ordinary and flash-sale products.

    3.2 Non-Functional Requirements¶

    Requirement Target Reasoning
    Inventory correctness Never oversell Physical inventory cannot be manufactured by retrying
    Reservation durability No acknowledged reservation loss Winners must not lose valid claims silently
    Product-page availability 99.99%+ Tens of millions may load it simultaneously
    Reservation availability Graceful admission, not overload collapse Excess demand should wait or receive a definitive response
    Product-page latency p95 below 200 ms Static and highly cacheable
    Reservation decision Normally below 1 second after admission Queueing happens before inventory service
    Fairness Explainable and bot-resistant Trust matters for scarce allocations
    Payment workflow Idempotent and recoverable PSP callbacks and client retries are unavoidable
    Auditability Reconstruct every unit transition Required for disputes and incident recovery

    3.3 Correctness Invariants¶

    State these explicitly. They drive the design.

    1. available + reserved + sold = configured inventory for each inventory pool.
    2. A physical or logical inventory unit is in exactly one state.
    3. A user has at most one live reservation per sale.
    4. A reservation can create at most one successful order.
    5. A payment attempt can affect an order at most once.
    6. Releasing a reservation is idempotent.
    7. A sold unit never returns to available inventory without an explicit refund/cancellation workflow.

    3.4 Capacity Estimates¶

    Assume:

    • 50M users open the event page around noon.
    • 10M users attempt to reserve within the first 10 seconds.
    • 10,000 total units.
    • 5-minute reservation TTL.
    • 80% of reservations attempt payment.
    • 70% of payment attempts succeed.
    • Product page response is 20 KB before media.
    • A sale has 10 product variants.

    Uncontrolled burst:

    Reservation attempts = 10M / 10 seconds = 1M requests/second
Inventory units       = 10,000
Demand-to-supply      = 1,000:1

    There is no reason to send 1M reservation requests per second into the authoritative database. At least 99.9% cannot win.

    If the waiting room admits 5,000 users per second:

    Maximum inventory pressure = 5,000 reserve requests/second
First allocation round      = at least 2 seconds if 5,000 claims/s all succeed
Expected first-round sales   = 10,000 * 0.80 * 0.70 = 5,600
Remaining first-round units  = 4,400 to resolve or recycle

    The 2-second floor describes allocation, not paid completion. Five-minute holds, unknown payments, variant skew, and later rounds determine sell-through time.

    Page traffic at 50M views and 20 KB is roughly 1 TB before images. A CDN must serve nearly all of it.

    The architecture should scale with request attempts while the authoritative allocation path scales roughly with inventory plus a bounded amount of contention.

    Counted versus unit-level inventory¶

    Two valid models exist:

    • Counted inventory: atomically decrement available_quantity when it is greater than zero.
    • Unit tokens: pre-create exactly 10,000 logical inventory units and atomically claim one.

    Counted inventory is simple but concentrates all writes on one hot row per SKU. Unit tokens spread lock contention and make the no-oversell invariant intuitive. This design uses unit tokens as the allocation primitive while retaining aggregate counters as derived, quickly readable state.

    The token can represent a logical sale unit, not necessarily a serialized physical shoe.

    5. State Machines¶

    5.1 Sale state¶

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> SCHEDULED
    SCHEDULED --> WAITING_ROOM_OPEN
    WAITING_ROOM_OPEN --> ACTIVE
    ACTIVE --> SOLD_OUT: all units sold, not merely held
    ACTIVE --> ENDED
    SOLD_OUT --> ENDED
    SCHEDULED --> CANCELLED
    WAITING_ROOM_OPEN --> CANCELLED
    ACTIVE --> CANCELLED

    5.2 Inventory unit state¶

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> AVAILABLE
    AVAILABLE --> RESERVED: atomic allocation
    RESERVED --> SOLD: successful checkout
    RESERVED --> AVAILABLE: expiry or cancellation
    SOLD --> [*]

    5.3 Reservation state¶

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> ACTIVE
    ACTIVE --> PAYMENT_PROCESSING: checkout starts before expiry
    ACTIVE --> EXPIRED: expiry wins race
    ACTIVE --> CANCELLED: user or operator cancels
    PAYMENT_PROCESSING --> CONFIRMED: authorization verified and inventory committed
    PAYMENT_PROCESSING --> ACTIVE: payment fails with time remaining
    PAYMENT_PROCESSING --> EXPIRED: payment deadline passes
    CONFIRMED --> [*]
    EXPIRED --> [*]
    CANCELLED --> [*]

    Only conditional transitions are permitted. A generic UPDATE reservation SET state = ... without checking the previous state would create double releases and payment races.

    6.1 View the sale item¶

    GET /v1/flash-sales/{sale_id}/items/{sale_item_id}

    The product page is public, identical for most users, and changes infrequently. It should never reach the inventory database.

    Caching policy¶

    • Product name, description, price, and media can have long TTLs with versioned URLs.
    • Sale state can use a short TTL, for example 1-5 seconds near the event.
    • Publish scheduled state transitions proactively to edge caches.
    • Use stale-while-revalidate and stale-if-error.
    • Keep reservation APIs uncacheable and separate from public product APIs.

    Why coarse availability?¶

    An exact count changes too frequently and would turn every page refresh into a synchronized read against the hottest state. It can also mislead users because visible availability may be claimed before they reserve. Coarse availability is a projection; only an allocation transaction answers whether the user won.

    One unit-token implementation uses row locking:

);

-- Persist the scoped key, request hash, and result in this transaction.

    Important database constraints:

    CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_live_reservation_per_user_sale
ON reservations (sale_id, user_id)
WHERE state IN ('ACTIVE', 'PAYMENT_PROCESSING', 'CONFIRMED');

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_reservation_per_unit
ON reservations (unit_id)
WHERE state IN ('ACTIVE', 'PAYMENT_PROCESSING', 'CONFIRMED');

    Including CONFIRMED makes this a one-purchase-per-user-sale policy as well as one-live-hold policy. A failed-capture cancellation must transition ownership and release the unit atomically under the documented repurchase policy. The ER diagram shows historical reservations; the partial unique index limits current ownership. The exact partial-index syntax depends on the database. If unsupported, enforce the invariant through a dedicated ownership table whose key is (sale_id, user_id).

    9.3 Why SKIP LOCKED?¶

    Without it, many workers may wait behind the first available row. SKIP LOCKED lets each transaction claim a different unlocked unit. Preassign units to allocation buckets and vary the starting bucket to reduce index-page contention.

    An empty SKIP LOCKED result means no matching unlocked row was found, not proof of sold out: another transaction may still roll back its claim. Return a bounded retry/admission outcome unless canonical committed inventory proves exhaustion. The application must also abort the transaction if the select returns no unit or the conditional update affects zero rows; never insert a reservation using an unset or stale unit_id. The count-based alternative below must commit the counter change, reservation, idempotency result, and outbox together, rolling back the decrement on a uniqueness conflict. Test two concurrent requests from one user and a locked last unit whose owner rolls back.

    9.4 Count-based alternative¶

    A minimal approach is:

    UPDATE sale_inventory
SET available = available - 1,
    reserved = reserved + 1,
    version = version + 1
WHERE sale_item_id = :sale_item_id
  AND available > 0;

    If one row is updated, the reservation won. This is correct and often sufficient after the waiting room limits admission. Its bottleneck is serialization on one hot row. Mention it first in an interview, then evolve to buckets or unit tokens only if required by measured throughput.

    9.5 Bucketed inventory¶

    Split a SKU's units across a bounded number of inventory buckets:

    item:size9:bucket0 -> 25 units
item:size9:bucket1 -> 25 units
...
item:size9:bucket39 -> 25 units

    For an even illustrative split across ten variants, size 9 has 1,000 units across these forty buckets; the full sale has 10,000.

    Route an admitted user to a bucket using a hash, then probe another bucket if necessary. Each bucket is a single-writer or atomically updated partition. The sum of configured bucket quotas equals physical inventory, so buckets cannot collectively oversell.

    Do not create more buckets than necessary. Excessive partitioning complicates utilization and rebalancing.

    10. Requirement 3: Complete Payment¶

    10.1 Why payment is a saga¶

    The inventory database and payment provider cannot participate in one atomic transaction. Failures can occur after either side commits. The workflow needs explicit state, idempotency, retries, compensation, and reconciliation.

    10.2 Recommended checkout flow¶

    sequenceDiagram

    Persist a unique order identity per reservation, not only per checkout request key. Retry or reconciliation reuses that order if a crash occurs after inventory enters processing but before order creation finishes; the bounded processing expiry repairs abandoned starts.

    Prefer payment authorization followed by capture when the provider supports it:

    1. Authorize the amount without final settlement.
    2. Commit the inventory reservation to sold.
    3. Capture the authorization.
    4. Void the authorization if inventory confirmation unexpectedly fails.

    If the provider captures immediately, a successful charge followed by failed local confirmation requires an automatic refund saga.

    Capture timeout retains CAPTURE_PENDING and allocated inventory while the existing provider operation is resolved. A definitive capture failure starts an idempotent order-cancellation and inventory-release workflow before any fulfillment. Authorization alone does not prove capture, and a lost capture response is not failure.

    10.3 Starting payment near expiry¶

    When checkout starts before expires_at, conditionally transition:

    ACTIVE -> PAYMENT_PROCESSING

    Set a short payment-processing deadline. Expiry workers no longer release ordinary ACTIVE TTL, but a separate timeout eventually releases abandoned PAYMENT_PROCESSING reservations.

    This makes the boundary explicit: merely opening a checkout page should not extend inventory indefinitely, while a legitimate payment attempt gets a bounded grace period.

    Processing expiry terminates the allocation, not an unknown provider operation. Fence that reservation from future confirmation before releasing its unit. Later authorization is voided and later capture refunded through the original payment identity; a delayed callback must never sell a unit reassigned to someone else.

    10.4 Webhook and client race¶

    Both the client and webhook may report success. Only signed provider status is authoritative. Process provider events with a uniqueness constraint:

    UNIQUE (payment_provider, provider_event_id)

    Every state transition checks the current order/payment version. Duplicate callbacks return success without repeating inventory or financial effects.

    10.5 Order status¶

    The initial checkout response may be asynchronous. If the client times out, it polls GET /orders/{id} or receives a push notification. Never ask the user to pay again solely because the client did not receive the success response.

    11. Deep Dive: Consistency Under Extreme Contention¶

    11.1 The naive read-then-write bug¶

    This is unsafe:

    available = SELECT available FROM inventory
if available > 0:
    UPDATE inventory SET available = available - 1

    Thousands of requests can all read available = 1 before any update commits. The check and mutation must be one atomic conditional operation or transaction.

    11.2 Correct approaches¶

    Approach Correctness Throughput Complexity
    Atomic conditional SQL decrement Strong Moderate per hot SKU Low
    Pessimistic row lock Strong Serialized Low
    Optimistic CAS with version Strong with retries Poor under extreme contention Medium
    Unit rows with SKIP LOCKED Strong Higher parallelism Medium
    Bucketed inventory ownership Strong if quotas are fixed High Medium-high
    Single-writer stream per inventory shard Strong ordered decisions High and predictable High
    Redis atomic script only Atomic in Redis, durability depends on setup Very high Hidden recovery risk

    11.3 Recommended evolution¶

    1. Start with an atomic SQL decrement and a waiting room.
    2. If one row cannot meet admitted throughput, split inventory into fixed buckets or unit tokens.
    3. If many simultaneous sales require predictable throughput, assign each inventory shard a single-writer command processor backed by a durable log.

    The waiting room is not a correctness mechanism. It protects the correctness mechanism from unnecessary concurrency.

    11.4 Single-writer allocation model¶

    flowchart LR
    Admit[Admitted Requests] --> Commands[Durable Reservation Commands]
    Commands --> P0[Inventory Processor 0]
    Commands --> P1[Inventory Processor 1]
    Commands --> PN[Inventory Processor N]
    P0 --> S0[(Inventory Shard 0)]
    P1 --> S1[(Inventory Shard 1)]
    PN --> SN[(Inventory Shard N)]
    P0 --> Results[Reservation Results]
    P1 --> Results
    PN --> Results

    Partition commands by inventory bucket so one processor owns transitions for that bucket. There is no concurrent writer inside a partition. The tradeoff is asynchronous response handling and careful failover so two processors never own the same partition simultaneously.

    11.5 Redis in front of the database¶

    A Redis Lua script can atomically decrement a hot counter, but treating Redis as the only inventory authority creates hard questions:

    • What happens after failover before asynchronous replication completes?
    • Can acknowledged reservations disappear?
    • How are database and Redis reconciled without overselling?
    • Can cache eviction or restore introduce stale inventory?

    Use Redis for admission counters, rate limits, status projections, and locks only when their failure semantics are understood. If Redis allocates inventory, it must be configured and operated as a durable authority with a recovery design, not described casually as a cache.

    12. Deep Dive: Efficient Reservation Expiry¶

    12.1 Why not one timer per reservation?¶

    In-process timers disappear on restart, cannot coordinate across replicas, and are difficult to rebalance. Expiry must be recoverable from durable state.

    12.2 Expiry index plus authoritative CAS¶

    Use a time-ordered expiry index, such as a Redis sorted set or database index:

    score  = expires_at epoch milliseconds
member = reservation_id

    Workers claim due IDs in batches, but the inventory database decides whether expiry is still legal.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant Z as Expiry Index
    participant W as Expiry Worker
    participant DB as Inventory DB
    participant K as Kafka

    W->>Z: Fetch reservation IDs due before now
    Z-->>W: Batch of IDs
    loop Each reservation
        W->>DB: CAS ACTIVE -> EXPIRED if expires_at <= now
        alt Transition won
            DB->>DB: RESERVED unit -> AVAILABLE in same transaction
            DB->>DB: Insert ReservationExpired outbox event
        else Already processing/confirmed/expired
            DB-->>W: No-op
        end
    end
    DB-->>K: Relay expiry events

    The sorted set is an optimization and scheduling hint. If it loses data, a database sweeper repairs the gap:

    SELECT reservation_id
FROM reservations
WHERE state = 'ACTIVE'
  AND expires_at <= now()
ORDER BY expires_at
LIMIT :batch_size
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;

    Index (state, expires_at) and process bounded batches.

    12.3 Delayed queues¶

    A delayed-message queue can schedule one expiry command per reservation. It simplifies normal processing but still needs an authoritative conditional state transition and periodic sweeper because messages may be duplicated, delayed, or lost beyond retention.

    12.4 Payment versus expiry race¶

    At the boundary, payment and expiry may execute concurrently:

    • Payment attempts ACTIVE -> PAYMENT_PROCESSING.
    • Expirer attempts ACTIVE -> EXPIRED.

    Use a row lock or versioned compare-and-set. Exactly one transition wins. If expiry wins, checkout returns reservation expired. If payment processing wins, the normal expiry worker does not release the unit until the processing deadline.

    12.5 Releasing inventory exactly once¶

    Release the inventory unit in the same transaction that transitions the reservation to EXPIRED or CANCELLED. A retry sees that the reservation is no longer active and performs no second increment.

    13. Deep Dive: Millions of Simultaneous Arrivals¶

    The system cannot autoscale quickly enough at noon if it is cold at 11:59. Capacity and admission must be prepared before the event.

    13.1 Virtual waiting room¶

    flowchart LR
    Users[Millions of Users] --> Edge[CDN / WAF / Bot Checks]
    Edge --> Queue[Virtual Waiting Room]
    Queue --> Status[(Distributed Queue State)]
    Controller[Admission Controller] --> Queue
    Metrics[Inventory and Service Health] --> Controller
    Queue -->|bounded admitted traffic| Reserve[Reservation API]
    Reserve --> Inventory[(Inventory Authority)]

    The waiting room provides:

    • A stable place for excess users.
    • Bounded downstream concurrency.
    • A fairness policy.
    • Bot challenges and identity binding.
    • Estimated progress without hammering core services.

    13.2 Admission rate¶

    Admission should react to:

    • Remaining available inventory.
    • Active reservations and expected expiry rate.
    • Reservation-service p95/p99 latency.
    • Database lock wait and CPU.
    • Payment-provider health.
    • Error and timeout rates.

    Use a conservative initial rate, then increase while SLOs remain healthy. Stop admitting when all units are sold and no meaningful reservation expiries remain.

    13.3 Queue status delivery¶

    Options:

    • Poll every 5-15 seconds with jitter.
    • Server-Sent Events for admitted state.
    • Edge-issued signed tokens containing coarse queue epochs.

    Polling is operationally simple but can itself produce millions of QPS. Cache coarse queue-position bands at the edge and return Retry-After or nextPollAfterMs to control frequency.

    Do not promise an exact position if batching, bot removal, or randomization can change it. Display a range or progress estimate.

    13.4 Protecting downstream systems¶

    • Pre-scale all critical services before the event.
    • Prewarm connections, caches, and database query plans.
    • Separate product-view capacity from reservation capacity.
    • Use bounded queues and reject excess work rather than allowing memory growth.
    • Apply per-user and per-device request limits.
    • Set short timeouts and circuit breakers around dependencies.
    • Degrade analytics, recommendations, and nonessential logging first.
    • Use load tests that reproduce a synchronized step function, not only gradual ramps.

    13.5 Backpressure¶

    If the inventory database slows, reduce admissions immediately. Allowing the reservation-service queue to grow only turns a controlled slowdown into stale requests, timeouts, retries, and collapse.

    14. Deep Dive: Fair Access to Limited Inventory¶

    "First come, first served" sounds fair but network distance, device speed, clock skew, and bots make it unequal.

    14.1 Recommended policy¶

    1. Open the waiting room before the sale.
    2. Collect eligible entrants during a published pre-sale window.
    3. At sale start, cryptographically randomize those entrants.
    4. Admit them in randomized order at a controlled rate.
    5. Append post-start arrivals in FIFO order or later randomized waves.
    6. Bind each queue entry and admission token to one account and device/session.

    This is closer to a lottery among users who arrived on time and avoids rewarding millisecond-level races.

    14.2 Fairness properties¶

    • Document the policy before the event.
    • Use an auditable random seed committed before shuffling and revealed afterward when appropriate.
    • Ensure retries do not create new queue positions.
    • Preserve order across waiting-room failover.
    • Expire admission tokens quickly and make them single-purpose.
    • Do not allow queue tokens to be transferred between accounts.

    14.3 Bot mitigation¶

    • Require authenticated, verified accounts for reservation.
    • Apply CAPTCHA or proof-of-work selectively before queue admission.
    • Use device, IP, ASN, behavioral, and account-age signals.
    • Rate-limit account creation and payment instruments.
    • Enforce one live queue entry and reservation per account.
    • Detect token sharing and scripted checkout velocity.
    • Run suspicious winners through a fast fraud check without blocking every user equally.

    Bot detection should reject or deprioritize with explainable policy. False positives during a scarce sale are costly.

    14.4 Avoiding hidden unfairness¶

    • Route all regions into the same logical policy rather than giving one region earlier sale activation.
    • Use server time, never client time.
    • Publish the sale start through one authoritative control plane.
    • Make admission independent of repeated refresh frequency.
    • Do not let internal retries consume a user's one opportunity.

    15. Idempotency and Retry Semantics¶

    Every mutation needs a stable idempotency key:

    Operation Idempotency scope Stored result
    Join waiting room (sale_id, user_id) Queue entry and status token
    Reserve (user_id, idempotency_key) Reservation or terminal sold-out response
    Start checkout (reservation_id, idempotency_key) Order and PSP session
    PSP request Provider merchant key Provider payment reference
    Webhook Provider event ID Processing outcome
    Expire reservation Reservation state/version Conditional no-op or expiry result

    In the selected design, reservation idempotency and the business mutation commit atomically in the inventory database. A separate response cache is only a projection. Financial retry safety additionally depends on the provider's documented idempotency horizon, not only a stable local key.

    Responses should distinguish:

    • Definitive success.
    • Definitive sold out or expired.
    • Retryable overload.
    • Unknown outcome requiring status lookup.

    Blind retries with new keys can create duplicate payments or consume additional capacity.

    16. Data Partitioning and Hot Keys¶

    Inventory¶

    Partition by (sale_id, sale_item_id, allocation_bucket). Keep all transitions for a bucket on one database shard or single-writer partition.

    Inventory units and their active reservations must be colocated in the same transactional shard. Diagrams show them as separate logical stores for ownership clarity, but claiming, confirming, expiring, or cancelling a reservation must update both sets of tables in one local transaction.

    The baseline keeps all buckets for one sale in the same database uniqueness domain, including (sale_id,user_id) and idempotency records. Splitting buckets across independent databases would make a per-shard unique index insufficient for the one-reservation-per-sale rule. That evolution needs a durable sale/user claim protocol or a product restriction to one stable allocation shard per user; do not assume SQL uniqueness becomes global.

    Reservations¶

    Primary reads occur by reservation ID, user and sale, and expiry time. Maintain:

    • Primary key on reservation_id.
    • Unique ownership key on (sale_id, user_id) for live claims.
    • Expiry index on (state, expires_at).
    • Inventory ownership key on unit_id.

    Orders and payments¶

    Partition by user or order ID, but retain direct indexes by reservation and provider reference. Keep financial state in a dedicated store with immutable ledger entries.

    Queue entries¶

    Partitioning depends on fairness policy. A globally ordered per-sale queue is a hot logical stream. Store it as append-only segments with a sequence allocator or assign randomized ranks deterministically and partition by rank range.

    The queue need not support arbitrary sorting on every status request. Store the user's assigned rank and a global admitted watermark.

    approximate users ahead = max(0, user_rank - admitted_watermark)

    18.2 Regional fairness¶

    A single global ordering may disadvantage users with longer network paths. The waiting room can admit regional quotas proportional to eligible entrants, while inventory buckets reserve corresponding quotas. Unused regional quotas can return to a global pool after a published interval.

    This improves geographic fairness but adds policy and capacity complexity. For an interview, first choose one global waiting room and home-region allocator, then discuss regional quotas as an extension.

    18.3 Failover¶

    Inventory failover must use fencing:

    • A lease epoch identifies the active allocator.
    • Every write includes the current epoch.
    • The database rejects stale-epoch writers.
    • Promotion waits until the new primary has all committed state.

    Availability is secondary to preventing two regions from allocating the same units.

    20. Failure Modes and Recovery¶

    CDN or product cache failure¶

    • Fall back through regional caches to the catalog database.
    • Serve a static sale page with minimal dynamic state.
    • Protect the catalog database with rate limits and stale responses.

    Waiting room failure¶

    • Stop new admissions rather than route everyone to inventory.
    • Continue honoring already issued, valid admission tokens.
    • Recover queue state and admitted watermark from durable storage.
    • Extend admission-token validity cautiously if the outage consumed users' windows.

    Reservation service failure after commit¶

    The client may see a timeout even though the reservation succeeded. A retry with the same idempotency key returns the committed result. The user can also query their active reservation.

    Inventory database failure¶

    • Stop allocations until a fenced primary is available.
    • Continue product views and queue waiting.
    • Do not fail open or use a stale replica for allocations.

    Expiry worker failure¶

    • Reservations remain unavailable longer but are not oversold.
    • Replacement workers resume from the expiry index.
    • A database sweeper catches missed expirations.
    • Alert on oldest overdue active reservation.

    Payment provider outage¶

    • Stop or slow admission if winners cannot pay.
    • Allow existing reservations a bounded extension according to published policy.
    • Use circuit breakers and provider failover only if payment intent portability is understood.
    • Never silently submit the same charge to two providers.

    Payment succeeds but callback is lost¶

    • Poll provider status for unresolved payment attempts.
    • Consume provider webhooks with retries.
    • Run periodic ledger reconciliation.
    • Keep the reservation in PAYMENT_PROCESSING until its processing deadline.

    Payment captured but inventory confirmation fails¶

    • Retry the idempotent confirmation if the reservation still owns the unit.
    • If confirmation is impossible, create an automatic refund and notify the user.
    • Record both financial entries in an immutable ledger.

    Kafka unavailable¶

    • Authoritative transactions continue with outbox rows.
    • Availability UI and analytics become stale.
    • Relay events after recovery.
    • Alert on oldest unrelayed outbox record.

    21. Reconciliation and Audit¶

    Asynchronous and external systems require continuous repair.

    Inventory reconciliation¶

    For each sale item, verify:

    configured units
  = AVAILABLE unit rows
  + units owned by ACTIVE reservations
  + units owned by PAYMENT_PROCESSING reservations
  + SOLD units

    Compare unit rows, reservation ownership, aggregate counters, and confirmed orders. Never repair by blindly increasing availability; determine the authoritative owner first.

    Payment reconciliation¶

    • Compare provider settlement reports with local payment attempts.
    • Find authorized but uncaptured payments.
    • Find captures without confirmed orders.
    • Find refunds requested but not completed.
    • Produce an operator queue for ambiguous cases.

    Fairness audit¶

    Retain:

    • Queue-entry time and assigned randomized rank.
    • Randomization algorithm version and committed seed reference.
    • Admission time and token identity.
    • Bot or eligibility decision reason.
    • Reservation decision and inventory bucket.

    Avoid retaining more device or identity data than policy requires.

    22. Security and Abuse Prevention¶

    • Sign queue, admission, and checkout tokens with key rotation.
    • Bind admission tokens to user, sale, expiry, and intended action.
    • Mark tokens as used atomically or make their effect idempotent.
    • Authenticate every reservation and checkout mutation.
    • Apply WAF, DDoS controls, and per-identity rate limits at the edge.
    • Protect sale configuration changes with strict authorization and audit.
    • Verify payment webhook signatures and timestamps.
    • Never store raw card data unless operating within the required compliance scope.
    • Encrypt personal and financial data.
    • Redact secrets from logs and traces.
    • Detect queue token marketplaces and account/payment-instrument reuse.

    Rate limits should not punish normal retries caused by the system itself. Make clients obey server-provided retry intervals.

    23. Observability¶

    User-facing SLIs¶

    • Product-page p50/p95/p99 latency and edge hit rate.
    • Queue join and status availability.
    • Admission-to-reservation latency.
    • Reservation success, sold-out, conflict, and unknown-outcome rates.
    • Checkout conversion and payment latency.
    • Duplicate-charge and refund rates.

    Correctness metrics¶

    • Invariant violations by sale item.
    • Units without a valid owner.
    • Multiple live reservations per user or unit.
    • Confirmed orders without sold inventory.
    • Captured payments without confirmed orders.
    • Double-release attempts.

    Any nonzero oversell or duplicate-charge metric should page immediately.

    Contention and capacity¶

    • Database lock-wait time and transaction aborts.
    • Allocation throughput by inventory bucket.
    • Reservation-service queue depth.
    • Admission rate and admitted watermark.
    • Waiting-room population and estimated drain time.
    • Idempotency hit rate and client retry volume.
    • Expiry lag and oldest overdue reservation.

    End-to-end tracing¶

    Propagate one trace ID across queue admission, reservation, order, PSP request, webhook, and confirmation events. Sample ordinary failures and retain all ambiguous financial outcomes.

    24. Testing Strategy¶

    Correctness tests¶

    • Run thousands of concurrent claims against one remaining unit and assert exactly one winner.
    • Race payment start against expiry repeatedly.
    • Deliver every webhook and event multiple times.
    • Crash services after each transaction boundary.
    • Retry timed-out reservations with the same and different idempotency keys.
    • Verify that cancellation and expiry cannot release the same unit twice.

    Load tests¶

    • Model a vertical traffic step at the sale start, not a gradual ramp.
    • Include synchronized client retries and queue-status polling.
    • Test one hot SKU and skewed size preferences.
    • Run at least the expected breaking point, not only target load.
    • Include cache misses, cold connections, and dependency latency.

    Failure tests¶

    • Kill the active allocator and validate fencing during failover.
    • Pause Kafka and expiry workers.
    • Fail Redis and the waiting-room state replica.
    • Delay or duplicate PSP callbacks.
    • Return ambiguous PSP timeouts after successful authorization.
    • Test database failover while reservations are committing.

    Shadow and rehearsal¶

    Run a full production-like rehearsal with synthetic inventory and traffic. Verify dashboards, paging, rollback controls, queue messaging, and operator runbooks before the public event.

    25. Important Tradeoffs¶

    Database transactions versus distributed locks¶

    Prefer database conditional writes or row locks when inventory lives in that database. A separate distributed lock adds another failure mode and does not make the subsequent database mutation atomic by itself.

    Counted inventory versus unit tokens¶

    Counted inventory is the smallest correct design and may be enough after admission control. Unit tokens or buckets improve parallelism and auditability but add rows and allocation logic. Evolve only when the hot-row limit is real.

    Synchronous reservation versus queued command¶

    Synchronous reservation gives immediate decisions after admission. A durable single-writer command stream provides predictable ordering and throughput but turns the API asynchronous. Both can work; the waiting room already provides user-visible queueing, so synchronous database allocation is simpler initially.

    Short versus long reservation TTL¶

    A short TTL recycles inventory quickly but punishes slow payment methods and accessibility needs. A long TTL improves checkout completion but lets abandoned reservations hide inventory. Choose from measured payment latency and publish the timer clearly.

    Strict FIFO versus randomized admission¶

    Strict FIFO rewards low network latency and bots at the exact start time. Randomizing users present before the sale is usually more equitable, while FIFO can govern later arrivals. Fairness is a product requirement, not only a data-structure choice.

    Availability versus inventory correctness¶

    For product reads, serve stale data. For allocation, stop when exclusive ownership is uncertain. It is better to delay a purchase than sell the same unit twice.

    End-to-end behavior¶

    1. The CDN serves the product page and media without touching core databases.
    2. The edge waiting room authenticates entrants, mitigates bots, establishes fair order, and absorbs millions of excess requests.
    3. The admission controller releases traffic according to remaining inventory and service health.
    4. The reservation service atomically claims a counted, bucketed, or unit-token inventory record and creates a time-bounded reservation.
    5. Database uniqueness constraints enforce one live reservation per user and one owner per unit.
    6. Checkout transitions the reservation into a bounded payment-processing state instead of holding a database transaction open.
    7. The payment orchestrator uses provider idempotency, signed webhooks, and preferably authorization before capture.
    8. Inventory confirmation atomically marks the reservation confirmed and unit sold; financial compensation handles rare cross-system failures.
    9. Expiry workers use a time index for efficiency and a conditional database transition for correctness; a sweeper repairs missed timers.
    10. Outbox events update coarse availability, notifications, analytics, and audit stores without participating in the allocation transaction.
    11. Reconciliation continuously checks inventory equations and external payment settlement.

    27. Interview Deep-Dive Questions¶

    Why is a reservation necessary?¶

    Payment takes seconds and happens in an external system. Holding a lock throughout payment would serialize users and fail during PSP latency. A durable reservation removes one unit immediately and gives checkout a bounded window.

    How do you guarantee no overselling?¶

    Make one strongly consistent store authoritative and claim inventory through an atomic conditional write, locked unit row, or exclusively owned inventory bucket. Every retry is idempotent, and no cache is trusted for allocation.

    Is an atomic SQL decrement enough?¶

    Often yes, especially when a waiting room limits admitted concurrency. It is the smallest correct solution. If the single row becomes a measured bottleneck, evolve to fixed buckets or unit tokens.

    How do expired reservations return inventory?¶

    An expiry index schedules candidates, but a conditional database transition decides whether expiry wins. The reservation becomes expired and the unit available in one transaction. A database sweeper handles missed scheduled work.

    What if payment succeeds exactly as the reservation expires?¶

    Payment start and expiry compete through a versioned state transition. If payment moves ACTIVE to PAYMENT_PROCESSING first, it gets a short protected deadline. If expiry moves it to EXPIRED first, checkout cannot proceed. Only one transition wins.

    How do you absorb ten million requests in ten seconds?¶

    Serve public content from the CDN, put users into a virtual waiting room at the edge, issue status updates at controlled intervals, and admit only a bounded rate based on inventory and database health.

    How is the sale fair?¶

    Randomize authenticated entrants present during a published pre-sale window, then admit them at a controlled rate. Bind rank and admission tokens to identity, make retries position-preserving, mitigate bots, and audit the randomization.

    Why not use Redis to decrement inventory?¶

    Redis scripts are fast and atomic, but using Redis as authority requires a durability, failover, fencing, and reconciliation design. It is safer to use Redis for admission and projections while a transactional database owns scarce inventory, unless Redis is explicitly engineered as the durable authority.

    What if the reservation response times out after committing?¶

    The client retries with the same idempotency key or queries its active reservation. The service returns the previously committed result rather than allocating a second unit.

    What if the PSP charges the user but the order remains pending?¶

    Webhooks and reconciliation discover the successful charge. Retry idempotent local confirmation if inventory is still owned; otherwise trigger an automatic refund and alert on the ambiguous workflow.

    28. L5 and L6 Expectations¶

    L5 Expectations: Core¶

    • Identifies the need for time-bounded reservations.
    • Defines product, inventory, reservation, order, and payment entities.
    • Uses an atomic database operation to prevent overselling.
    • Uses caching for product reads and idempotency for retries.
    • Explains basic reservation expiry and payment flow.

    L5 Expectations: Strong Signals¶

    • Quantifies the synchronized burst and introduces a virtual waiting room.
    • Handles payment/expiry races with explicit state transitions.
    • Designs efficient expiry with a scheduling index and authoritative CAS.
    • Discusses hot-row evolution, outbox events, reconciliation, and degradation.
    • Defines an auditable fairness and bot-mitigation policy.

    L6 Expectations¶

    • Separates edge admission, authoritative commerce, and asynchronous recovery planes.
    • Connects admission rate to service health and inventory state.
    • Designs fenced multi-region ownership, disaster recovery, and financial compensation.
    • Distinguishes correctness data from approximate projections and analytics.
    • Drives policy tradeoffs around fairness, accessibility, TTL, geographic allocation, and failure behavior.

    29. Five-Minute Interview Summary¶

    A flash sale has extreme synchronized demand for a tiny inventory pool. I would serve the product page and coarse availability through a CDN, while a virtual waiting room absorbs reservation attempts and establishes an auditable order. An admission controller releases only bounded traffic based on remaining inventory and database health, so millions of impossible requests never reach the authoritative path.

    The reservation service is the sole inventory authority. It uses an atomic conditional decrement initially, or fixed inventory buckets/unit tokens if one hot row becomes a bottleneck. In one transaction it claims inventory, creates a five-minute reservation, and writes an outbox event. Database uniqueness constraints enforce one live reservation per user and one owner per unit. Idempotency keys make timeout retries safe.

    Checkout is a saga because the payment provider and inventory database cannot share a transaction. Starting payment conditionally moves the reservation into a short PAYMENT_PROCESSING state, preventing an expiry race. Prefer payment authorization, then atomically convert reserved inventory to sold, then capture. Duplicate client callbacks and provider webhooks are idempotent, and reconciliation handles ambiguous outcomes or refunds.

    Reservation expiries are scheduled through a time index, but the database performs the authoritative conditional transition and inventory release in one transaction. A periodic sweeper catches missed timers. The design favors highly available stale reads but stops allocations whenever exclusive inventory ownership is uncertain, because no overselling is the primary invariant.

    30. Final Takeaways¶

    1. Introduce a reservation between viewing and paying; never hold inventory locks during external payment.
    2. State no-oversell and ownership invariants before choosing technology.
    3. Use a CDN for public reads and a waiting room for synchronized demand.
    4. Let admission control reduce contention; do not confuse it with correctness.
    5. Start with the smallest correct atomic inventory mutation and evolve only when measured throughput requires buckets or unit tokens.
    6. Make every mutation and asynchronous consumer idempotent.
    7. Resolve payment, expiry, cancellation, and confirmation races with conditional state transitions.
    8. Schedule expiry efficiently, but let durable state decide whether release is legal.
    9. Treat payment as a saga with authorization, capture, compensation, and reconciliation.
    10. Keep exact inventory authority separate from cached availability projections.
    11. Define fairness explicitly through waiting-room policy, identity binding, bot defense, and auditability.
    12. Prefer stale availability over failure for reads, but pause allocation rather than risk split-brain overselling.

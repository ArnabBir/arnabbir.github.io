    Design a Calendar Service¶

    Interview Roadmap¶

    Clarify personal and shared calendars, recurring events, invitations, reminders, availability, and timezone behavior. Establish identity and recurrence invariants before selecting storage. Begin with transactional event series plus attendee responses, then add materialized occurrence windows, invitation delivery, free/busy, conflict handling, regional ownership, and timezone-database migrations.

    Product Framing¶

    A calendar stores a user's intent about time, not merely UTC intervals. Organizers create single or recurring events, invite attendees, modify one occurrence or a whole series, and view calendars in different zones. Attendees accept, decline, or propose another time. Reminders and email are derived delivery; they do not define whether an event exists.

    The scope includes private/shared calendars, ACLs, recurrence, invitations, free/busy, reminders, and synchronization. Resource booking can use the same model with stricter exclusivity. Out of scope are video meetings, email transport internals, and task management.

    Requirements¶

    • Create, update, move, and delete single events and recurring series.
    • Edit or cancel one occurrence, this-and-future occurrences, or an entire series.
    • Invite attendees and track independent responses without losing organizer changes.
    • Render correctly across timezones and daylight-saving transitions.
    • Query agenda ranges, sync changes by cursor, compute free/busy, and schedule reminders.
    • Support delegated calendars, sharing, resource reservations, audit, and privacy controls.
    • Target p99 under 250 ms for range reads and 99.99% availability for reads.

    Correctness Invariants¶

    1. Wall-clock event intent stores local date/time plus IANA timezone and recurrence rule; UTC instants are derived under a recorded timezone-data version. Absolute-instant and all-day events carry explicit distinct time semantics.
    2. An occurrence identity is stable across rematerialization and is not its display UTC timestamp.
    3. A recurrence exception applies to exactly one original occurrence identity; moved occurrences cannot reappear from base expansion.
    4. Organizer revision and each attendee response revision evolve independently; accepting cannot overwrite a newer title or time.
    5. One invitation command has one logical effect, although notifications may be delivered repeatedly.
    6. A resource with exclusive policy has no overlapping accepted reservation in canonical state.
    7. ACL revocation and event deletion dominate stale indexes, reminders, and sync projections.

    Capacity Estimates¶

    Assume 300 million users, 60 million daily active, 20 range reads and two mutations per active user/day: roughly 14,000 reads/s and 1,400 writes/s average, with 10x workday peaks. Invitation and reminder bursts cluster around quarter hours. If users retain 5,000 events, canonical objects reach 1.5 trillion logical records only at extreme maturity; active-window indexes are the serving concern.

    Never expand an unbounded daily recurrence forever. Materialize a rolling window, for example six months behind and eighteen months ahead, and expand farther on bounded queries. At 20 future occurrences per active series, one billion active series create 20 billion index rows; partition and tier by owner and time. Reminder fanout can exceed mutation rate by an order of magnitude.

    Core Entities and Data Model¶

    Calendar(calendar_id, owner_id, timezone, acl_version, home_region)
EventSeries(series_id, calendar_id, organizer_id, local_start, local_end,
            timezone_id, tzdb_version, rrule, state, revision)
OccurrenceOverride(series_id, original_local_key, new_local_start, new_local_end,
                   state, revision)
Attendee(series_id, principal_or_address, role, response, response_revision)
Invitation(invitation_id, series_id, attendee, organizer_revision, state)
OccurrenceIndex(calendar_id, start_utc, end_utc, series_id, original_local_key,
                materialization_generation)
Reminder(reminder_id, principal_id, occurrence_key, fire_at, state, version)
Change(calendar_id, sequence, object_id, operation, revision)

    EventSeries, overrides, attendees, ACLs, and changes are canonical. Occurrence indexes, free/busy, search, notification queues, and external email views are projections. An all-day event stores local dates with an exclusive end date, not midnight UTC.

    APIs and Interfaces¶

    POST /v1/calendars/{id}/events                         Idempotency-Key: <uuid>
PATCH /v1/events/{seriesId}?scope=SINGLE|FUTURE|ALL   If-Match: <revision>
DELETE /v1/events/{seriesId}?occurrence_key=...
PUT /v1/events/{seriesId}/attendees/me/response       {response, responseRevision}
GET /v1/calendars/{id}/events?start=...&end=...&cursor=...
GET /v1/freebusy?principal_ids=...&start=...&end=...
GET /v1/calendars/{id}/changes?cursor=...

    Range APIs cap duration and result count, return timezone and source revision, and paginate by (start_utc, occurrence_key) within a pinned materialization generation and canonical revision boundary. A moving occurrence or tzdb rollout otherwise changes sort keys during pagination. Sync cursors reference a calendar sequence, not wall time. Mutations use idempotency keys and optimistic organizer revisions. A conflict response includes current state so clients can merge explicitly.

    High-Level Architecture¶

    flowchart LR
    Clients --> API[Calendar API]
    API --> Canon[(Series, overrides, attendees, change log)]
    Canon --> Outbox[(Transactional outbox)]
    Outbox --> Expand[Recurrence materializer]
    Expand --> Occ[(Occurrence indexes)]
    Outbox --> Invite[Invitation workflow]
    Outbox --> Remind[Reminder scheduler]
    API --> Query[Range and free/busy query]
    Query --> Occ
    Query --> Canon
    Invite --> Mail[Email/push adapters]
    Remind --> Mail

    The canonical write transaction stores the object revision, calendar change sequence, idempotency result, and outbox. Materializers can lag; reads validate canonical deletion and ACL versions and can expand a small missing range on demand.

    Core Requirement Flows¶

    Create and Invite¶

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant O as Organizer
    participant C as Calendar service
    participant D as Canonical DB
    participant W as Invitation worker
    participant A as Attendee calendar
    O->>C: create series + attendees + key
    C->>D: transaction series, attendee rows, change, outbox
    D-->>C: organizer revision
    C-->>O: accepted
    D->>W: at-least-once invitation event
    W->>A: idempotent invitation upsert
    W-->>O: email/push delivery attempt

    Internal attendees receive a shared invitation identity and a local calendar projection. External addresses receive signed, expiring response links. Notification failure does not roll back the event; a durable workflow retries and exposes delivery status.

    Update Scope¶

    SINGLE writes an override keyed by original local occurrence. ALL changes the base series and increments revision. FUTURE splits the series at an occurrence boundary: the old rule gets an UNTIL before the boundary, a new related series starts there, and exceptions/attendees migrate according to explicit policy. The split is one transaction when colocated, preserving an audit link.

    Read and Sync¶

    Range reads query materialized occurrences and an override index by effective start/end, suppress overridden base identities, merge overrides, validate ACL and tombstones, and expand bounded gaps if the generation lags. An occurrence originally outside the range but moved inside must be found through that effective-time index; scanning only base recurrence positions misses it. Device sync pulls ordered canonical changes after a cursor; push is only a wake-up hint. If retention expired, return a consistent calendar snapshot at sequence S, then changes after S.

    Deep Dive 1: Recurrence Expansion¶

    Recurrence rules describe a set over local civil time: frequency, interval, weekdays, month positions, count or until, plus exclusions and additions. Expansion must be deterministic under one library and rule version. Occurrence identity can be (series_id, original_local_datetime, fold_policy), where original_local_datetime remains stable even if an override moves display time.

    Never generate all occurrences. A materializer expands bounded time buckets and records generation, series revision, and tzdb version. Upserts are idempotent by occurrence identity. A series revision invalidates affected buckets; workers recompute into a new generation and atomically switch a bucket pointer. Readers encountering stale or missing buckets synchronously expand only within strict limits and enqueue repair.

    Pathological rules are bounded by accepted syntax, maximum expansion per query, and CPU quotas. Monthly-on-day-31 skips months without that date unless product semantics specify clamping. Leap-day, negative month positions, exclusions, and count-after-exclusion behavior require standards-compatible tests.

    In standards-compatible mode, RFC 5545 sections 3.3.10, 3.8.4.4 and 3.8.5.1 require invalid generated dates/nonexistent local times to be skipped without counting them. DTSTART counts toward COUNT; exclusions apply afterward and do not create replacement occurrences. RECURRENCE-ID names the original scheduled start even when moved. Clamping a monthly date or shifting a generated DST-gap occurrence is a separately labeled product extension, not interchangeable RFC behavior (accessed 2026-09-08).

    Deep Dive 2: Timezones and Daylight Saving¶

    For a 9:00 AM weekly meeting in America/New_York, preserve 9:00 local time; UTC shifts when DST changes. For a flight at an absolute instant, product semantics might instead preserve UTC. The API must capture which intent applies, with wall-clock recurrence as the default for meetings.

    Local times can be nonexistent during spring-forward or ambiguous during fall-back. Define policy: reject nonexistent organizer input with alternatives, and require or consistently choose a fold for ambiguous input. Store IANA zone, local fields, chosen offset/fold, and tzdb version. Numeric offsets alone lose future rules.

    Governments update timezone rules. A tzdb rollout runs shadow rematerialization, reports changed future instants, then publishes a new generation. Near-term reminders are rescheduled from canonical intent. Do not silently rewrite historical displayed instants. Notify organizers only when product policy deems future changes material.

    flowchart TD
    Intent[Local time + IANA zone + rule] --> Expand[Expand local occurrences]
    TZDB[Versioned timezone database] --> Expand
    Expand --> Resolve{Valid local instant?}
    Resolve -->|normal| UTC[UTC index instant]
    Resolve -->|gap| Reject[Skip generated gap or reject invalid input]
    Resolve -->|fold| Choice[Recorded fold/offset]
    Choice --> UTC
    UTC --> Reminder[Reminder and range indexes]

    Deep Dive 3: Invitations and Concurrent Responses¶

    Organizer fields and attendee responses must not share one last-write-wins document. An organizer update increments organizer_revision; each attendee response conditionally updates its own row. The response records which organizer revision was viewed, allowing UI to ask for reconfirmation after material time changes without discarding the prior answer.

    Invitation workflows carry stable IDs. Duplicate email is undesirable but harmless; duplicate canonical attendee insertion is not. Internal delivery performs an idempotent projection upsert. Address changes, forwarding, group expansion, and removed attendees append transitions. A removed attendee cannot accept through a stale link because response authorization validates current membership and invitation version.

    Large distribution lists are expanded asynchronously into bounded batches with a manifest. Cancellation has priority and marks the invitation version first; delayed sends consult current state. Privacy options hide attendee lists or responses. Calendar spam defenses include sender reputation, consent gates, quarantine calendars, and rate limits.

    Deep Dive 4: Free/Busy, Resources, and Reminders¶

    Free/busy returns coarse intervals without titles or attendee identity. Build per-principal time-bucket projections from accepted/tentative occurrences, then validate access policy. Merge adjacent intervals and cap query fanout. Suggestions intersect participant availability in their working hours, score candidate times, and disclose approximation; they do not reserve time.

    Rooms require exclusivity. A resource calendar transaction conditionally inserts an interval reservation against a conflict index, or serializes requests per resource/time bucket. Recurrence booking can be all-or-nothing for a bounded horizon or return conflicting occurrences; this policy must be explicit. Human calendars usually allow overlaps.

    For intervals crossing buckets, independent bucket locks are insufficient: lock every intersected bucket in a common order under one transaction, or use a resource-owned interval exclusion constraint. Define half-open intervals so an event ending at 10:00 does not conflict with one starting at 10:00. The authoritative exclusion check includes pending accepted reservations, not just the eventual free/busy projection.

    Reminders derive fire_at from occurrence plus user preference. A hierarchical time wheel or time-partitioned queues feed workers. The worker claims with a lease, checks latest event/ACL state, writes a delivery intent idempotently, then sends at least once. Exactly-once notification is not promised. Quiet hours and device deduplication reduce repeated disturbance.

    Partitioning and Scaling¶

    Partition canonical data by calendar ID so series, overrides, ACL, and change sequence are local. Cross-calendar invitations are workflows, not distributed transactions. Large enterprise shared calendars get dedicated shards. Occurrence indexes partition by calendar and month; free/busy projections by principal and day. Reminder queues partition by fire-time bucket plus hash to spread quarter-hour spikes.

    Hot public calendars use edge caches and read replicas with current ACL/deletion epochs. Group invitations and tzdb rebuilds run in bulk pools separate from interactive writes. Fair queues isolate tenants. Reshard through calendar ownership epochs, snapshot at sequence S, replay later changes, fence old writes, and shadow-compare range results before switching.

    Consistency and Idempotency¶

    Series revisions, overrides, attendee rows, resource exclusion, ACLs, and per-calendar change order are strongly consistent. Occurrence indexes, free/busy, search, reminders, and notifications are eventual. Every derived row carries source revision and generation. Reads reject a projection newer or incompatible with canonical state and can omit stale data conservatively.

    Outbox delivery is at least once. Consumers upsert by stable occurrence/invitation/reminder IDs and source version. Mutation keys store request hashes and original responses. A retry after timeout returns the prior revision. Deletion tombstones and ACL epochs survive longer than cache and event retry windows.

    Reliability and Failure Modes¶

    If materialization lags, range reads expand small windows on demand and mark results degraded; huge queries fail explicitly. Invitation provider outage queues workflows without affecting event creation. Reminder backlog prioritizes near-term items, sheds low-value repeated reminders, and never sends an old reminder without rechecking source version. Cache loss uses request coalescing to protect occurrence stores.

    A crash after canonical commit is recovered from outbox. Duplicate expansion is an upsert. Missing sequence gaps stop a projection partition and trigger replay. Corrupt indexes rebuild from series snapshots and overrides. Resource booking rejects when its canonical conflict store is unavailable rather than double-book. Region failover uses fencing and idempotent client retries; reconciliation compares canonical events, occurrence windows, invitations, and scheduled reminders.

    Multi-Region Strategy¶

    Each calendar has a home region with synchronous multi-zone writes. Range projections and public calendars replicate near readers. Mutations route home because organizer order, ACL, and recurrence splits benefit from one owner. Invitations bridge independently owned calendars through versioned workflows.

    On region loss, promote a replica with a higher ownership epoch. If RPO is nonzero, lost idempotency rows cannot prevent duplicate retries. Recover acknowledged mutation, ACL, resource-reservation and key state before reopening exclusive booking; otherwise expose uncertain changes and reconcile instead of assuming client retry repairs the missing prefix. Disconnected regional projections may serve bounded-stale reads but must apply a globally distributed deny epoch for deleted or revoked calendars. Tzdb and policy artifacts are signed control-plane releases, while calendar traffic stays in regional data planes.

    Security and Abuse Prevention¶

    Apply OAuth scopes, resource-level ACLs, delegated-access audit, mTLS, encryption, key rotation, and least-privilege service identities. Free/busy reveals only allowed granularity. Signed invitation links bind invitation, recipient, action, version, and expiry. Sanitize descriptions, URLs, and attachments; prevent SSRF and notification-header injection.

    Defend against calendar spam, invitation floods, resource squatting, schedule scraping, reminder amplification, and compromised delegates. Sensitive event titles must not appear in logs or lock-screen notifications without policy. Retention distinguishes user deletion, enterprise hold, organizer audit, and anonymized operational metrics.

    Observability and Testing¶

    Measure mutation/range latency, change and materialization lag, stale generation reads, recurrence expansion CPU, occurrences per rule, invitation age and duplicate sends, response conflicts, reminder lateness, free/busy fanout, resource contention, ACL denials, and tzdb-induced shifts. Trace by series and occurrence identity without logging titles.

    Property-test recurrence against standards corpora across leap years, month ends, gaps, folds, exclusions, and series splits. Model-test organizer updates racing attendee responses and deletion racing reminders. Test cursor snapshots, retries, duplicate/out-of-order events, ACL revocation, and resource overlap. Chaos-test queue and region failures. Shadow every tzdb/library upgrade and compare occurrence sets over a multi-year horizon.

    Important Tradeoffs¶

    Storing only UTC simplifies indexes but destroys recurring wall-clock intent; local intent plus derived UTC wins. Eager infinite expansion is impossible; bounded windows add read fallback and rebuild complexity. Embedding attendee responses in one event document simplifies reads but creates lost updates and large fanout; independent rows win. Active-active calendar writes reduce latency but complicate splits, ACLs, and sync order; home ownership is usually preferable. Exactly-once reminder delivery is unattainable across external providers; idempotent intents and user-visible deduplication target the actual effect.

    Final Architecture¶

    flowchart TB
    Apps --> Regional[Regional API and query cells]
    Regional --> Owner[Calendar home owner]
    Owner --> Canon[(Series, overrides, attendees, ACL, changes, outbox)]
    Canon --> Stream[(At-least-once event stream)]
    Stream --> Materialize[Versioned recurrence expansion]
    Materialize --> Index[(Occurrence and free/busy indexes)]
    Stream --> Workflow[Invitation workflows]
    Stream --> Schedule[Reminder scheduler]
    Workflow --> Delivery[Email and push adapters]
    Schedule --> Delivery
    Regional --> Index
    Regional --> Canon
    Control[Signed tzdb and policy control plane] --> Materialize

    Interview Deep-Dive Questions¶

    1. Why is occurrence identity not its UTC start time?
    2. How does editing this-and-future split a recurring series?
    3. What happens to a 9:00 meeting across daylight-saving changes?
    4. How can an attendee respond while the organizer edits the title?
    5. How do you prevent a stale cancellation email or reminder?
    6. How do room bookings differ from human calendar events?
    7. How would you roll out a new timezone database safely?

    L5 and L6 Expectations¶

    An L5 answer should model series and overrides, preserve timezone intent, paginate ranges, separate attendee responses, make invitations/reminders idempotent, and address materialization and failures. An L6 answer should define occurrence identity, future-series splitting, DST gap/fold policy, tzdb migration, resource exclusion, projection repair, regional fencing, privacy, overload tiers, and organizational ownership of standards and notification policy.

    Five-Minute Summary¶

    A calendar stores civil-time intent in a series, not just UTC rows. Persist local start/end, IANA zone, tzdb version, recurrence rule, stable occurrence identity, overrides, independent attendee responses, ACLs, a calendar change sequence, and outbox atomically. Expand only bounded windows into versioned UTC occurrence indexes, and merge canonical exceptions at read time. A single edit writes an override; a future edit splits the rule at a deterministic boundary. Internal invitations are idempotent cross-calendar workflows, while email and push are retryable delivery. Reminder workers claim time buckets and recheck current source version before sending. Partition by calendar, keep one fenced home-region writer, rebuild projections from snapshots plus changes, and treat ACL deletion as higher priority than cache freshness. Test DST gaps, folds, leap dates, retries, concurrent responses, and timezone-library migrations as core correctness, not edge cases.

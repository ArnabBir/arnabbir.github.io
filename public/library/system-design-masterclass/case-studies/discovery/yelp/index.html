    Yelp: Local Business Search and Reviews¶

    This chapter develops an end-to-end design for a Yelp-like service. It is intended both as a study guide and as a structure for a 45-60 minute system design interview.

    The core challenge is not storing business pages or reviews. It is serving relevant, geographically constrained search results with low latency while reviews, ratings, and business data continue to change.

    1. Interview Roadmap¶

    A strong interview discussion can use this order:

    Phase Time Goal
    Requirements 5 minutes Establish the product boundary and scale
    Entities and APIs 5 minutes Define the contract and data ownership
    High-level design 15 minutes Satisfy each functional requirement
    Deep dives 15-20 minutes Search, geo-indexing, ratings, consistency, and scale
    Wrap-up 5 minutes Failures, tradeoffs, and final architecture

    2. Understanding the Problem¶

    Yelp lets users discover nearby businesses, inspect business details and reviews, and contribute reviews. Search may combine:

    • Free text: good ramen, Blue Bottle, or dentist.
    • A category: restaurants, plumbers, hotels, or salons.
    • A location: latitude/longitude, city, neighborhood, or map area.
    • Filters: rating, price, open now, distance, or attributes.
    • Ranking signals: textual relevance, distance, quality, popularity, and personalization.

    We will design the core public product, not the business-owner administration platform.

    3. Requirements¶

    3.1 Functional Requirements¶

    Core requirements¶

    1. Users can search for businesses by name, free text, category, and location.
    2. Users can view a business and its paginated reviews.
    3. Authenticated users can create or update a review with a mandatory 1-5 rating and optional text.

    Clarifications¶

    • A user may have at most one review per business.
    • Search results show a business summary: name, category, location, rating, review count, and optionally a representative image.
    • Search uses the user's coordinates or a resolved named location.
    • Newly created reviews should appear on the business page immediately or within seconds.
    • Aggregate ratings and search results may lag briefly.

    Out of scope¶

    • Business onboarding and owner verification.
    • Ads and sponsored ranking.
    • Social feeds, follows, reactions, and review comments.
    • Reservations, food ordering, and payments.
    • Personalized recommendations.
    • Full moderation and fraud-detection design.
    • Turn-by-turn maps and routing.

    3.2 Non-Functional Requirements¶

    Requirement Target Reasoning
    Search latency p95 under 500 ms Search is the primary interactive path
    Business-page latency p95 under 300 ms Business reads are frequent and cacheable
    Availability 99.99% for reads Users should still browse during partial failures
    Review durability No acknowledged review loss Reviews are user-authored source data
    Consistency Strong for review ownership; eventual for search and aggregates Prevent duplicate reviews without coupling all reads
    Scale 100M daily active users, 10M businesses Establishes read-heavy internet scale
    Geographic support Global, location-aware Nearby results require spatial indexing

    3.3 Correctness Invariants¶

    • An acknowledged review is durably stored and is never lost because a cache, stream, aggregate, or search index fails.
    • A user has at most one current review per business; retries with the same idempotency key return the original mutation result.
    • Only eligible review states contribute to rating_sum, review_count, and average_rating, and each review version affects those aggregates at most once.
    • Review updates, deletions, and moderation changes preserve a versioned audit history and cannot be overwritten by delayed older events.
    • Canonical business and review databases are authoritative; ratings, caches, and search documents are rebuildable projections.
    • Search pagination remains bound to a stable sort tuple or point-in-time view so page traversal does not repeat or omit results solely due to concurrent index updates.
    • Authorization is checked against canonical ownership for every review mutation; possession of an ID or stale cache entry grants no write access.

    3.4 Back-of-the-Envelope Estimates¶

    These estimates establish orders of magnitude rather than exact capacity plans.

    Assume:

    • 100M daily active users.
    • 10 searches per active user per day.
    • 5 business-page views per user per day.
    • 1M review writes per day.
    • 10M businesses.
    • 1B total reviews.
    • 2 KB average review record including metadata, excluding secondary indexes and replicas.
    • 5 KB average business search document, excluding images.

    Traffic:

    Searches/day          = 100M * 10 = 1B
Average search QPS    = 1B / 86,400 ~= 11,600
Peak search QPS       = 5 * average ~= 58,000

Business views/day    = 100M * 5 = 500M
Average view QPS      ~= 5,800
Peak view QPS         ~= 29,000

Review writes/day     = 1M
Average review QPS    ~= 12
Peak review QPS       ~= 100-200

    Storage:

    Reviews               = 1B * 2 KB ~= 2 TB raw
With indexes/replicas  ~= 6-10 TB
Search documents      = 10M * 5 KB ~= 50 GB raw
With shards/replicas   ~= 150-300 GB

    The ratio confirms a read-heavy design. Search throughput and hot-business traffic matter more than average review-write throughput.

    3.5 Sensitivity and headroom¶

    Capacity should be expressed as a formula so a changed interview assumption does not invalidate the design:

    peak_search_qps = DAU * searches_per_user_per_day * peak_factor / 86,400

At 100M DAU, 10 searches/day, and 5x peak:       57,900 QPS
If searches rise to 20/day:                     115,700 QPS
If peak factor also rises from 5x to 8x:        185,200 QPS

    Suppose one OpenSearch data node sustains 800 representative queries/s at the target p95 with 30% safety headroom already included. The initial 58K peak needs roughly 58,000 / 800 ~= 73 query-serving node equivalents before replica topology, zone-failure reserve, and indexing capacity. If 800 were instead a saturation benchmark, the denominator would be 800 * 0.70; use one convention, not both. This is a planning input, not a universal node benchmark; replay production query mixes to measure the real denominator.

    Review projections also amplify writes. At 200 peak review mutations/s, an aggregate update, search-document update, cache invalidation, moderation task, and audit event produce about 200 * 5 = 1,000 downstream operations/s. A ten-minute consumer outage creates roughly 200 * 600 = 120,000 review events to drain. If consumers can safely process 2,000/s while 200/s continue arriving, recovery takes about 120,000 / (2,000 - 200) ~= 67 seconds.

    4. Core Entities¶

    erDiagram
    USER ||--o{ REVIEW : writes
    BUSINESS ||--o{ REVIEW : receives
    CATEGORY ||--o{ BUSINESS_CATEGORY : classifies
    BUSINESS ||--o{ BUSINESS_CATEGORY : belongs_to
    BUSINESS ||--|| RATING_AGGREGATE : has

    USER {
        uuid user_id PK
        string display_name
        string status
        timestamp created_at
    }

    BUSINESS {
        uuid business_id PK
        string name
        string description
        decimal latitude
        decimal longitude
        string address
        string city
        string timezone
        string status
        timestamp updated_at
    }

    REVIEW {
        uuid review_id PK
        uuid user_id FK
        uuid business_id FK
        int rating
        string text
        string status
        timestamp created_at
        timestamp updated_at
    }

    RATING_AGGREGATE {
        uuid business_id PK
        bigint rating_sum
        bigint review_count
        decimal average_rating
        bigint version
    }

    CATEGORY {
        int category_id PK
        string name
        int parent_category_id
    }

    Why keep the aggregate?¶

    Computing AVG(rating) across thousands or millions of reviews on every business read or search is too expensive. RATING_AGGREGATE provides an incrementally maintained value that can be copied into the cache and search index.

    5. API Design¶

    5.1 Search businesses¶

    GET /v1/businesses/search?q=ramen&lat=37.7749&lon=-122.4194&radius_m=5000&category=restaurant&min_rating=4&cursor=...

    Response:

    {
  "businesses": [
    {
      "businessId": "b_123",
      "name": "Marufuku Ramen",
      "categories": ["Ramen", "Japanese"],
      "location": { "lat": 37.785, "lon": -122.432 },
      "distanceMeters": 1520,
      "averageRating": 4.5,
      "reviewCount": 2841,
      "thumbnailUrl": "https://cdn.example.com/business/b_123/cover.jpg"
    }
  ],
  "nextCursor": "opaque-token"
}

    Notes:

    • Use an opaque cursor instead of an offset for stable pagination at scale.
    • Cap radius_m, page size, and query complexity.
    • Coordinates may be replaced by a location_id produced by geocoding.

    5.2 Get business details¶

    GET /v1/businesses/{business_id}

    The response contains the business profile, categories, opening hours, attributes, aggregate rating, and media references. Images themselves are served by a CDN.

    5.3 Get reviews¶

    GET /v1/businesses/{business_id}/reviews?sort=recent&limit=20&cursor=...

    Possible sort modes include recent, highest, lowest, and useful. Each sort mode needs a corresponding index or a precomputed ranking strategy.

    5.4 Create a review¶

    POST /v1/businesses/{business_id}/reviews
Idempotency-Key: 765899d0-...
Authorization: Bearer <token>
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "rating": 5,
  "text": "Excellent noodles and fast service."
}

    Return 201 Created after the durable database commit. The response should include the canonical review and current processing state.

    5.5 Update a review¶

    PUT /v1/businesses/{business_id}/reviews/me
Authorization: Bearer <token>

{
  "rating": 4,
  "text": "Updated after a second visit."
}

    PUT is naturally idempotent. Optimistic concurrency can use an If-Match header or a review version.

    6. High-Level Architecture¶

    6.1 Start with one synchronous PostgreSQL/PostGIS system¶

    For a new product, OpenSearch and Kafka are not prerequisites. One multi-AZ PostgreSQL cluster with PostGIS can own businesses, categories, reviews, rating aggregates, and an outbox table. A synchronous review transaction inserts or updates the review, adjusts rating_sum and review_count, and inserts a versioned cache-refresh event in the outbox. After commit, the outbox relay invalidates or refreshes the cache; no network cache call runs inside the SQL transaction. Search uses a GiST location index plus PostgreSQL full-text or trigram indexes:

    SELECT business_id, name, average_rating,
       ST_Distance(location, :point) AS distance_m
FROM businesses
WHERE status = 'ACTIVE'
  AND ST_DWithin(location, :point, :radius_m)
  AND search_text @@ websearch_to_tsquery('simple', :query)
ORDER BY ts_rank(search_text, websearch_to_tsquery('simple', :query)) DESC,
         distance_m ASC, business_id ASC
LIMIT :limit;

    Use a generated tsvector, a GIN text index, a GiST geography index, and the pinned keyset pagination described below. This baseline gives immediate ratings, simple transactions, and one repair surface. It is appropriate while representative load tests remain below roughly 2,000 peak searches/s, p95 stays below 300 ms, database CPU stays below 60% at peak, replica lag stays below one second, and review writes do not create visible lock waits. These are measured trigger examples, not laws; hardware and query shape determine the actual limits.

    Do not migrate merely because the business count crosses a round number. Migrate when at least one sustained signal appears despite query/index tuning: search p95 exceeds 500 ms, search consumes over 50% of primary capacity, text/geo relevance requires typo tolerance or facets PostgreSQL cannot economically provide, synchronous rating lock p99 exceeds 100 ms, or projection work threatens the review-write SLO.

    6.2 Evolve projections without changing the source of truth¶

    First introduce a transactional outbox while still applying the rating synchronously. Next relay outbox events to Kafka and build OpenSearch in shadow mode. Compare result quality, counts, and latency, then switch search reads with an alias or feature flag. Finally move rating and cache projections asynchronous only after consumers are idempotent and reconciliation exists. The review row remains authoritative throughout.

    flowchart LR
    A[Stage 1: PostgreSQL and PostGIS] -->|Search pressure measured| B[Stage 2: Outbox and Kafka]
    B -->|Shadow index validated| C[Stage 3: OpenSearch reads]
    C -->|Write contention measured| D[Stage 4: Async aggregates and caches]
    A --- A1[Synchronous review and rating transaction]
    B --- B1[Durable change stream]

    During migration, dual-read comparisons are safe; application dual writes are not. Populate OpenSearch from a database snapshot at watermark W, replay outbox events after W, verify sampled documents and deletions, then cut over. If OpenSearch regresses, route reads back to bounded PostGIS queries while the data volume still permits it.

    7.2 Why not query the primary database?¶

    A relational database can filter a small dataset using a spatial extension such as PostGIS, but the complete query combines:

    • Fuzzy and prefix text matching.
    • Category and attribute filters.
    • Distance calculations.
    • Relevance and popularity scoring.
    • Faceting and aggregations.

    A search engine is designed for this combination. It stores denormalized business documents and provides inverted and spatial indexes. The primary database remains the authoritative source.

    7.3 Search document¶

    {
  "business_id": "b_123",
  "name": "Marufuku Ramen",
  "description": "Japanese ramen restaurant",
  "categories": ["restaurant", "japanese", "ramen"],
  "location": { "lat": 37.785, "lon": -122.432 },
  "city_id": "sf",
  "neighborhood_ids": ["lower-pacific-heights"],
  "average_rating": 4.5,
  "review_count": 2841,
  "price_tier": 2,
  "is_open": true,
  "status": "ACTIVE",
  "popularity_score": 0.82,
  "updated_at": "2026-09-05T12:00:00Z"
}

    Copying display fields into this document avoids making one database request per search result. is_open is only a dated projection: an open-now filter needs business-local schedules, holiday exceptions, timezone data and an explicit query-time instant. Recheck freshness or calculate from the schedule at serving time; a boolean indexed hours ago cannot mean open now.

    7.4 Geo-indexing¶

    Common options:

    Technique Strength Limitation
    Geohash Easy bucketing and prefix-based locality Cell boundaries require neighbor lookup
    Quadtree Adaptive hierarchical partitioning More index-management complexity
    R-tree Efficient geometric range queries Commonly provided by spatial databases
    Search-engine geo point Built-in distance and polygon queries Couples query behavior to search engine

    For this design, use the search engine's geo_point index. Internally it divides the world into cells and examines only cells intersecting the requested region.

    Exact distance can use the Haversine formula after coarse cell filtering:

    a = sin^2(delta_lat / 2)
  + cos(lat1) * cos(lat2) * sin^2(delta_lon / 2)

a = clamp(a, 0, 1)  # floating-point roundoff guard
distance = 2R * atan2(sqrt(a), sqrt(1-a))

    The important idea is to avoid calculating distance against all 10M businesses.

    7.5 Ranking¶

    A simplified ranking function is:

    final_score =
    w1 * text_relevance
  + w2 * distance_decay
  + w3 * bayesian_rating
  + w4 * log(1 + review_count)
  + w5 * popularity
  + w6 * business_quality

    Raw average rating should not dominate ranking. A business with one five-star review should not necessarily outrank one with 2,000 reviews averaging 4.7.

    A Bayesian-adjusted rating helps:

    weighted_rating = (v / (v + m)) * R + (m / (v + m)) * C

R = business average rating
v = business review count
C = global or category average
m = confidence threshold

    In a real system, ranking evolves into a learned model, but explaining the initial deterministic signals is sufficient for the core design.

    7.6 Search pagination¶

    Offset pagination (page=500) forces the search engine to collect and discard many preceding results. Stable keyset pagination needs both an immutable read snapshot and the last item's sort tuple; a tuple plus a vague "query version" is insufficient while documents change. In OpenSearch, page one opens a point-in-time snapshot and the signed cursor contains {pit_id, query_hash, expires_at, last_score, last_distance, last_business_id}. Every continuation uses the same PIT and search_after tuple:

    (score, distance, business_id)

    For the equivalent ordering score DESC, distance ASC, business_id ASC, the relational keyset predicate is:

    WHERE score < :last_score
   OR (score = :last_score AND distance > :last_distance)
   OR (score = :last_score AND distance = :last_distance
       AND business_id > :last_business_id)
ORDER BY score DESC, distance ASC, business_id ASC
LIMIT :limit;

    The predicate is evaluated only inside the pinned PIT or an equivalent immutable result generation. PostgreSQL cannot safely carry an ordinary MVCC snapshot through stateless pooled requests. For the baseline implementation, page one therefore materializes a bounded, expiring SearchSnapshot(snapshot_id, query_hash, ordinal, business_id, score, distance) result set; later pages use WHERE snapshot_id = :snapshot_id AND ordinal > :last_ordinal ORDER BY ordinal LIMIT :limit. The cursor is opaque, signed, query-bound, and expiring. If its PIT or materialized snapshot expires, return an explicit restart response instead of silently querying current data. business_id is the deterministic tie-breaker.

    7.7 Cache strategy¶

    Search queries have a large key space because coordinates differ. Improve hit rate by normalizing:

    • Lowercase and canonicalize query text.
    • Sort filter parameters.
    • Cache coarse candidate sets by geocell, not final distances or exact-radius results shared across different coordinates. Expand the candidate radius by the maximum cell displacement, then recompute distance and enforce the caller's original radius before final ranking.
    • Cache only the first result page for 15-60 seconds.
    • Include search-index version or schema version in the key.

    Do not depend on caching for correctness. Cached/PIT candidates still pass a current business-removal and review-visibility gate within a declared freshness bound; if that policy cannot be established, omit affected content. Keep next cursors after the last scanned candidate so all-filtered pages advance. Caching is an optimization for hot locations and queries.

    8.2 Review storage and access pattern¶

    The dominant query is:

    SELECT ...
FROM reviews
WHERE business_id = ? AND status = 'VISIBLE'
ORDER BY created_at DESC, review_id DESC
LIMIT 20;

    Create an index equivalent to:

    CREATE INDEX reviews_by_business_recent
ON reviews (business_id, status, created_at DESC, review_id DESC);

    At moderate scale, sharded relational storage is attractive because it provides transactions and uniqueness constraints. At very large scale, a wide-column store can efficiently keep reviews clustered by business, but enforcing one review per user and supporting updates becomes more complicated.

    8.3 Sharding reviews¶

    Shard by a stable hash of business_id:

    shard = hash(business_id) mod number_of_logical_shards

    Benefits:

    • A business's review pages require one shard.
    • Rating-rebuild jobs can scan one business locally.
    • The access pattern aligns with the partition key.

    Risk:

    • A globally famous business can create a hot partition.

    Mitigations:

    • Cache its first review pages.
    • Add read replicas.
    • Store old review pages in object storage or a secondary read model.
    • If necessary, split an extreme business by (business_id, bucket) and fan out reads. Do this only for exceptional hot keys because it complicates ordering.

    8.4 Consistent review pagination¶

    Use a cursor containing (created_at, review_id). A query for the next page becomes:

    WHERE business_id = :business_id
  AND status = 'VISIBLE'
  AND (created_at, review_id) < (:last_created_at, :last_review_id)
ORDER BY created_at DESC, review_id DESC
LIMIT :limit;

    Concurrent new reviews appear before the cursor and do not shift already visited pages.

    The review, synchronous aggregate change when enabled, and versioned outbox event are committed in the same local database transaction. Preserve a version high-watermark or conditional fill token even while the value is absent; otherwise an old in-flight load can refill the cache after deletion. Unconditional eviction itself is harmless to correctness but cannot establish version fencing.

    9.2 One review per user per business¶

    Enforce the invariant in storage:

    ALTER TABLE reviews
ADD CONSTRAINT one_review_per_user_business
UNIQUE (user_id, business_id);

    An application-side SELECT followed by INSERT is insufficient because two concurrent requests may both observe no review and then insert.

    If resubmission means update, perform an upsert or expose a separate update API. Preserve review history in an audit table or immutable change log if moderation requires it.

    9.3 Idempotency¶

    Mobile clients retry after timeouts. The service should store the (user_id, idempotency_key) and response for a bounded period. Repeating the same key returns the original result rather than performing another mutation.

    The uniqueness constraint protects the domain invariant; the idempotency key gives retries predictable API semantics.

    The review, request hash, and stored idempotency outcome commit together. For sharded reviews, route (business_id,user_id) to a stable bucket so two requests for one pair cannot reach independent uniqueness constraints. PostgreSQL unique and exclusion constraints apply inside their database boundary, not across unrelated shards (accessed 2026-09-08). The baseline spatial columns and query point use geography when radius is in meters, as specified by ST_DWithin; latitude/longitude geometry would otherwise use angular units.

    10. Deep Dive: Maintaining Average Ratings¶

    10.1 Incremental calculation¶

    Store:

    rating_sum
review_count
average_rating = rating_sum / review_count if review_count > 0 else null

    For a new visible review with rating r:

    rating_sum   = rating_sum + r
review_count = review_count + 1

    For an update from old_rating to new_rating:

    rating_sum = rating_sum - old_rating + new_rating

    For deletion or moderation removal:

    rating_sum   = rating_sum - old_rating
review_count = review_count - 1

    10.2 Avoiding lost updates¶

    Two consumers can update the same aggregate concurrently. Options include:

    1. Atomic SQL increments.
    2. Optimistic concurrency using a version field and retry.
    3. Kafka partitioning by business_id, giving ordered processing for each business.

    Use Kafka ordering plus atomic updates. Partition ordering reduces races, while atomic storage operations protect against deployment mistakes or multiple consumers.

    10.3 Exactly-once effect¶

    Kafka commonly provides at-least-once delivery. Consumers must tolerate duplicate events.

    Maintain processed event IDs for a complete, ordered delta stream. If events can be reordered or versions skipped, store the last applied (review_id, version, visible_rating_or_null) contribution, not just the version. For each newer full-state event, subtract the stored contribution if visible, add the new contribution if visible, and update contribution/version in the same transaction as the aggregate. An older event does nothing. For example, if visible rating 2 at v1 jumps directly to rating 5 at v3, add 3 relative to stored v1; applying v3's producer delta of only +1 from unseen v2=4 would undercount. Visibility transitions update both sum and count, and removing the last visible review yields null, not division by zero.

    BEGIN
  INSERT INTO processed_events(event_id) VALUES (?)
  -- If duplicate key, stop.
  UPDATE rating_aggregate ...
COMMIT

    This produces an exactly-once effect even when delivery is repeated.

    10.4 Reconciliation¶

    Incremental aggregates can drift due to bugs or historical data corrections. Run periodic reconciliation:

    1. Recompute sums and counts from authoritative visible reviews.
    2. Compare them with stored aggregates.
    3. Correct mismatches.
    4. Emit an event to refresh caches and search documents.

    Ambiguous names such as Springfield require country, region, user location, and popularity as disambiguation signals.

    12. Deep Dive: Hot Businesses and Skew¶

    A celebrity venue, viral review, or city-wide event can concentrate reads and review activity on one business_id, defeating capacity plans based only on average traffic.

    12.1 Read-path isolation¶

    • Cache the business summary and first review pages as versioned objects, with request coalescing and TTL jitter to prevent stampedes.
    • Serve images through the CDN and bound review-page size so a hot page does not create per-review hydration fan-out.
    • Use read replicas for colder pages, but cap concurrency and serve a stale safe page rather than allowing one business to exhaust database connections.
    • Detect hot keys from per-business QPS and latency instead of permanently adding a special storage path for every business.

    12.2 Write and aggregate hot keys¶

    Review writes remain authoritative SQL transactions, partitioned by business_id. Partitioning Kafka by the same key preserves per-business event order but makes a viral business one hot consumer partition. The aggregator should use atomic deltas, micro-batch consecutive changes for that business, and scale unrelated partitions independently; it must not shard one aggregate blindly because updates and moderation reversals still need one auditable result.

    If one business exceeds a single aggregate writer, maintain bucketed partial sums keyed by (business_id, bucket_id) and derive the displayed total from versioned partials. Reconciliation still recomputes the canonical aggregate from eligible reviews, so bucket movement or duplicate delivery cannot silently corrupt the rating.

    12.3 Overload behavior¶

    Prioritize durable review commits and ownership checks over immediate aggregate, search, and cache refresh. Bound queues, expose source-to-projection lag, and degrade to a slightly stale rating or cached first page. Never bypass uniqueness, acknowledge an uncommitted review, or run an unbounded primary-database query because the search or cache tier is overloaded.

    13. Data Consistency Model¶

    Data Consistency Explanation
    Review creation Strong Never acknowledge before durable commit
    One-review invariant Strong Enforced by a unique constraint
    Review read-after-write Prefer strong/session consistent User should see their own submission
    Average rating Eventual, normally seconds Derived asynchronously
    Search document Eventual, normally seconds Search is a projection
    Search cache Eventual with short TTL Slightly stale results are acceptable
    Business administration changes Eventual in search; strong in source DB Removed businesses need priority invalidation

    For read-your-write behavior, the review creation response includes the review, and the client can optimistically insert it into the review list. Alternatively, route the user's immediate read to the primary or a causally caught-up replica.

    Business metadata and aggregate rating arrive as events. The indexer creates one denormalized document per business.

    Business metadata and rating aggregates have independent version spaces. Apply partial fields under their own source versions, or have one materializer join them into a new unified document generation. Comparing an aggregate version directly with a business revision can discard valid updates or regress unrelated fields. Aggregate publication itself uses a transactional outbox.

    Operational requirements:

    • Include entity version and event ID in every event.
    • Ignore stale events that arrive after newer versions.
    • Send repeatedly failing records to a dead-letter queue with alarms.
    • Track source-to-index lag.
    • Support complete index rebuilds without downtime.

    Zero-downtime reindexing¶

    1. Create businesses_v2 with the new mapping.
    2. Backfill documents from a database snapshot.
    3. Replay events generated after the snapshot watermark.
    4. Verify counts and sampled records.
    5. Atomically move the businesses_read alias from v1 to v2.
    6. Retain v1 briefly for rollback.

    15. Reliability and Failure Modes¶

    15.1 Search cluster unavailable¶

    • Serve recently cached first-page results when available.
    • Optionally degrade to category-based popular businesses by region.
    • Do not fall back to an unbounded relational text query.
    • Apply circuit breakers so failures do not exhaust service threads.

    15.2 Redis unavailable¶

    • Treat Redis as optional and read from the source database/search cluster.
    • Rate-limit and shed nonessential traffic to protect databases.
    • Use bounded local caches for the hottest immutable projections.

    15.3 Kafka delayed or unavailable¶

    • Review writes continue because events remain safely in the outbox.
    • Ratings and search documents become stale.
    • Alert on outbox age and consumer lag.
    • Relay events after Kafka recovers.

    15.4 Database primary unavailable¶

    • Business reads can continue from cache and replicas.
    • Review writes pause or fail over to a synchronous replica.
    • Prefer rejecting writes over acknowledging data that may be lost.

    15.5 Poison events¶

    • Retry with exponential backoff.
    • After a threshold, move the event to a dead-letter queue.
    • Keep processing unrelated partitions where possible.
    • Provide replay tooling after correcting the bad data or consumer bug.

    17. Abuse, Privacy, and Security¶

    Although full moderation is outside the core design, mention the boundaries:

    • Authenticate review mutations and authorize updates by ownership.
    • Rate-limit by user, device, IP, and business.
    • Scan text and media asynchronously for policy violations.
    • Track review status: PENDING, VISIBLE, REMOVED, or QUARANTINED.
    • Encrypt traffic and sensitive data; avoid storing unnecessary location history.
    • Keep audit records for moderation actions.
    • Support privacy deletion while retaining legally required anonymous accounting data.
    • Detect coordinated spam using account age, velocity, device signals, similarity, and graph features.

    Reviews awaiting moderation may be visible only to the author. Rating aggregation must include only eligible review statuses.

    18. Observability¶

    Track service-level indicators rather than only machine health.

    Search¶

    • QPS and p50/p95/p99 latency.
    • Zero-result and error rates.
    • Cache hit rate.
    • Search cluster CPU, heap, rejected queries, and shard skew.
    • Geocoder latency and ambiguity rate.

    Reviews¶

    • Write success and conflict rates.
    • Database commit latency.
    • Idempotency replays.
    • Outbox age and relay failures.
    • Kafka lag by consumer and partition.

    Correctness¶

    • Aggregate reconciliation mismatch rate.
    • Search-index freshness and source/index version gaps.
    • Missing or duplicate document counts.
    • Read-after-write visibility delay.

    Use end-to-end request IDs and propagate trace context through Kafka event headers.

    19. Testing Strategy¶

    Correctness and contract tests¶

    • Race concurrent creates and updates for the same (user_id, business_id) and assert one current review with deterministic idempotent responses.
    • Duplicate, delay, and reorder review events; each eligible review version must affect the aggregate and search document at most once.
    • Exercise moderation, deletion, and update transitions and compare incremental ratings with a full authoritative recomputation.
    • Page through reviews and search while concurrent writes occur, asserting cursor stability, tie-break ordering, and documented read-your-write behavior.
    • Test authorization, named-location ambiguity, Unicode normalization, geo-boundary cases, and API/schema compatibility.

    Search quality and migration tests¶

    • Maintain labeled query sets for text relevance, category, typo tolerance, distance, named places, boundaries, and Bayesian ranking.
    • Shadow new mappings and ranking versions, then compare zero-result rate, relevance judgments, latency, and source/index version gaps before alias cutover.
    • Rebuild an index from a snapshot plus event watermark and verify counts, sampled records, deletions, and rollback to the old alias.

    Load and failure tests¶

    • Load-test peak geo searches, one hot business, first-page cache stampedes, skewed Kafka partitions, and synchronized client retries.
    • Inject Redis, search, Kafka, geocoder, and database failures and verify bounded fallback behavior rather than unbounded primary-store queries.
    • Pause consumers and outbox relays, then verify lag alarms, replay, reconciliation, and recovery without acknowledged review loss.

    20. Capacity and Partition Planning¶

    Search cluster¶

    • Partition primarily for capacity, then use replicas for read throughput and availability.
    • Avoid too many tiny shards; shard count should reflect data size and node count.
    • Route queries to regional replicas.
    • Separate heavy indexing from user query capacity where needed.

    Review database¶

    • Start with logical shards substantially exceeding physical hosts, such as 1,024 logical buckets.
    • Map buckets to physical database clusters through a routing layer.
    • Move buckets during rebalancing without changing external IDs.
    • Replicate each shard across availability zones.

    Business IDs and review IDs¶

    Use UUIDv7, ULID, or a Snowflake-style identifier to generate unique IDs without a central database round trip. Avoid exposing shard topology directly in IDs unless operational simplicity justifies it.

    21. Important Tradeoffs¶

    SQL versus NoSQL for reviews¶

    Choose sharded SQL initially because:

    • Review writes need uniqueness and transactions.
    • Writes are far lower than reads.
    • The main access pattern has a clear composite index.

    Choose a wide-column store when review volume or availability requirements exceed the relational setup and the team accepts application-managed uniqueness and secondary read models.

    Synchronous versus asynchronous rating updates¶

    Synchronous updates provide immediate aggregate consistency but increase write latency and contention. Asynchronous updates keep review writes isolated and resilient. Yelp-like search can tolerate a few seconds of rating staleness, so asynchronous processing is the better default.

    Cache invalidation versus TTL¶

    Business status changes, especially closure or policy removal, should trigger active invalidation. Ordinary rating updates can rely on event-driven refresh plus a TTL safety net.

    Database geo queries versus search engine¶

    PostGIS is sufficient for smaller products or simple distance queries. A dedicated search engine is justified when text relevance, typo tolerance, facets, filters, and geo-ranking are all required at high throughput.

    End-to-end behavior¶

    1. Search requests go to a geo-aware, denormalized OpenSearch index.
    2. Business details come from Redis or the authoritative business database.
    3. Reviews are read from a database partitioned by business and paginated by cursor.
    4. Review writes commit with an outbox event in one transaction.
    5. Kafka consumers update rating aggregates, invalidate caches, moderate content, and refresh search documents.
    6. Reconciliation and reindex jobs repair drift and support schema evolution.
    7. Regional caches and search replicas keep the dominant read paths fast and available.

    23. Interview Deep-Dive Questions¶

    How do you prevent a business with one five-star review from ranking first?¶

    Use Bayesian rating, a minimum-review confidence threshold, and separate textual relevance from quality signals.

    What happens immediately after a user submits a review?¶

    The authoritative review is visible through the review service, while aggregate ratings and search may remain stale for several seconds. The response or client-side optimistic update provides read-your-write behavior.

    How do you guarantee only one review per user and business?¶

    Use a database unique constraint on (user_id, business_id). API idempotency alone is not enough.

    How do you avoid losing the rating update event?¶

    Write the review and outbox event in the same database transaction, then relay the outbox asynchronously.

    How do you handle a celebrity business receiving huge traffic?¶

    Cache its details and first review pages, use read replicas, coalesce concurrent cache misses, and isolate pathological hot partitions only when observed.

    How do you rebuild the search index?¶

    Backfill a versioned index from a source snapshot, replay events after the snapshot watermark, validate it, and atomically switch an alias.

    Why is eventual consistency acceptable?¶

    Search rank and average rating can be slightly stale without violating a financial or ownership invariant. Durable review storage and uniqueness remain strongly consistent.

    24. L5 and L6 Expectations¶

    L5 Expectations: Core¶

    • Clarifies the three core requirements.
    • Defines reasonable APIs and entities.
    • Separates search from the transactional database.
    • Uses caching and cursor pagination.
    • Explains the basic review-write and rating-update flow.

    L5 Expectations: Strong Signals¶

    • Quantifies traffic and storage.
    • Explains geo indexing, denormalized search documents, and ranking.
    • Handles concurrency for one-review uniqueness and aggregate updates.
    • Uses an outbox, idempotent consumers, sharding, and failure degradation.
    • Discusses consistency explicitly.

    L6 Expectations¶

    • Drives requirement and product tradeoffs rather than listing technology.
    • Designs operational paths: index rebuilds, reconciliation, regional ownership, and hot-key handling.
    • Connects architecture to SLOs, capacity, cost, and failure isolation.
    • Identifies evolution paths instead of prematurely adding maximum complexity.

    25. Five-Minute Interview Summary¶

    Yelp is a read-heavy system whose hardest path is low-latency text and geographic search. I would store canonical businesses and reviews in transactional databases, partition reviews by business, and enforce one review per user and business with a unique constraint. A denormalized OpenSearch document supports text, filters, geo-distance, and ranking without joining the primary database for every result.

    Business details and popular review pages are cached in Redis, while images are served from object storage through a CDN. Review creation commits the review and a transactional outbox event together. Kafka consumers then update the rating aggregate, moderation state, cache, and search index asynchronously. This gives durable and strongly consistent review ownership while accepting short-lived eventual consistency for ratings and search.

    The design scales reads through regional search replicas, caches, and database replicas. It handles retries with API idempotency and idempotent consumers, protects against dual-write loss with the outbox pattern, and repairs derived-state drift through reconciliation and zero-downtime search reindexing.

    26. Final Takeaways¶

    1. Use the database for durable truth and invariants.
    2. Use a search engine for combined text, filter, ranking, and geo workloads.
    3. Denormalize the search read model to avoid per-result database calls.
    4. Partition review data according to its dominant access pattern: business_id.
    5. Enforce one review per user and business at the database layer.
    6. Update ratings incrementally, process events idempotently, and reconcile periodically.
    7. Use a transactional outbox instead of unsafe database-plus-Kafka dual writes.
    8. Accept eventual consistency only for derived data, not acknowledged user content or ownership rules.
    9. Design explicit degraded behavior for cache, search, Kafka, and database failures.
    10. Explain tradeoffs and evolution paths rather than naming technologies without justification.

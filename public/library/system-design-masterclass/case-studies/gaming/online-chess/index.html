    Design an Online Chess Platform¶

    Interview roadmap¶

    Design real-time online chess with matchmaking, authoritative clocks, legal moves, reconnection, ratings, spectators, tournaments, and anti-cheat. Clarify time controls, scale, fairness, and correspondence play. Start with one authoritative game server and durable move log, then add regional routing, queues, hot-event fanout, clock compensation, rating pipelines, and trust systems.

    Product framing and requirements¶

    Players seek rated or casual games, accept matches, make legal moves, resign, offer draws, and reconnect. Spectators watch with optional anti-cheat delay. The platform stores game history and updates ratings. Tournaments and computer analysis are secondary consumers. We support standard chess first; variants require versioned rules engines.

    Move acknowledgement should be below 150 ms p99 in-region, matchmaking below 10 seconds for common pools, clock disagreement below 100 ms under normal networks, and 99.99% active-game availability. A game must have one authoritative move order and result. Clients render optimistically but never decide legality, clock, or rating.

    Correctness invariants¶

    • One game generation has one fenced authority and a totally ordered sequence of legal moves.
    • Move n+1 applies only to the exact position hash after move n and the side to move.
    • Clock time never increases except an increment/delay credit defined by the pinned time control or an explicit versioned adjustment; elapsed time is charged exactly once per accepted turn.
    • A terminal result is immutable unless an auditable adjudication creates a superseding administrative record.
    • Rated game pairing rules and rating algorithm version are fixed when the game starts.
    • Duplicate client commands produce one logical move or result.
    • Spectators and opponents never receive private anti-cheat evidence or hidden pairing attributes.

    Capacity estimates¶

    Assume 50 million daily players, 5 million concurrent connections, 500,000 active games, and 200,000 move commands/s at peak. A game averages 80 plies. At 300 bytes/event, 10 million games/day produce 240 GB/day of canonical moves before replication, while WebSocket fanout dominates bandwidth. A championship with five million spectators and a 1 KB update creates 5 GB of edge egress per move, or 40 Gbit/s at one move/second before protocol overhead. Keep one canonical move and replicate through regional/gateway fanout; one game ID is both a sequencing hot key and a potentially large egress burst. Presence heartbeats at one/minute produce 83,000/s; avoid second-level durable writes.

    Pool fragmentation must be measured before adding dimensions. If 120,000 players seek games concurrently, splitting into 4 regions, 6 time controls, 2 rated modes, and 5 broad rating bands leaves an average 120,000 / 240 = 500 tickets/cell, but demand is skewed. If the quietest region has 5% of traffic and a niche time control has 2%, that combination sees only 120,000 * 0.05 * 0.02 / (2 * 5) = 12 tickets per rated/rating cell before variant, trust, or latency constraints. At one arrival every 2 seconds and only 20% mutually compatible opponents, expected compatible arrival is roughly one per 10 seconds. Dashboards therefore track live cardinality and arrival rate per effective pool, and the product merges or widens sparse pools rather than assuming global averages.

    Core flows and algorithms¶

    Matchmaking¶

    Queue entries are grouped by variant, rated flag, time control, region, and rough skill band. A matcher scans oldest entries and computes eligible opponents. The acceptable rating distance expands with wait time, for example band = min(max_band, base + growth * wait_seconds). Cost combines rating difference, estimated RTT, color balance, repeated-opponent penalty, and wait fairness. Choose mutual minimum cost within hard constraints.

    Tickets use leases and compare-and-set state from WAITING to MATCHED; a unique player-active-game constraint prevents double pairing. Match creation transactionally fixes rules, time control, ratings, region, and game generation, then emits an outbox event. Bots and suspected collusion cohorts may enter separate pools, but opaque trust policy must not leak through API responses.

    The primary queue index is ordered by (pool_id, rating_bucket, enqueue_time, ticket_id). A secondary (pool_id, region, enqueue_time) index supports latency-first scans, and (player_id, state) enforces one live ticket. A matcher claims the oldest unleased anchor, computes its widening window, scans adjacent rating buckets up to a fixed candidate cap, and batch-fetches RTT, color history, block lists, and recent opponents. Hard filters run first. Eligible candidates sort deterministically by total cost, then oldest enqueue_time, smaller rating difference, lower predicted maximum RTT, and lexical ticket_id; this tie-break order makes replay and incident diagnosis possible.

    Widening is piecewise and policy-versioned. One common pool might allow +/-50 rating for 0-5 seconds, add 25 points every 5 seconds to +/-200, then permit adjacent regions below a 120 ms RTT ceiling after 20 seconds. Both players' windows and region consent must accept the pair. The scan budget remains bounded, for example four buckets and 128 candidates per attempt, so an old impossible ticket cannot monopolize a shard. Rejected anchors back off briefly while new candidates arrive.

    Worked matchmaking example. Ticket A, rating 1510, has waited 17 seconds, so its window is +/-125. Candidates B at 1580 and C at 1490 both accept A. Their costs after normalization are 23 and 23. B enqueued at 10:00:03 and C at 10:00:01, so C wins the oldest-ticket tie-break. If C does not mutually allow the predicted 95 ms region, B wins without changing the ordering formula.

    Atomic pair reservation across partitions¶

    Queue indexes may be partitioned by pool and rating bucket, so the two tickets need not share a transactional shard. Do not lock two shards synchronously in arbitrary order. A reservation coordinator creates a durable PairIntent(pair_id, ticket_a, ticket_b, policy_version, expires_at, state) on a consensus-backed match ledger, then conditionally changes each ticket from WAITING to RESERVED(pair_id) using its ticket version. Claims execute in canonical ticket-ID order. If either compare-and-set fails, the coordinator releases any successful claim with matching pair_id; expiry sweepers do the same after a bounded lease.

    After both claims are visible, one idempotent ledger transaction creates game_id, fixes game policy, changes the intent to COMMITTED, and writes an outbox record. Ticket shards consume that record and mark their rows MATCHED. A player-active-game uniqueness record keyed by player ID is also claimed before commit. Recovery treats the match ledger as authority: a committed intent is completed, never compensated into two fresh tickets; an uncommitted expired intent is released. This is a small saga with a linearizable decision record, not a claim of distributed exactly-once execution.

    Cleanup first wins a conditional PREPARING -> ABORTED decision in the match ledger, competing with PREPARING -> COMMITTED; only then may it release matching ticket claims. Ticket shards must not independently recycle a claim merely because its wall-clock lease elapsed while the coordinator could still commit. Active-player keys remain protected by the same terminal decision. The later client-to-matchmaker sequence assumes both tickets and active-player keys are colocated; it is not a cross-shard SQL transaction.

    Legal moves and event sourcing¶

    The authority reconstructs position from a recent checksummed snapshot plus subsequent events. A pure, versioned rules engine validates side to move, piece movement, check, castling rights, en passant, promotion, repetition, fifty-move rule, and insufficient material. It returns new position hash and terminal status. Persist the event with expected prior sequence and authority epoch before acknowledging.

    Pin the exact draw policy. FIDE Laws effective 2023, articles 5, 6.9 and 9, distinguish claimable threefold/50-move draws from automatic fivefold/75-move draws and make a timeout a draw if the opponent cannot possibly mate by any legal sequence. Dead position is broader than a simple piece-count test. These are over-the-board laws; online auto-claim, lag compensation and disconnect policies are explicitly versioned product adaptations, not claims of FIDE online certification (accessed 2026-09-08).

    Position hashing uses Zobrist keys as a lookup accelerator, but a candidate repetition match compares the exact rule-relevant position identity, including side to move, castling and applicable en-passant rights. A hash collision must not decide a draw. Snapshots retain repetition history/counts and the halfmove counter as well as FEN; a position FEN alone does not encode how often it has occurred. Generate legal moves by applying pseudo-legal candidates and rejecting positions leaving the king in check. Rules engine determinism is tested exhaustively with perft positions. Upgrades do not alter running or historical games; each game pins rules_version.

    Authoritative clocks¶

    At turn start store each remaining duration and a monotonic server timestamp. On a move, compute elapsed = now_monotonic - turn_started, optionally subtract bounded server-observed network compensation under a published policy, then apply increment/delay and persist resulting clocks with the move. Client timestamps cannot be trusted. The move and charged time commit atomically, so retries do not charge twice.

    Monotonic timestamps from different hosts or boots are not comparable. Recovery records a clock/authority epoch and reconstructs remaining duration from the last committed clocks plus an explicitly bounded outage-time policy. If elapsed-time mapping cannot be trusted, pause/adjudicate under that policy rather than subtracting a new host's monotonic clock from the old host's value. Normal move increments are allowed increases under the pinned time-control version.

    For a timeout, schedule an in-memory timer but verify against authoritative state when it fires. A delayed timer cannot flag a player who already moved. If move and timeout arrive concurrently, the authority serializes them; policy defines whether arrival before authoritative deadline wins. Gateways continuously estimate RTT and show a smooth local countdown, corrected by server events.

    Reconnection and command idempotency¶

    Each device connection has an epoch. A reconnect fetches snapshot and events after the client's last sequence, then atomically supersedes its older socket. Commands deduplicate by (game_id, actor_id, command_id) and require expected event sequence. A stale command receives current state, not blind retry. If acknowledgement is lost, retry returns the prior move result. Disconnection does not pause rated clocks unless the predefined format says so.

    Spectators and tournaments¶

    Canonical events enter a fanout bus. Ordinary games use gateway subscriptions; hot games write compact event objects to an origin cache and CDN/WebSocket tree. A configurable delay queue withholds events for anti-cheat. Reconnect uses sequence from durable history. Spectator chat has separate moderation, partitions, and overload policy; it cannot affect the game authority.

    Tournament control assigns rounds, tie-break policy, pairings, and start deadlines. It references games rather than embedding play state. Swiss pairing is a constrained optimization using score groups, color balance, previous opponents, byes, team/country constraints, and deterministic tie-breaks. Pairing inputs and algorithm version are immutable and auditable. If computation fails, operators can publish a signed replacement pairing generation.

    Full Swiss pairing and tournament recovery¶

    At the round deadline, freeze an input generation containing eligible players, scores, opponent history, color history, withdrawals, prior byes, ranking seed, and pairing-policy version. Sort by score, then configured tournament tie-breaks and stable player ID. Assign an odd player a bye by scanning from the lowest score for the lowest-ranked eligible player who has not received one; the bye and its points are explicit pairing records.

    Process score groups high to low. Float the lowest-ranked compatible player from an odd group into the next group according to the policy's float history. Split an even group into upper and lower halves, then search pairings between halves. A legal edge forbids previous opponents and violates no absolute color, team, federation, club, or availability constraint. Edge cost penalizes score difference, repeated float direction, color imbalance, same-affiliation pairings when soft, and deviation from seed order. Solve minimum-cost perfect matching; for typical groups, branch-and-bound with most-constrained-player-first and deterministic candidate order is practical. If no perfect matching exists, backtrack floats and exchanges across adjacent groups. Relax only constraints listed as soft, in a fixed order, and record each relaxation. Color assignment then minimizes alternation and imbalance, with deterministic lot/seed tie-breaks.

    flowchart TD
  F[Freeze round input generation] --> B{Odd field}
  B -->|yes| Y[Assign eligible deterministic bye]
  B -->|no| G[Build score groups]
  Y --> G
  G --> O[Float odd groups by policy]
  O --> H[Split upper and lower halves]
  H --> M[Minimum-cost legal matching]
  M --> P{Perfect matching found}
  P -->|no| X[Backtrack floats and ordered soft constraints]
  X --> M
  P -->|yes| C[Assign colors]
  C --> V[Validate every player exactly once]
  V --> R[Publish signed pairing generation]

    Worked Swiss round. A four-player score group contains seeds A,B,C,D; A-C and B-D are rematches. The initial upper/lower pairing is rejected. The alternative A-D,B-C avoids rematches; if both opponents' color histories allow assigning A Black, it also avoids a third White for A, so no color relaxation is needed. If neither color assignment meets the hard rules, backtrack rather than declaring the pair legal. This is an illustrative configurable pairing heuristic, not a complete implementation of a named FIDE Swiss system.

    Tournament recovery operates at round scope, not game scope alone. The controller is stateless over a consensus-backed tournament log and acquires a fenced (tournament_id, round, controller_epoch) lease. Pairing publication is compare-and-set from DRAFT to one immutable PUBLISHED generation; game creation is idempotent by (tournament_id, round, board). A replacement controller reconciles every board into not-created, created-not-started, active, or terminal, replays missing outbox messages, and starts only games referencing the published generation. It never recomputes pairings merely because some game creation calls timed out.

    If published pairings are provably corrupt before play begins, an authorized operation cancels all not-started board allocations and publishes a superseding signed generation linked to the original. Once any game starts, repair policy preserves completed/active games and creates explicit administrative results or a tournament-wide pause; silently changing opponents would corrupt standings. Round advancement requires all boards terminal or adjudicated, a standings checksum, and one committed next-round input snapshot.

    Worked tournament-wide recovery. The controller crashes while creating 5,000 round-three games. The ledger shows one published pairing generation, 4,930 GAME_CREATED outbox records, 40 games committed without delivered notifications, and 30 boards absent. The new epoch replays the 40 notifications and idempotently creates only the 30 absent boards. It does not rerun Swiss pairing, so no player can receive a different opponent after partial publication.

    Deep dives¶

    Rating updates¶

    Game completion writes a unique rating-work item. A per-player ordered processor applies Glicko-2 or Elo with pinned algorithm version. Two simultaneous games must not overwrite each other; lock/order by player or transactionally compare rating version. Multi-player transaction across both players can use a database transaction when colocated; otherwise write an immutable rating ledger and materialize balances in deterministic event order. A correction never edits historical entries. Elo/Glicko updates are path-dependent: reversing one old delta does not reproduce the ratings that later games would have produced. Either apply an explicitly defined current-rating adjustment policy or replay the affected deterministic rating history into a new generation and append the resulting adjustments with provenance.

    Fair play¶

    Signals include move agreement with engines at multiple depths, think-time correlation, accuracy conditional on position difficulty, tab/device behavior where lawful, account graph, rating trajectory, and collusion patterns. No single signal proves cheating. Online rules may delay suspicious tournament broadcasts or rate-limit accounts; punitive decisions use versioned models, calibrated thresholds, human review, and appeals. Keep raw sensitive telemetry access restricted and retention bounded. Never run invasive analysis in the move critical path.

    Authority placement and migration¶

    Consistent hashing places games by ID on regional authority shards, but an allocator accounts for player RTT and shard load. A shard holds leases from a quorum store with epoch. To drain, stop accepting new games, snapshot active games, stop and fence old writes, transfer the final acknowledged tail and clock handoff state, increment epoch, and have clients reconnect. The old process cannot append after losing its epoch. Live migration may cost a short pause; silently running two authorities is worse.

    Partitioning, scaling, and regions¶

    Partition canonical events by game ID, preserving order. Snapshot every 10-20 plies to bound recovery. Archive completed games to object storage while retaining query indexes. WebSocket gateways shard by connection and subscribe through regional buses. Rating processing partitions by player ID; tournament standings by tournament ID. Presence uses expiring leases and approximate counts.

    Home a game in the region minimizing the maximum player RTT subject to residency and capacity. Both players connect through nearby edges to that region. Cross-region synchronous move replication would add latency; instead commit to zonal quorum in the home region and asynchronously replicate events to a paired region. On regional loss, increment authority epoch in surviving quorum, recover from replicated log, and expose possible RPO according to mode. High-stakes tournaments can pay for synchronous dual-region commits or a dedicated venue region.

    Hot spectator fanout never changes game partition load. Cache snapshots and events by sequence, use hierarchical pub-sub, and degrade chat, analysis overlays, then spectator freshness before player moves. Matchmaking can widen region/rating bands only with explicit user policy. Admission stops new games before active-game headroom is consumed.

    Consistency, idempotency, and failures¶

    Move order, clock, result, and game authority require linearizable behavior per game. Lobby presence, spectator count, search, analysis, and fair-play features are eventual. Match and move creation use transactional outboxes. Queue redelivery is absorbed by command/game IDs. Rating effects are idempotent by game and player. We do not claim exactly-once WebSocket delivery; sequence-based catch-up is the contract.

    Gateway loss reconnects without stopping clocks. Game-process loss reconstructs from snapshot/log under a higher lease epoch. Event-store unavailability stops move acknowledgement rather than accepting uncommitted play. Fanout failure does not block canonical appends; players poll/catch up. Matchmaker partition may create no new matches, never duplicate active games. Rules-engine crash quarantines a game for recovery. Clock source uses monotonic time; wall-clock changes affect display/audit timestamps, not elapsed duration.

    Reconciliation finds active games without leases, expired clocks lacking terminal events, terminal games missing rating work, rating work with mismatched versions, and gaps between event store and archive. Manual adjudication is signed, reasoned, and emits superseding events. Replay cannot alter ratings twice.

    Security, abuse, observability, and testing¶

    Use short-lived game-scoped tokens, TLS, command authorization, invite entropy, DDoS protection, and account/device risk controls. Validate all moves server-side. Defend against engine assistance, sandbagging, boosting, collusion, multi-accounting, disconnect abuse, chat harassment, spectator scraping, and tournament disruption. Protect minors and minimize behavioral telemetry. Moderation and adjudication are auditable with separation of duties.

    Measure matchmaking wait/quality, abandoned tickets, move round-trip, validation time, stale/duplicate commands, clock compensation and disputes, reconnect recovery, authority lease health, event append latency, active-game headroom, spectator lag/fanout, rating backlog, rules-version errors, and fair-play model drift/appeals. Synthetic bots play legal scripted games continuously across regions.

    Property-test legal move generation, repetition, terminal states, clock monotonicity, command idempotency, and epoch fencing. Perft suites verify rules. Model-check move-versus-timeout races. Replay historical games across storage and rating versions. Load-test synchronized tournament starts and championship spectators. Chaos-test process death between append/ack, gateway loss, bus lag, region partition, delayed timers, and duplicate rating events. Canary rules changes only on new unrated games.

    Tradeoffs and final architecture¶

    Peer-to-peer play is cheap but cannot provide trusted clocks or legality; server authority wins. A relational row per game is simple but loses an auditable move history; append-only events plus snapshots provide both. Global strong replication improves RPO but hurts blitz latency; zonal commits with tier-specific regional durability are practical. A single matchmaking queue maximizes choice but ignores RTT and variants; segmented pools with widening balance quality and wait.

    flowchart TD
 Q[Leased matchmaking ticket] --> M[Atomic pair and fixed game policy]
 M --> A[Fenced regional game authority]
 A --> V[Versioned legal-move and clock engine]
 V --> E[(Append-only game events)]
 E --> P[Player sequence catch-up]
 E --> S[Delayed hierarchical spectator fanout]
 E --> R[Ordered rating ledger]
 E --> F[Offline fair-play analysis]

    The final architecture gives each game one fenced regional authority that validates commands, computes clocks, and conditionally appends ordered events before acknowledgment. Snapshots accelerate recovery. Gateways provide reconnect and sequence catch-up. Matchmaking and tournaments create immutable policy generations. Rating, fair play, archive, and spectator fanout consume events independently, keeping the move path small.

    L5 and L6 expectations¶

    An L5 candidate should establish server authority, model ordered moves and clocks, choose event log plus snapshots, explain matchmaking, reconnect, command idempotency, partitioning, spectator fanout, rating, and process failure.

    An L6 candidate should define move/timeout semantics, epoch fencing and migration, regional durability tiers, tournament auditability, rating ordering, anti-cheat governance, overload priorities, and privacy. They should quantify latency/headroom tradeoffs and connect high-stakes modes to additional cost and operational controls.

    Five-minute summary¶

    Atomically match leased tickets and pin rules, rating, time control, and region. Route each game to one fenced authority. For every command, verify identity, connection/authority epoch, expected sequence, side to move, legality, and authoritative monotonic clock; append the move and resulting clocks before acknowledging. Deduplicate command IDs and let reconnect fetch a snapshot plus events after sequence N. Timers recheck canonical state, so stale timeout callbacks cannot end a moved game. Partition events by game and snapshot periodically. Keep spectator fanout, analysis, ratings, chat, and anti-cheat off the move path; use delayed hierarchical fanout for hot matches and ordered idempotent rating work. Commit zonally for normal latency, replicate for recovery, and buy stronger durability for high-stakes events. Degrade spectators before active play and measure move latency, clock disputes, stale commands, lease health, recovery, rating lag, and fanout delay.

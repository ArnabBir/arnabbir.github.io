    Design WhatsApp¶

    Interview roadmap¶

    Scope one-to-one and group text/media messaging, delivery receipts, offline sync, and multiple devices. Exclude calls, payments, public channels, and cryptographic protocol internals, but preserve end-to-end encryption (E2EE) constraints. Start with durable store-and-forward delivery, then add groups, device fanout, ordering, and regional failover.

    Product framing and requirements¶

    Users send messages to contacts and groups, receive them online or after reconnecting, and observe sent, delivered, and read states. Each registered device receives encrypted payloads. The service should accept messages under 200 ms p99 within a region, deliver to connected recipients within one second normally, retain offline messages for a defined period, and support billions of users. Availability is high, but authentication, membership, and cryptographic device identity cannot be bypassed.

    E2EE means servers route ciphertext and limited metadata; they cannot search or moderate plaintext. Abuse controls rely on rate, graph, reports, and privacy-preserving signals.

    Source boundary: Meta's 2021 multi-device explanation documents per-device identity keys, client-side pairwise encryption/fanout, Sender Keys for groups and encrypted history transfer. It says delivered messages are not retained on the server. This chapter's server-side conversation sequencer and optional 30-day canonical archive are proposed product choices, not verified WhatsApp architecture or retention policy (accessed 2026-09-08).

    Invariants and estimates¶

    • A client-generated message ID is unique within a sender identity and conversation.
    • Accepted ciphertext is durably stored before acknowledgement.
    • Each conversation has a server-assigned monotonic sequence, though clients may display provisional sends.
    • Delivery is at least once; device clients deduplicate.
    • Only devices eligible at send time receive group sender-key material under the protocol policy.
    • A receipt advances monotonically per device/conversation.
    • Membership removal prevents future messages but cannot retract decrypted history.

    Assume 2 billion daily users, 100 messages/user/day: 200 billion/day, 2.3 million/s average and 10 million/s peak. At 1 KB envelope average, ingress is 200 TB/day before replicas. Connections may exceed one billion concurrently. Media bypasses messaging servers via encrypted object storage. Metadata retention and offline TTL dominate durable queue size.

    Deep dives¶

    Ordering and duplicates¶

    A conversation home shard serializes appends and assigns sequence numbers. This gives deterministic group order without global ordering. Failover fences the previous leader with epochs; a sequence may have gaps, never reuse. Clients merge provisional local sends with acknowledged sequences and deduplicate by client ID. Cross-conversation ordering is intentionally undefined.

    Groups and device fanout¶

    Small groups expand synchronously into device inbox tasks after commit. Large groups shard membership snapshots and fan out asynchronously with fair scheduling. The message records membership version; workers evaluate joined_seq/left_seq against the message's accepted membership epoch. A removed user never receives messages accepted after removal; delivery of older already-authorized ciphertext follows the explicit history/revocation policy. Adding a companion device requires key transfer/verification and a bounded history policy, not silent server plaintext recovery.

    Presence, connections, and push¶

    Gateways keep soft presence in an expiring distributed map. Presence is advisory and aggressively privacy-limited. Consistent hashing routes a device to a gateway region; reconnect resumes by durable cursor, so losing gateway memory is harmless. Mobile push contains only a wake-up token, not plaintext. Backpressure slows sync batches, coalesces wakes, and rejects abusive senders before durable queues exhaust.

    Encrypted media¶

    The client encrypts media with a random key, uploads ciphertext using a constrained signed URL, and sends the encrypted key and content hash in the message envelope. CDN/object storage sees opaque bytes. Expired unreferenced uploads are collected. Integrity checks detect corruption without revealing plaintext.

    Partitioning and regions¶

    Partition conversations by ID and map each to a home region/shard; split exceptionally large broadcast-like groups or move them to a channel product with weaker semantics. Device inboxes partition by device and time bucket. Membership partitions by conversation. Gateways partition live connections by device hash.

    Keep synchronous replicas across failure zones within the home region and async replicas to a paired region. Failover requires quorum/lease fencing to avoid two sequencers. During isolation, non-home regions can retain encrypted client retries locally but should not assign conflicting order. Device inboxes are regional projections rebuilt from the message log.

    Consistency, failures, and recovery¶

    Message append and idempotency mapping are strongly consistent per conversation. Delivery, receipts, presence, and push are eventual. Receipts use max(current,incoming) after verifying device ownership, eligible delivered ranges and a known delivery frontier. Cumulative receipt N covers eligible messages through N, with server-certified sequence gaps; it cannot acknowledge an unseen future sequence or skip an unresolved eligible envelope. A transactionally written outbox prevents accepted messages from disappearing before fanout.

    Gateway loss causes reconnect and replay. Bus outage increases event age while accepted messages remain safe. Inbox loss triggers replay from conversation events for affected devices. Slow or offline devices have quotas and TTLs; policy decides archive retrieval after queue expiry. Poison envelopes are quarantined without blocking a partition. Overloaded groups receive per-conversation token buckets so one chat cannot starve the fleet.

    Security, abuse, privacy¶

    Bind sessions to registered device keys, rate-limit registration and sends, detect enumeration, and protect key-directory updates with transparency/audit mechanisms. Minimize and expire IP, social graph, and delivery metadata. Group invite controls, blocking, unknown-sender requests, report flows, and forwarding limits mitigate spam. Reports may include user-selected decrypted messages; this must be explicit. Encrypt storage and logs, isolate keys, and never log ciphertext payloads unnecessarily.

    Lifecycle, migrations, and overload drills¶

    Account and device lifecycle deserves its own state machine. Registering a device proves control of the account and establishes a new identity key; removing it revokes future envelopes and key updates. A phone-number reassignment must not expose the former owner's graph or queued messages. The identity service therefore separates stable account identity from reusable address, waits through a quarantine period where policy permits, and requires contacts to observe a safety-number change. Backup restoration is a client-side encrypted import, not a server shortcut around E2EE.

    Schema changes use envelope versions understood by both old and new clients. Writers initially emit the old representation while readers accept both, then capability telemetry permits dual-write or new emission. A conversation-log repartition is performed by fencing writes briefly at a revision, copying immutable history, tailing changes, validating counts/checksums, and switching the routing epoch. Inbox projections can simply be rebuilt after the cutover. Key protocol changes require a longer compatibility window and explicit downgrade prevention.

    Consider a regional broker outage during a major holiday. Send acceptance continues while the replicated message log and outbox have capacity. Delivery lag alarms trigger push coalescing, disable presence and read-receipt chatter, reserve workers for one-to-one and small groups, and slow large-group fanout. If retained outbox bytes approach the safety limit, admission control first rejects abusive and broadcast-like traffic; it does not acknowledge work that cannot be retained. Recovery drains with fair per-conversation scheduling so recent interactive chats are not trapped behind one old group backlog.

    Retention is also semantic. Expiring a device inbox does not necessarily delete canonical encrypted history; the product must define whether history is server-retained, client-backup-only, or available for a bounded window. Message deletion can distribute a tombstone, but a recipient may already have decrypted or copied content. UI wording and compliance guarantees must reflect that physical limit rather than imply remote erasure.

    Large attachment download is independently resumable. A recipient validates ciphertext chunks and the authenticated content hash before exposing plaintext. Signed object URLs are short-lived and can be refreshed only by a currently eligible device, but revocation cannot claw back bytes already downloaded. CDN cache keys identify encrypted immutable objects, so tenants never share plaintext and origin retries cannot alter message meaning. Media reference counts are projections; garbage collection requires both retention expiry and a safety delay to survive delayed message fanout.

    L6 mechanisms: key lifecycle, device semantics, and failover¶

    Identity keys, signed prekeys, and one-time prekeys¶

    Each device generates its long-lived identity signing/DH key material locally and registers a public bundle containing {account_id,device_id,identity_public_key,signed_prekey_public,signature,prekey_id,one_time_prekeys[],key_version}. The server verifies proof of account/device registration and the signed-prekey signature but never receives private keys. Signed prekeys rotate on a schedule and overlap long enough for delayed initiations. One-time prekeys are atomically claimed at most once; low-watermark telemetry asks the device to replenish them. If none remain, initiation may use the signed prekey with reduced initial forward secrecy, and the client surfaces/records that protocol state rather than silently fabricating a one-time key.

    An initiating device fetches the recipient device list plus one bundle per device, verifies signatures and transparency proofs, and performs the protocol's authenticated asynchronous key agreement. The initial ciphertext includes the public material and prekey IDs needed by each recipient. The directory returns monotonic device-list and key versions; rollback, unknown-device addition, identity-key replacement, or reused one-time-prekey IDs trigger safety-number change handling and security telemetry. The server can rate-limit claims but cannot choose alternate keys without detection by signed identities and key transparency.

    That detection requires an authenticated identity anchor and independently checked log consistency, such as previously verified device keys, security-code comparison, or trusted-device approval. A signature under a newly substituted identity and an inclusion proof from a server-controlled log do not alone defeat a malicious server. Protocol libraries and test vectors, not this architectural sketch, must define the ratchet cryptography.

    Double Ratchet and group sender keys¶

    After initial agreement, each device pair runs a Double Ratchet session. A DH ratchet advances the root key when the peer's ratchet public key changes; sending and receiving chain KDFs derive a unique message key per message and erase prior chain keys. Headers carry ratchet public key, previous-chain length, and message number. A bounded skipped-key cache permits out-of-order delivery; exceeding the bound requires session repair rather than retaining unlimited keys. Retransmission resends the same ciphertext envelope and message key result for the same client message ID; it never encrypts a different plaintext under a reused key/nonce.

    Pairwise encryption of every group message is O(m*d) ciphertexts. Instead, each sending device creates a group sender-key state containing a signing public key and symmetric chain seed, distributes it over existing pairwise encrypted sessions to every currently eligible member device, and then sends one group ciphertext. Every message advances the sender chain and is signed by that sending device. Sender keys are scoped to (group_id,membership_epoch,sender_device_id).

    flowchart TD
 A[Membership or device-list change] --> B[Commit new membership epoch]
 B --> C[Invalidate affected sender-key distributions]
 C --> D[Each active sender device creates or rotates sender key]
 D --> E[Pairwise encrypt distribution to eligible devices]
 E --> F[Recipient records group epoch and sender device]
 F --> G[One ciphertext per group message]
 G --> H{Epoch and sender chain valid?}
 H -->|Yes| I[Verify signature, derive key, erase old key]
 H -->|No| J[Request bounded key redistribution or sync]

    Removing a member increments the membership epoch before later sends are accepted. Active senders rotate and distribute new sender keys excluding removed devices; adding a member also starts a new epoch so it does not gain old sender chains. Messages accepted under epoch E use the membership snapshot for E; delayed fanout cannot substitute current membership. A server cannot force already offline senders to rotate, so it rejects their stale-epoch sends and clients refresh membership/key distribution first.

    Multi-device membership and key rotation¶

    Account membership is a signed device-list state, separate from group membership. Linking device D2 requires an existing trusted device or explicit account recovery to authorize D2's identity key. Contacts fetch a versioned list and establish independent pairwise sessions to D2; servers fan out future one-to-one messages to each eligible recipient device and the sender's companion devices. History transfer is an E2EE device-to-device or encrypted-backup operation with a visible time boundary.

    Adding or removing a device increments device_list_version. Removal immediately stops server envelope fanout, revokes its unclaimed prekeys, rotates account-scoped distribution state, and causes group sender-key redistribution according to policy. A group records both membership_epoch and recipient device-list versions used for a send. Rapid device churn is batched only within a short explicit window; security-critical removal is never delayed for batching. Clients retain old chain keys only for the bounded out-of-order window and erase them after advancement, so rotation protects future messages but cannot retract keys already delivered.

    Storage sizing and receipt semantics¶

    At 200 billion messages/day and a 1 KB canonical encrypted envelope, raw ingress is 200 TB/day. Three in-region copies plus one asynchronous DR copy consume about 800 TB/day before compression and metadata. A 30-day canonical retention example is 24 PB; adding 25% indexes/outbox overhead reaches roughly 30 PB. Device inboxes store references, not ciphertext: with an average 2.5 recipient devices and a 32-byte reference, they add 200B * 2.5 * 32, or 16 TB/day raw. For pairwise multi-device encryption, the canonical record contains distinct recipient ciphertexts or references to them; the 1 KB average must include that multiplicity or be multiplied by its measured envelope expansion. Group fanout can dominate that estimate, so apply per-device offline TTLs, time-bucket compaction, and a separate bounded-history product policy. At 10 million peak messages/s, provision at least 10 GB/s raw append bandwidth, about 30 GB/s across three synchronous copies, before headroom.

    accepted(seq) means the home quorum durably stored ciphertext and outbox, not that any recipient received it. A device-level delivered receipt means that named recipient device authenticated, durably stored, and acknowledged all messages through sequence N; read means its UI crossed the product-defined read boundary through N. Store monotonic (delivered_through,read_through) per recipient device and conversation. User-level “delivered” can mean any device or all active devices, but the API/UI must choose and name one; a practical policy shows delivered after any current device and exposes no device detail to senders. Group receipts are sparse per (message,user) near the recent read window and aggregate to counts for large groups rather than creating an unbounded sender-visible matrix. Removed devices stop affecting “all active devices” after the removal version, and delayed lower receipts never regress state.

    Regional failover boundary¶

    Each conversation has {home_region,shard,epoch} in a quorum-backed directory. The home leader accepts and sequences writes only with a current lease; synchronous zonal replicas acknowledge locally and a paired region tails ciphertext, idempotency rows, membership epochs, and outbox. On regional failure, the control plane proves the old lease expired or obtains quorum fencing, promotes the paired replica, increments epoch, and publishes routing. The new leader may leave sequence gaps but never reuses a sequence. Clients resend ambiguous (sender_device,client_msg_id) requests and receive the committed result if replication included it.

    Prevent reuse of sequence numbers from a lost asynchronous tail by reserving sequence ranges durably in the surviving quorum before allocation, or by defining sequence identity as (authority_epoch, local_sequence) throughout messages, receipts and cursors. Merely incrementing an ownership epoch without changing or protecting the sequence space is insufficient. Missing idempotency records after nonzero-RPO recovery also require explicit uncertain-history reconciliation rather than an unconditional once-only claim.

    If replica lag exceeds the declared recovery point, failover pauses acceptance or returns retryable uncertainty rather than risk two outcomes for one client ID. Non-home regions may spool client ciphertext in an unsequenced encrypted ingress queue, but only the fenced owner converts it into accepted messages. After promotion, rebuild device inbox projections from the canonical log/outbox, prioritize interactive conversations, and bound reconnect waves with jitter and per-device leases. Failback is another fenced migration, never an automatic reversal.

    Observability and testing¶

    Track accept latency, durable-append errors, delivery latency percentiles, undelivered age, duplicate suppression, receipt lag, connections/gateway, reconnect storms, membership fanout cost, hot conversations, push effectiveness, queue bytes, and regional replication lag. Use opaque IDs in traces.

    Property-test membership interval eligibility and monotonic receipts. Inject duplicate/reordered bus events, timeout every acknowledgement boundary, kill gateways during sync, partition sequencer replicas, rotate devices, and load-test Zipfian groups plus reconnect storms. Cryptographic implementations need protocol vectors, independent review, and downgrade tests.

    Tradeoffs and final architecture¶

    Per-recipient copies simplify sync but amplify group storage; references in device inboxes keep ciphertext canonical. Global sequencing is unnecessary and would add coordination cost; conversation-local sequencing matches user expectations. Active-active conversation writes invite split-brain order, so home-region ownership plus fenced failover wins. E2EE reduces server features and moderation visibility but is a product privacy requirement.

    Questions and level expectations¶

    1. How does leader failover avoid duplicate sequences?
    2. Which membership snapshot controls a delayed group fanout?
    3. What happens when one device remains offline for months?
    4. How does adding a device affect historical keys?
    5. How is a reconnect storm contained?

    L5 candidates should define store-and-forward, local ordering, device inboxes, idempotent retry, offline sync, and group skew. L6 candidates should design fencing, membership-version semantics, reconnect overload policy, metadata privacy, key lifecycle boundaries, replay operations, and regional disaster recovery while explaining product compromises imposed by E2EE.

    Five-minute summary¶

    Assign each conversation to a fenced home shard that durably appends encrypted envelopes and an outbox before acknowledging. Use client message IDs for idempotency and conversation-local sequences for order. At-least-once consumers expand committed messages into per-device inbox references using membership versions; gateways stream them to connected devices or issue content-free push wakes. Reconnecting devices resume from durable cursors and acknowledge cumulative monotonic receipts. Store encrypted media directly in object storage. Partition logs by conversation, inboxes by device and time, and protect large groups with shardable fair fanout. Keep E2EE keys on devices, minimize metadata, and degrade presence or push without losing accepted messages.

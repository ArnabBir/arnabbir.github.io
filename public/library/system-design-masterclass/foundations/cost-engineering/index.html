    Cost Engineering for System Design¶

    Cost is a resource constraint and a design signal. A system that meets latency and availability goals but cannot sustain its unit economics is not production-ready. Cost engineering connects workload, architecture, reliability, and ownership in one model.

    Learning goals¶

    After this chapter, you should be able to:

    • build a unit-cost model from requests, bytes, compute time, and retention;
    • calculate storage, replication, egress, standby, observability, and compute cost drivers;
    • separate fixed, variable, step, and risk costs;
    • run sensitivity and break-even analyses rather than quote one forecast;
    • define cost-aware SLOs and overload/degradation policy; and
    • show L5 service optimization and L6 portfolio and commitment judgment.

    First principles: price the workload path¶

    Cloud bills are outputs. Begin with resource consumption per business unit:

    cost per successful operation = allocated monthly cost / successful operations
contribution per operation = revenue per operation - variable cost per operation

    Choose a unit tied to product value: successful checkout, active tenant, streamed hour, indexed GB, or completed job. Requests alone can hide fan-out and failures. Keep unsuccessful and retried work visible.

    Contribution is not accounting gross margin, and a nonmonetary value proxy cannot be subtracted from dollars. FinOps Unit Economics distinguishes resource-efficiency metrics from business-unit metrics. Record the allocation boundary and time window; a zero-success interval has undefined cost per success, not zero cost (accessed 2026-09-08).

    flowchart LR
    D[Business demand] --> W[Requests and bytes]
    W --> C[Compute and memory]
    W --> S[Storage and I/O]
    W --> N[Network and egress]
    W --> O[Logs metrics and traces]
    C --> T[Total cost]
    S --> T
    N --> T
    O --> T
    R[Reliability topology] --> T
    T --> U[Cost per successful unit]

    Classify cost behavior:

    • Fixed: control plane, minimum database cluster, baseline on-call tooling.
    • Variable: request CPU, bytes stored, egress, per-operation fees.
    • Step: another shard, NAT gateway, license tier, or reserved capacity block.
    • Risk: unplanned failover, incident egress, emergency scaling, penalties.
    • Opportunity: engineering complexity and delivery delay.

    Allocation is not optimization. Tags and account boundaries can assign spend, but only workload drivers explain how to change it.

    Build a transparent monthly model¶

    Use one worksheet with units and assumptions:

    requests_month = requests_per_second * seconds_month
compute_hours  = instance_count * hours_month
stored_GB      = ingest_GB_day * retained_days * replication / compression
transfer_GB    = sum(bytes in mutually exclusive billable route classes)
monthly_cost   = compute + storage + IOPS + egress + managed_fees
               + observability + support + allocated_shared_cost

    Use disjoint transfer rows, such as internet delivery, cross-zone replication, cross-region replication, and backup export. A replicated byte crossing a region must not be counted once as replication and again as an additional cross-region byte. Separate charges can still apply to that same byte, such as network transfer and gateway processing; model those as separate price components.

    Use 2,592,000 seconds for a 30-day month and 720 hours. Keep decimal GB versus binary GiB consistent with the price sheet. Taxes, support, committed-use discounts, free tiers, and negotiated rates belong in separate explicit rows.

    Do not hard-code public list prices into architecture claims. Prices vary by provider, region, tier, date, contract, and traffic direction. Store each input with source URL, region, retrieval date, and owner; rerun the model during reviews.

    Steady footprint versus GB-months¶

    Storage capacity and storage billing are different quantities. With no expiry, replication, or compression, a new system ingesting 4 TB/day reaches 120 TB after 30 days. Under continuous uniform ingestion, its average footprint over that month is 60 TB, so the month consumes about 60 TB-months, not 120 TB-months. The next month starts with the retained first-month data. Integrate the occupied or provisioned capacity over time and apply the provider's actual metering rules. A retention-sized steady-state estimate is appropriate only after the window fills.

    Compute and memory¶

    Compute demand follows useful work, headroom, and topology:

    cores_needed = peak_requests_per_second * CPU_seconds_per_request
provisioned_cores = cores_needed / target_peak_utilization

    At 40,000 requests/s and 2.5 ms CPU/request, demand is 100 CPU-seconds/s, or 100 fully utilized cores. At a 60% target peak utilization, the starting provision is 100 / 0.60 = 167 cores. Then add N-1 zone or host failure policy, deployment surge, skew, and background work.

    Memory can be the actual constraint. Include heap or process working set, caches, connection buffers, queues, page cache, sidecars, and fragmentation. Right-sizing CPU while paging or triggering long GC cycles increases both cost and user harm.

    Autoscaling reduces variable idle capacity only when startup and signal delay fit the workload. A ten-minute boot cannot absorb a one-minute flash crowd. Keep warm headroom, scheduled scaling, admission control, or queueing for work whose deadlines allow it. Scale on demand signals such as concurrency or queue age, not CPU alone.

    Spot or interruptible capacity suits checkpointable, retryable work. It is not automatically cheap if interruption causes large replay, egress, missed deadlines, or simultaneous fallback to expensive capacity. Commitments lower unit price but convert variable cost into a forecast obligation. Cover stable baseline first and preserve flexibility for uncertain growth.

    Storage: retained bytes are only the start¶

    Monthly storage cost includes logical data, replicas, indexes, versions, metadata, WAL/logs, snapshots, temporary compaction space, and API/I/O charges.

    Suppose ingest is 4 TB/day, retention 90 days, compression is 2.5:1, replication factor 3, indexes add 25% of compressed primary bytes, and operational free-space reserve is 30%:

    compressed primary = 4 TB/day * 90 / 2.5 = 144 TB
replicated data    = 144 TB * 3 = 432 TB
with indexes       = 432 TB * 1.25 = 540 TB
provisioned at 70% = 540 TB / 0.70 = 771.4 TB

    This is a steady-state physical-capacity estimate after the 90-day retention window fills. It excludes backups and write amplification. Provider-billed storage can differ: some managed prices already include durability copies, and usage-priced object storage does not bill an empty 30% disk reserve like provisioned block capacity. Do not multiply a provider's per-GB price by internal replica count unless that is its billing contract. Object-storage lifecycle tiers can reduce old-data price, but retrieval fees, minimum storage duration, restore latency, and request charges may dominate during reprocessing. Tie storage class to access distribution and RTO, not age alone.

    Retention is a product, legal, reliability, and cost decision. Cutting raw retention from 90 to 30 days may save storage but make a 45-day fraud investigation impossible. A tiered design can retain aggregates online, raw data in colder storage, and deletion evidence separately.

    Network and egress¶

    Use the Network Calculations Workbook to make bandwidth, serialization, connection, retry, and failover assumptions explicit before pricing the topology.

    Network cost depends on direction and topology: internet egress, cross-zone, cross-region, CDN origin traffic, NAT processing, load-balancer bytes, private links, and provider-to-provider transfer can have different rates.

    At 20,000 responses/s averaging 120 KB:

    payload rate = 20,000 * 120 KB = 2.4 GB/s
per day      = 2.4 * 86,400 = 207,360 GB/day
per month    = 6,220,800 GB/month

    Protocol overhead, retries, cache misses, and TLS do not make this smaller. If an edge cache raises byte hit ratio from 70% to 90%, origin payload falls from 30% to 10%, a 66.7% reduction in origin bytes. Request hit ratio and byte hit ratio differ; large-object misses can dominate egress.

    Compression trades CPU and latency for bytes. Measure by content type and device. Moving compute near data can reduce transfer, but duplicating datasets across regions may cost more storage and replication. Include traffic during resharding, repair, restore, replay, and region evacuation.

    Replication, standby, and reliability cost¶

    Replication pays for durability and availability, but replicas also incur compute, storage, log transfer, backups, and operations. A read replica is not free merely because it serves queries.

    Compare three patterns:

    Topology Normal cost Failure behavior Hidden cost
    single region, multi-zone lower survives zone faults no regional continuity
    warm regional standby medium scale and promote within RTO data lag, untested capacity, failover egress
    active-active regions high lower routing failover time conflict, duplicated baseline, coordination and testing

    A standby sized at 20% is cheap only if it can reach required capacity before the RTO. If failover requires 15 minutes to provision but the RTO is 5 minutes, the cost model is buying an invalid design. Conversely, a fully active duplicate may be unjustified for a journey with a four-hour RTO.

    N-1 capacity is a billable requirement. Three equal zones at 50% normal utilization rise to 75% after one zone fails. At 65%, survivors rise to 97.5% before skew. Model the failure workload and degradation policy, not only normal average.

    Observability cost and signal value¶

    Observability cost comes from collection CPU, agents, network, ingestion, indexing, cardinality, retention, queries, archives, and human attention. More telemetry is not automatically more observable.

    For 100,000 requests/s with one 4 KB log per request:

    log ingest = 100,000 * 4 KB = 400 MB/s
per day    = 34.56 TB/day

    At 30-day hot retention, that is over 1 PB before replication and indexes. Prefer structured low-volume outcome logs, metrics for aggregate health, sampled traces, and temporary targeted debug telemetry. Never sample away rare correctness or audit evidence by accident.

    Metrics cardinality approximates the product of label dimensions that coexist. IDs, URLs with identifiers, and unbounded error text can bankrupt a time-series backend. Set per-team budgets for active series, log GB/day, trace spans, and retention. Provide dashboards that connect telemetry cost to owning service and signal use.

    Managed service versus self-managed¶

    Compare total cost of ownership, not hourly node price:

    TCO = infrastructure + licenses + support + engineering labor
    + incident loss + migration cost + compliance work + opportunity cost

    A managed database may cost more per unit but reduce patching, backups, failover, and specialized staffing. It may also add egress, feature, quota, or lock-in constraints. Self-management can be rational at scale when workload-specific efficiency and internal expertise exceed the operating burden. Include 24x7 ownership and upgrade/recovery testing in both cases.

    Sensitivity and break-even analysis¶

    A forecast without sensitivity is a disguised guess. Identify variables with uncertainty and cost leverage:

    Variable Base Low High Cost effect
    peak requests/s 40k 25k 80k compute and quotas
    response size 120 KB 70 KB 200 KB CDN and egress
    cache byte hit ratio 85% 70% 93% origin compute and network
    retention 90 d 30 d 365 d storage and compliance
    compression 2.5x 1.5x 4x storage versus CPU

    Use tornado analysis: vary one input while holding others fixed, rank the monthly deltas, then test correlated scenarios. Traffic and telemetry often rise together; treating them as independent understates the high case.

    For build-versus-buy, let managed fixed cost be $30,000/month plus $0.10 per million operations, and self-managed cost be $80,000/month plus $0.02 per million. Ignoring omitted risks, break-even volume V in millions is:

    30,000 + 0.10V = 80,000 + 0.02V
0.08V = 50,000
V = 625,000 million operations/month

    The illustrative break-even is 625 billion operations/month, about 241,000 operations/s averaged over 30 days. These are invented prices for the algebra, not provider quotes.

    The result is only as useful as included labor, commitments, support, migration, and reliability assumptions. Use it to locate questions, not manufacture certainty.

    Cost-aware SLOs¶

    An SLO defines user value; cost constrains how it is delivered. Do not weaken correctness silently to save money. Segment journeys: checkout may need 99.99% while historical export can be queued with a 24-hour completion SLO.

    Calculate the marginal cost of a reliability target. Moving from 99.9% to 99.99% may require multi-region capacity, stronger replication, lower-risk deployment, and more on-call investment. Compare that increment with expected user, contractual, and business harm. The cheapest valid design meets the required SLO with recovery evidence; it does not maximize availability everywhere.

    Create degradation ladders tied to cost and overload:

    1. Disable expensive optional enrichment.
    2. Reduce result depth or freshness within the product contract.
    3. Queue deferrable work.
    4. Preserve paid or critical tenant fairness without starving control traffic.
    5. Reject quickly above the safe envelope.

    Track cost per good request alongside availability and latency. A retry storm can increase spend while successful throughput falls. A useful operational guardrail is:

    cost efficiency = good operations / consumed resource unit

    Alert on abrupt efficiency drops, not on spending alone.

    Decision framework¶

    1. Define business unit, demand distribution, SLO, RTO/RPO, and data lifecycle.
    2. Draw the request and data path, including retries, replication, telemetry, and recovery.
    3. Convert each path into CPU-seconds, GB-months, operations, and transferred bytes.
    4. Add failure headroom, standby, commitments, shared services, support, and labor.
    5. Calculate unit cost at low, base, high, and failure scenarios.
    6. Rank sensitivities and optimize the largest controllable driver.
    7. Validate changes against latency, correctness, availability, privacy, and operability.
    8. Assign budget ownership and calibrate forecast against actual usage monthly.

    Failure cases and tradeoffs¶

    Cost action Possible regression Guardrail
    raise utilization p99 and failover saturation load test and N-1 ceiling
    reduce replicas lower availability or durability explicit SLO, RPO, and restore evidence
    shorten retention lost investigation or replay legal/product review and tiered archive
    sample telemetry missed rare failures preserve audit and error exemplars
    reserve capacity stranded commitment cover stable baseline; scenario analysis
    use spot capacity interruption and replay checkpointing and fallback budget
    centralize tenants larger blast radius cells, quotas, isolation, restore units
    compress aggressively CPU and latency increase end-to-end benchmark by payload

    L5 and L6 expectations¶

    An L5 candidate quantifies service unit cost, sizes capacity from measurements, identifies dominant drivers, and preserves SLOs while optimizing. An L6 candidate also models portfolio commitments, shared-cost allocation, tenancy tiers, regional topology, migration economics, marginal reliability cost, budget policy, and how architecture standards prevent spend from becoming unowned externality.

    Interview traps¶

    • Quoting a monthly cloud bill without a per-unit model.
    • Using average traffic and forgetting peak, skew, and failure headroom.
    • Counting logical storage but not replicas, indexes, WAL, backups, and free space.
    • Ignoring cross-zone, cross-region, NAT, and observability charges.
    • Declaring serverless or managed services cheaper at every scale.
    • Cutting telemetry without protecting audit and rare-error evidence.
    • Buying commitments before separating stable baseline from uncertain growth.
    • Optimizing cost by violating an unstated SLO.

    Exercises with worked answers¶

    1. Compute sizing¶

    A handler needs 4 ms of CPU per request at 25,000 peak requests/s. Target peak CPU is 50%, and one zone out of three must be survivable without shedding. Estimate total provisioned cores before skew and deployment surge.

    Answer: Peak demand is 25,000 * 0.004 = 100 cores. At 50% target utilization, 200 active cores are required under normal policy. To lose one of three equal zones while retaining 200 cores, each zone needs 100 cores, for 300 total. Add skew, background work, sidecars, and deployment surge after measurement.

    2. Retention sensitivity¶

    A stream ingests 2 TB/day, compresses 4:1, uses replication factor 3, and has 20% index overhead. Compare 30-day and 180-day stored footprints before free-space reserve.

    Answer: At 30 days: 2 * 30 / 4 * 3 * 1.2 = 54 TB. At 180 days: 324 TB. The extra 150 days costs 270 TB of replicated indexed footprint, before backups and compaction. Evaluate colder tiers and retrieval/RTO rather than assuming all 180 days must remain hot.

    3. Cache economics¶

    Origin traffic is 1 PB/month at a 60% byte hit ratio. A cache change reaches 85% without changing demand. What happens to origin bytes?

    Answer: Total demand is 1 PB / (1 - 0.60) = 2.5 PB/month. At 85% hit ratio, origin is 2.5 * 0.15 = 0.375 PB/month, a 62.5% reduction from 1 PB. Subtract added cache storage, invalidation, request, and compute cost, and verify freshness and tail latency.

    Takeaways¶

    • Express spend per successful business unit and preserve units in every equation.
    • Model compute, memory, storage, I/O, egress, reliability, and telemetry together.
    • Include failure capacity and recovery traffic, not only normal averages.
    • Use sensitivity and break-even analysis to expose uncertain assumptions.
    • Optimize the largest controllable driver without violating correctness or SLOs.
    • Assign architecture and budget ownership, then calibrate forecasts with actuals.

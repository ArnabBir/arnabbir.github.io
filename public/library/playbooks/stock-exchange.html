LESSON 01 OF 88 One idea at a time

An exchange is a matching system

The chapter designs an electronic stock exchange. Its central job is to bring compatible buy and sell instructions together. The instruction is an order; a completed match produces executions, also called fills.

Think of the exchange as an organized meeting place, not as a shop that owns every share on its shelves. Buyers and sellers submit instructions, and the exchange applies its matching rules.

The chapter gradually adds the machinery around matching: admitting orders, checking risk and funds, keeping a reliable order history, publishing market data, and producing reports.

Follow the example

Teaching example: a seller offers 20 shares of DEMO at 100. A compatible buyer arrives. Matching connects those two instructions; the exchange does not need to invent a new seller.

Common misunderstanding

Do not confuse the exchange with the broker app or with the entire payment and settlement ecosystem. This chapter focuses on the exchange backend.

Remember this

The core job is to match orders correctly and predictably.

The client, the broker, and the exchange

A retail client commonly interacts through a broker. The broker supplies the website or app and sends the order onward. Institutional clients may use specialized software and different connection paths.

The chapter emphasizes that clients have different latency and volume needs. A pension fund, an active trading firm, and a retail user need not interact with the system in the same way.

The architecture separates these access paths from the matching logic. Different entrances can feed the exchange without requiring a different matching rule for each screen or network connection.

Follow the example

Teaching example: Maya taps Buy in a broker app. The app is her interface, but the order still needs to enter the exchange and reach its matching engine.

Common misunderstanding

A responsive broker screen does not prove that an order has been matched. The screen and the matching engine are separate parts of the journey.

Remember this

The broker is an access path; the exchange performs the match.

Risk checks and money reserved for orders

Before an order reaches matching, the order manager applies risk rules and checks the user's wallet. The chapter gives a simple daily limit as an example of a risk rule.

Sufficient funds are not only a momentary yes/no question. An order can wait in the book. The chapter says the funds needed for that waiting order must be withheld so the user cannot spend them again.

The detailed wallet design is referred to another chapter. Here, learn the exchange-side responsibility: check and protect the resources needed by accepted orders. The exact reservation records and release protocol are not specified in this chapter.

Follow the example

Teaching example: a buy order is waiting for a match. Its required funds must not simultaneously be treated as freely spendable for another order.

Common misunderstanding

The source gives a shares-per-day risk example early and a dollar-per-day example later. They are illustrative rules, not one fully specified risk policy.

Remember this

Risk limits control exposure; reservations prevent overspending.

LESSON 05 OF 88 One idea at a time

Availability and recovery are separate goals

The non-functional requirements include at least 99.99% availability, fault tolerance, low latency, and security. These describe how the system behaves, not just which buttons its API supports.

Availability asks whether the service can be used. Fault tolerance asks how failures are contained and how the system recovers. A very fast engine that cannot recover its orders is not enough.

Security includes account-related identity checks and protection of public interfaces. This is the chapter's engineering scope, not a complete legal or compliance specification.

Follow the example

Teaching example: one engine process fails. The design must decide how another instance gets the right state and when it is safe to take over.

Common misunderstanding

A second server is not automatically a recovery strategy. The state, failure detection, and takeover decision also matter.

Remember this

The exchange must be fast while working and recoverable when it fails.

LESSON 06 OF 88 One idea at a time

Why the slowest requests matter

The chapter emphasizes round-trip latency and especially the 99th percentile. It defines the measured path from an order entering the exchange to its return as a filled execution.

An average can hide a small set of painfully slow requests. A tail percentile describes the slow end of the observed distribution. Later, the chapter calls stable response time latency determinism.

Read the measurement boundary carefully. The chapter also allows limit orders to wait in the book, but it does not give a separate detailed latency definition for acknowledgment versus waiting for a match. Do not invent a promise that every limit order fills within milliseconds.

Follow the example

Teaching example: 99 requests take 1 ms and one takes 200 ms. The average alone does not describe the experience of that slow request.

Common misunderstanding

Fast processing cannot create a compatible counterparty. Waiting for a market match and time spent executing software are not interchangeable ideas.

Remember this

Always state which path and which percentile you are measuring.

LESSON 07 OF 88 One idea at a time

Turn one billion orders into a rate

The chapter assumes 100 symbols and one billion orders per day. It uses a 6.5-hour trading session for its back-of-the-envelope estimate.

First convert the session to seconds: 6.5 x 3,600 = 23,400. Then divide: 1,000,000,000 / 23,400 is about 42,735 orders per second, rounded in the chapter to 43,000 QPS.

The chapter uses a five-times peak factor, giving about 215,000 QPS from the rounded average. These are the chapter's design assumptions, not a current measurement of a named exchange.

Follow the example

Worked arithmetic: 43,000 x 5 = 215,000. This is why sizing only for the daily average would miss the stated peak assumption.

Common misunderstanding

Do not divide by a full 24-hour day when reproducing this estimate. The source uses the trading-session duration.

Remember this

Convert the active time window first, then calculate average and peak.

Keep four questions in your head

A useful reading frame is to ask four questions about every order. May it enter? What is its position in the processing order? What does it match? How will the result be remembered and shared?

Admission leads to the gateway, risk checks, and wallet checks. Ordering leads to the sequencer. Matching leads to the order book and matching engine. Remembering and sharing lead to event history, market data, and reporting.

This is a teaching map of the chapter, not an extra architecture. It helps you attach later details to a purpose instead of memorizing a disconnected list of services.

Follow the example

Teaching example: when you encounter the reporter, place it under remembering and sharing. It does not choose the next resting order to match.

Common misunderstanding

Do not put every responsibility into the matching engine just because matching is the center of the system.

Admission, order, match, remember: four questions organize the chapter.

A limit order sets a price boundary

A limit order asks to buy or sell at a specified price. The chapter notes that the order might not find a match immediately and might be only partially matched.

A buy limit expresses the buyer's price boundary; a sell limit expresses the seller's boundary. Price compatibility and available quantity determine whether there is something to match.

Common misunderstanding

Placing a valid limit order is not the same as receiving an immediate execution.

A limit controls price; it does not guarantee an immediate fill.

LESSON 11 OF 88 One idea at a time

Bid, ask, and spread

A bid is a buying price. An ask is a selling price. The best bid is the highest displayed buying price, and the best ask is the lowest displayed selling price.

The spread is the difference between best ask and best bid. This is a reading of the book, not a promise that all orders execute at either displayed number.

The source's example starts with best bid 100.08 and best ask 100.10. When the entire 100.10 ask level is consumed, the best ask becomes 100.11 while the illustrated best bid stays unchanged.

Follow the example

Worked arithmetic: initial spread = 100.10 - 100.08 = 0.02. After that ask level disappears, spread = 100.11 - 100.08 = 0.03.

Common misunderstanding

Best bid and best ask belong to different sides. The highest sell price is not the best ask.

Remember this

Best bid is highest buy; best ask is lowest sell.

LESSON 15 OF 88 One idea at a time

A candle summarizes an interval

A candlestick summarizes prices over a time window. Its four basic prices are open, high, low, and close, often shortened to OHLC.

Open is the first price in the interval and close is the last. High and low describe the extremes. The body and upper/lower shadows are visual ways to show those values.

The chapter mentions intervals such as one minute, five minutes, one hour, one day, one week, and one month. Later it stores candles in bounded structures so the market-data publisher does not retain an unlimited number in memory.

Follow the example

Teaching example: interval prices 100, 103, 99, 101 produce open 100, high 103, low 99, close 101.

Common misunderstanding

A candle is an interval summary. It does not retain the complete order queue or every event in that interval.

Remember this

One candle summarizes first, highest, lowest, and last price.

LESSON 21 OF 88 One idea at a time

One match produces two executions

The chapter says a match results in two executions: one for the buy side and one for the sell side. These represent the two sides of the same trading match.

An order and an execution are not identical. An order is an instruction. An execution is an outcome for some matched quantity. A valid order may have no execution yet, and a partially filled order can receive more executions later.

Keeping these identities separate is essential for the order manager, the execution API, and the reporter that joins request details with outcome details.

Follow the example

Teaching example: Buy B1 meets Sell S1 for 20 shares. There is a buy-side execution linked to B1 and a sell-side execution linked to S1. That is not 40 distinct shares traded.

Common misunderstanding

Do not double-count the traded quantity merely because both sides have an execution record.

Remember this

Two execution records can describe the two sides of one match.

LESSON 23 OF 88 One idea at a time

Market data: from executions to views

The matching engine produces a stream of executions. The market data publisher, abbreviated MDP, uses order information and matching results to construct market views.

Those views include order books and candlestick charts. The data service stores or serves the market data, and brokers relay it to their clients.

Pay attention to both inputs. The chapter's figures include orders and matched results. A view of waiting orders needs information about the order stream, not only a list of completed fills.

Follow the example
